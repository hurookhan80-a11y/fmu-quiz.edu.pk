Hemostasis and Blood Coagulation MCQs for FMU 1st Year MBBS Physiology

Home › Physiology MCQs › Hemostasis and Blood Coagulation

Haemostasis is the process that stops bleeding when a blood vessel is injured. First the vessel narrows and platelets form a plug, then the clotting system produces fibrin, which strengthens the plug into a stable clot. Later the clot is dissolved as the vessel wall heals, so blood flow is restored without blocking the vessel.

  1. Platelets are derived from which precursor cell, and their lifespan is about 10 days?

    • A. Lymphoblasts
    • B. Megakaryocytes
    • C. Myeloblasts
    • D. Monoblasts
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Megakaryocytes

    Platelets are cytoplasmic fragments shed from megakaryocytes in the bone marrow. They circulate for about 8-10 days before being removed, mainly by the spleen.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 37: Hemostasis and Blood Coagulation

  2. Vitamin K is required for the activation of which clotting factor (among others)?

    • A. Factor VII
    • B. Factor I (fibrinogen)
    • C. Factor VIII only
    • D. Factor XII only
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Factor VII

    Vitamin K is needed for gamma-carboxylation and activation of factors II, VII, IX and X (and proteins C and S). Factor VII has the shortest half-life, so its level falls first in vitamin K deficiency.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 37: Hemostasis and Blood Coagulation

  3. Clotting Factor I is:

    • A. Thrombin
    • B. Fibrinogen
    • C. Factor X
    • D. Prothrombin
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Fibrinogen

    Factor I is fibrinogen, which thrombin converts into fibrin to form the clot mesh. Factor II is prothrombin, the precursor of thrombin.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 37: Hemostasis and Blood Coagulation

  4. Which anticoagulant is NOT effective in vitro (in a test tube)?

    • A. Coumarins (warfarin)
    • B. EDTA
    • C. Heparin
    • D. Citrate
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Coumarins (warfarin)

    Coumarins such as warfarin act in vivo by blocking hepatic synthesis of vitamin K-dependent factors, so they do not anticoagulate blood in a test tube. Heparin works both in vivo and in vitro by activating antithrombin III.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 37: Hemostasis and Blood Coagulation

  5. Aspirin produces its antiplatelet effect mainly by inhibiting synthesis of:

    • A. Prostacyclin only
    • B. Bradykinin
    • C. Thromboxane A2
    • D. Leukotrienes
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Thromboxane A2

    Aspirin irreversibly inhibits cyclo-oxygenase in platelets, blocking thromboxane A2 production for the life of the platelet. This reduces platelet aggregation and is the basis of its use in preventing arterial thrombosis.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 37: Hemostasis and Blood Coagulation

  6. Which clotting factor is vitamin K-dependent and has the shortest half-life?

    • A. Factor VII
    • B. Factor I (fibrinogen)
    • C. Factor XIII
    • D. Factor VIII
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Factor VII

    Factors II, VII, IX, and X are vitamin K-dependent; factor VII has the shortest half-life, so PT prolongs first in warfarin therapy or vitamin K deficiency. Fibrinogen (factor I) and factor VIII are not vitamin K-dependent.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 37: Hemostasis and Blood Coagulation

  7. Factor I of the clotting cascade is:

    • A. Prothrombin
    • B. Thrombin
    • C. Fibrinogen
    • D. Tissue factor
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Fibrinogen

    Factor I is fibrinogen, which thrombin converts to fibrin monomer that polymerizes into the clot mesh. Prothrombin is factor II, and tissue factor is factor III.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 37: Hemostasis and Blood Coagulation

  8. Which fibrinolytic agent is used to dissolve clot in acute myocardial infarction?

    • A. Streptokinase
    • B. Heparin
    • C. Aspirin
    • D. Warfarin
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Streptokinase

    Streptokinase activates plasminogen to plasmin and lyses fibrin clot, so it is used as thrombolytic therapy in acute MI. Heparin and warfarin prevent further clotting but do not actively dissolve an established clot.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 37: Hemostasis and Blood Coagulation

  9. After transfusion a patient develops rashes and itching without fever or hypotension. This is most likely due to:

    • A. Intravascular haemolytic reaction
    • B. Iron overload
    • C. Allergic reaction to donor plasma proteins
    • D. Bacterial contamination of blood
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Allergic reaction to donor plasma proteins

    Urticaria, rash and itching after transfusion indicate a mild allergic reaction to donor plasma proteins. Intravascular haemolysis presents with fever, pain, hypotension and haemoglobinuria, not isolated itching.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology — Ch. 37: Hemostasis and Blood Coagulation

  10. The three overlapping mechanisms that achieve hemostasis after vascular injury are:

    • A. Vasodilation, platelet inhibition, and fibrinolysis
    • B. Inflammation, immunity, and repair
    • C. Vascular spasm, platelet plug formation, and blood coagulation
    • D. Thrombosis, embolism, and infarction
    • E. Chemotaxis, opsonization, and phagocytosis
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Vascular spasm, platelet plug formation, and blood coagulation

    Hemostasis proceeds in three integrated steps: immediate vasoconstriction (vascular spasm) reduces blood loss, platelets adhere and aggregate into a plug, and the coagulation cascade reinforces it with a fibrin clot. Defects in any step — vessels, platelets, or clotting factors — cause bleeding.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 37: Hemostasis and Blood Coagulation

  11. The initial vascular spasm after a vessel is cut is triggered mainly by:

    • A. Bradykinin
    • B. Histamine release
    • C. Local myogenic contraction plus thromboxane A2 released from platelets
    • D. Prostacyclin
    • E. Nitric oxide
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Local myogenic contraction plus thromboxane A2 released from platelets

    Cut vessels constrict instantly through myogenic spasm of smooth muscle, reinforced by thromboxane A2 released from adhering platelets — a potent vasoconstrictor that also promotes further platelet aggregation. This rapid response can nearly seal small vessels even before the clot forms.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 37: Hemostasis and Blood Coagulation

  12. Platelet adhesion to exposed subendothelial collagen after injury is mediated mainly by:

    • A. Fibrinogen binding to GPIIb/IIIa only
    • B. von Willebrand factor bridging collagen to platelet GPIb receptors
    • C. Complement C3b
    • D. ADP receptors
    • E. Thrombin receptors
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. von Willebrand factor bridging collagen to platelet GPIb receptors

    When endothelium is damaged, von Willebrand factor binds exposed collagen and then tethers platelets via their GPIb receptors — the critical first adhesion step, especially under high shear in small vessels. Deficiency causes von Willebrand disease, the most common inherited bleeding disorder, with defective platelet adhesion.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 37: Hemostasis and Blood Coagulation

  13. Aspirin impairs platelet function by:

    • A. Depleting platelet granules
    • B. Activating plasmin
    • C. Irreversibly inhibiting cyclooxygenase, preventing thromboxane A2 synthesis
    • D. Blocking ADP receptors irreversibly
    • E. Inhibiting vitamin K
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Irreversibly inhibiting cyclooxygenase, preventing thromboxane A2 synthesis

    Aspirin acetylates platelet cyclooxygenase-1 permanently, blocking production of thromboxane A2 — a key amplifier of platelet aggregation and vasoconstriction. Because platelets lack nuclei, the effect lasts for their 7–10-day lifespan, which is why low-dose aspirin provides lasting antiplatelet protection.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 37: Hemostasis and Blood Coagulation

  14. The extrinsic pathway of coagulation is initiated by:

    • A. Tissue factor (factor III) released from damaged tissues activating factor VII
    • B. Contact of factor XII with collagen
    • C. Platelet factor 3
    • D. Calcium ions alone
    • E. Kallikrein
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Tissue factor (factor III) released from damaged tissues activating factor VII

    The extrinsic pathway begins when tissue factor from injured tissues binds factor VII, activating it; the VIIa–tissue factor complex then activates factor X. It is the faster pathway in vivo and is assessed by the prothrombin time (PT/INR) — which is why warfarin, affecting factor VII first, prolongs the PT.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 37: Hemostasis and Blood Coagulation

  15. Which set of factors belongs to the intrinsic pathway of coagulation?

    • A. XIII, protein C, protein S
    • B. VII, III, X
    • C. XII, XI, IX, VIII
    • D. II, V, I
    • E. Fibrinogen, plasmin, D-dimer
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. XII, XI, IX, VIII

    The intrinsic pathway is triggered by contact activation: factor XII → XI → IX, with factor VIII as the essential cofactor for IXa to activate X. It is measured by the aPTT — prolonged in hemophilia A (VIII deficiency), hemophilia B (IX deficiency), and by heparin therapy.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 37: Hemostasis and Blood Coagulation

  16. The common pathway of coagulation begins with the activation of:

    • A. Factor X, leading through factor V to prothrombin (II) and fibrinogen (I)
    • B. Protein C
    • C. Factor VII
    • D. Factor XII
    • E. Factor XIII
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Factor X, leading through factor V to prothrombin (II) and fibrinogen (I)

    Both intrinsic and extrinsic pathways converge on factor X. Xa with its cofactor Va converts prothrombin (II) to thrombin, and thrombin converts fibrinogen (I) to fibrin — the common final sequence of clot formation. Factor XIII then cross-links fibrin for strength.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 37: Hemostasis and Blood Coagulation

  17. The vitamin K–dependent clotting factors are:

    • A. XII, XI, IX, VIII
    • B. I, V, VIII, XIII
    • C. Fibrinogen, plasmin, thrombin
    • D. III, IV, VI, XI
    • E. II, VII, IX, X (plus proteins C and S)
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: E. II, VII, IX, X (plus proteins C and S)

    Vitamin K is required for gamma-carboxylation of factors II, VII, IX, and X (and the anticoagulant proteins C and S), enabling them to bind calcium and phospholipid surfaces. Warfarin blocks vitamin K recycling, depleting these functional factors — factor VII falls first, prolonging the PT/INR earliest.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 37: Hemostasis and Blood Coagulation

  18. A 10-year-old boy with recurrent hemarthroses (bleeding into joints) and a markedly prolonged aPTT but normal PT and platelet count most likely has:

    • A. Vitamin K deficiency
    • B. Factor VII deficiency
    • C. Von Willebrand disease
    • D. Hemophilia A (factor VIII deficiency)
    • E. Thrombocytopenia
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Hemophilia A (factor VIII deficiency)

    Hemophilia A — X-linked factor VIII deficiency — causes deep tissue and joint bleeding with isolated aPTT prolongation (intrinsic pathway), while PT (extrinsic) and platelets remain normal. Hemarthrosis is its classic presentation in childhood; treatment is factor VIII replacement.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 37: Hemostasis and Blood Coagulation

  19. Hemophilia B (Christmas disease) is caused by deficiency of:

    • A. Factor IX
    • B. von Willebrand factor
    • C. Factor VII
    • D. Factor XI
    • E. Factor VIII
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Factor IX

    Hemophilia B is X-linked deficiency of factor IX, clinically indistinguishable from hemophilia A (factor VIII deficiency) — both prolong the aPTT with normal PT. Specific factor assays distinguish them, which matters because treatment requires the correct factor concentrate.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 37: Hemostasis and Blood Coagulation

  20. Von Willebrand disease, the most common inherited bleeding disorder, presents with mucocutaneous bleeding (epistaxis, menorrhagia) because von Willebrand factor is needed for:

    • A. Plasmin activation
    • B. Fibrin cross-linking
    • C. Thrombin generation
    • D. Vitamin K carboxylation
    • E. Platelet adhesion to collagen and for stabilizing circulating factor VIII
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: E. Platelet adhesion to collagen and for stabilizing circulating factor VIII

    Von Willebrand factor has two roles: bridging platelets to subendothelial collagen (adhesion) and carrying/protecting factor VIII in plasma. Its deficiency therefore impairs primary hemostasis (mucosal bleeding, prolonged bleeding time) and may mildly prolong the aPTT from low factor VIII — distinguishing it from the deep joint bleeds of hemophilia.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 37: Hemostasis and Blood Coagulation

  21. Heparin exerts its anticoagulant effect mainly by:

    • A. Blocking platelet ADP receptors
    • B. Chelating calcium
    • C. Inhibiting vitamin K epoxide reductase
    • D. Inhibiting cyclooxygenase
    • E. Potentiating antithrombin III, which inactivates thrombin and factor Xa
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: E. Potentiating antithrombin III, which inactivates thrombin and factor Xa

    Heparin binds antithrombin III and enormously accelerates its inhibition of thrombin (IIa) and factor Xa (and other proteases). Because it acts immediately in blood, heparin is the anticoagulant of choice for acute thrombosis; its effect is monitored with the aPTT and reversed with protamine.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 37: Hemostasis and Blood Coagulation

  22. Warfarin is monitored with the PT/INR (rather than aPTT) because:

    • A. It depletes vitamin K–dependent factors including factor VII of the extrinsic pathway, which the PT measures
    • B. It inhibits platelets
    • C. It only affects the intrinsic pathway
    • D. It activates plasmin
    • E. It chelates calcium
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. It depletes vitamin K–dependent factors including factor VII of the extrinsic pathway, which the PT measures

    Warfarin blocks vitamin K epoxide reductase, reducing functional factors II, VII, IX, and X. Factor VII has the shortest half-life, so the extrinsic pathway (measured by PT) prolongs first — making PT/INR the appropriate monitor. The INR standardizes PT results for consistent warfarin dosing.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 37: Hemostasis and Blood Coagulation

  23. Thrombin (factor IIa) contributes to hemostasis by all of the following EXCEPT:

    • A. Converting fibrinogen to fibrin
    • B. Providing positive feedback that amplifies its own generation
    • C. Converting fibrinogen to fibrin
    • D. Stimulating endothelial prostacyclin release
    • E. Activating platelets and factors V, VIII, XI, and XIII
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Stimulating endothelial prostacyclin release

    Thrombin is the central enzyme of coagulation: it cleaves fibrinogen to fibrin, powerfully activates platelets, and amplifies the cascade by activating factors V, VIII, XI, and XIII (positive feedback). Prostacyclin, by contrast, is an endothelial product that INHIBITS platelet aggregation — opposing thrombin's procoagulant actions.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 37: Hemostasis and Blood Coagulation

  24. Factor XIIIa (fibrin-stabilizing factor) strengthens the clot by:

    • A. Cross-linking fibrin strands into a stable mesh
    • B. Inhibiting plasmin
    • C. Activating platelets
    • D. Generating more thrombin
    • E. Releasing calcium
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Cross-linking fibrin strands into a stable mesh

    After thrombin converts fibrinogen to loose fibrin strands, factor XIIIa (activated by thrombin) forms covalent cross-links between them, creating a tough, insoluble mesh resistant to premature lysis. Factor XIII deficiency causes delayed bleeding — clots form but then break down — with normal PT, aPTT, and platelet counts.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 37: Hemostasis and Blood Coagulation

  25. Plasmin, the enzyme that dissolves clots (fibrinolysis), is generated from plasminogen by:

    • A. Tissue plasminogen activator (t-PA) and urokinase
    • B. Factor XIII
    • C. Heparin
    • D. Thrombin
    • E. Warfarin
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Tissue plasminogen activator (t-PA) and urokinase

    Endothelial t-PA and urinary urokinase convert plasminogen to plasmin, which digests fibrin (and fibrinogen), limiting clot growth and clearing vessels — producing D-dimers as fibrin breakdown products. Recombinant t-PA exploits this system to dissolve coronary and cerebral thrombi in MI and ischemic stroke.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 37: Hemostasis and Blood Coagulation

  26. A 55-year-old man with crushing chest pain receives alteplase (recombinant t-PA) within 2 hours of symptom onset. The therapeutic rationale is:

    • A. To raise HDL cholesterol
    • B. To prevent platelet adhesion
    • C. To convert plasminogen to plasmin and lyse the coronary thrombus, restoring myocardial perfusion
    • D. To lower blood pressure
    • E. To inhibit vitamin K
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. To convert plasminogen to plasmin and lyse the coronary thrombus, restoring myocardial perfusion

    In acute ST-elevation MI (and ischemic stroke), a fibrin-rich thrombus occludes the artery; t-PA rapidly generates plasmin to dissolve it. Benefit is exquisitely time-dependent ('time is muscle') — earlier lysis salvages more myocardium — but bleeding, including intracranial hemorrhage, is the major risk.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 37: Hemostasis and Blood Coagulation

  27. A septic patient develops oozing from venipuncture sites, thrombocytopenia, prolonged PT and aPTT, low fibrinogen, and markedly elevated D-dimers. This constellation indicates:

    • A. Hemophilia A
    • B. Von Willebrand disease
    • C. Idiopathic thrombocytopenic purpura
    • D. Vitamin K deficiency alone
    • E. Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC)
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: E. Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC)

    DIC is consumption coagulopathy: widespread microvascular clotting depletes platelets and factors (prolonging PT/aPTT, lowering fibrinogen) while fibrinolysis of the microthrombi floods the blood with D-dimers — so the patient simultaneously clots and bleeds. Sepsis, obstetric catastrophes, and malignancy are classic triggers; treatment targets the underlying cause.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 37: Hemostasis and Blood Coagulation

  28. Proteins C and S function as natural anticoagulants by:

    • A. Stimulating erythropoiesis
    • B. Inactivating factors Va and VIIIa, limiting thrombin generation
    • C. Promoting fibrin cross-linking
    • D. Activating platelets
    • E. Inhibiting plasmin
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Inactivating factors Va and VIIIa, limiting thrombin generation

    Activated protein C, with protein S as cofactor, proteolytically destroys factors Va and VIIIa — the essential cofactors of the common pathway — thereby braking thrombin generation. Deficiencies (e.g., factor V Leiden, which resists protein C) cause inherited thrombophilia with recurrent venous thrombosis.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 37: Hemostasis and Blood Coagulation

  29. Calcium ions (factor IV) are required in coagulation because they:

    • A. Generate thromboxane
    • B. Inhibit heparin
    • C. Activate platelets directly
    • D. Bridge gamma-carboxylated clotting factors to phospholipid surfaces where the cascade complexes assemble
    • E. Lyse fibrin
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Bridge gamma-carboxylated clotting factors to phospholipid surfaces where the cascade complexes assemble

    The vitamin K–dependent factors (II, VII, IX, X) use calcium to anchor onto negatively charged phospholipid membranes (from activated platelets), concentrating the enzyme complexes where clotting is needed. This is why citrate — which chelates calcium — is used as the anticoagulant in banked blood.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 37: Hemostasis and Blood Coagulation

  30. After a blood vessel is injured, platelets adhere to exposed collagen and form a temporary plug. Which plasma protein acts as a bridge for platelet adhesion, especially under high shear stress?

    • A. Fibrinogen alone, without any platelet receptor
    • B. Albumin
    • C. Von Willebrand factor
    • D. C-reactive protein
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Von Willebrand factor

    Von Willebrand factor binds exposed collagen and platelet glycoprotein Ib, anchoring platelets at sites of injury, particularly in fast-flowing vessels; it also carries factor VIII. Fibrinogen mainly links activated platelets to each other via GPIIb/IIIa during aggregation, while albumin and C-reactive protein do not mediate adhesion.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Hemostasis and Blood Coagulation

  31. A patient on long-term warfarin therapy has a prolonged prothrombin time. Warfarin impairs clotting mainly by reducing the functional levels of which factors?

    • A. Factors VIII and IX only
    • B. Platelet glycoprotein receptors
    • C. Vitamin K-dependent factors II, VII, IX and X
    • D. Factors V and VIII stored in platelets
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Vitamin K-dependent factors II, VII, IX and X

    Warfarin blocks vitamin K recycling needed for gamma-carboxylation and activation of factors II, VII, IX and X (and proteins C and S), prolonging the prothrombin time. Factor VIII is not vitamin K-dependent, and warfarin does not act by blocking platelet receptors.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Hemostasis and Blood Coagulation
