Pulmonary Ventilation, Pulmonary Circulation, and Gas Exchange MCQs for FMU 1st Year MBBS Physiology

Home › Physiology MCQs › Pulmonary Ventilation, Pulmonary Circulation, and Gas Exchange

Pulmonary ventilation is the movement of air in and out of the lungs, driven by the diaphragm and the respiratory muscles. Blood flows through the pulmonary circulation at a low pressure, passing the alveoli where oxygen is taken up and carbon dioxide is released. Matching of air flow and blood flow in each lung region keeps gas exchange efficient.

  1. A 45-year-old patient develops hemorrhagic shock with systolic BP 70 mmHg. Which change in pulmonary blood flow zones is most likely?

    • A. Expansion of Zone I due to alveolar pressure exceeding arterial pressure
    • B. Expansion of Zone 3 with increased capillary recruitment
    • C. Reduction of Zone I because arterial pressure rises above alveolar pressure
    • D. Conversion of the entire lung to Zone 2 pattern
    • E. Increased perfusion of the lung apex compared to the base
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Expansion of Zone I due to alveolar pressure exceeding arterial pressure

    In haemorrhagic shock pulmonary arterial pressure falls severely. Where alveolar pressure exceeds arterial pressure, capillaries are compressed and perfusion ceases, expanding Zone 1 (ventilated but unperfused) lung, normally minimal. Zone 3 (continuous flow) would shrink, not expand, and apical perfusion falls rather than rises. The whole lung does not become Zone 2.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Pulmonary Ventilation, Pulmonary Circulation, and Gas Exchange

  2. A 55 year old patient with chronic pulmonary fibrosis undergoes pulmonary function testing. Which abnormality in gas exchange is most likely to appear?

    • A. Decreased arterial PCO2
    • B. Impaired diffusion capacity for O2
    • C. Increased venous admixture (shunt effect)
    • D. Reduced ventilation-perfusion ratio
    • E. Increased physiologic dead space
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Impaired diffusion capacity for O2

    In pulmonary fibrosis the alveolar-capillary membrane is thickened by collagen, increasing diffusion distance. By Fick's law diffusing capacity (DLCO) falls and O2 transfer is impaired, causing exertional hypoxaemia that worsens as transit time shortens. PCO2 is often low from hyperventilation, shunt and dead space are not the primary defect, and the V/Q ratio is not primarily reduced.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Pulmonary Ventilation, Pulmonary Circulation, and Gas Exchange

  3. A 60 year old man develops pneumonia leading to alveolar collapse in the right lung base. Which disturbance in ventilation-perfusion relationship is most characteristic here?

    • A. Predominant dead space ventilation with wasted air flow
    • B. Right-to-left intrapulmonary shunt of blood
    • C. Regions of ventilation without corresponding perfusion
    • D. Alveolar hyperventilation causing elevated PO2
    • E. Diffusion limitation across the alveolar-capillary membrane
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Right-to-left intrapulmonary shunt of blood

    Pneumonia with alveolar collapse means alveoli are perfused but not ventilated (V/Q approaching zero). Blood traversing them is not oxygenated and mixes with oxygenated blood as a right-to-left intrapulmonary shunt, causing hypoxaemia poorly responsive to supplemental O2. This is the opposite of dead space (ventilated but unperfused) and is not primarily a diffusion block.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Pulmonary Ventilation, Pulmonary Circulation, and Gas Exchange

  4. A 60 year old man with advanced emphysema performs a ventilation-perfusion (V/Q) test. His measured physiological dead space is markedly increased compared to normal. Which mechanism best explains this finding?

    • A. Increased alveolar ventilation with normal perfusion
    • B. Adequate ventilation occurring in alveoli without effective perfusion
    • C. Reduced tidal volume causing alveolar hypoventilation
    • D. Diffusion limitation of O2 across the alveolar membrane
    • E. Increased shunting of blood through unventilated alveoli
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Adequate ventilation occurring in alveoli without effective perfusion

    Physiological dead space is the volume of gas that does not participate in gas exchange. It increases when alveoli are ventilated but capillary perfusion is absent or severely reduced, as when emphysema destroys alveolar walls and capillaries, creating high V/Q wasted ventilation. Shunting (perfused but unventilated units) reduces arterial O2 but does not increase dead space.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Pulmonary Ventilation, Pulmonary Circulation, and Gas Exchange

  5. A diver breathing compressed air at 100 feet develops euphoria and poor motor control. What is the most likely cause?

    • A. Nitrogen dissolving in neuronal membranes producing narcotic effect
    • B. Oxygen toxicity from high arterial PO2
    • C. CO2 retention due to hypoventilation
    • D. Pulmonary barotrauma from rapid ascent
    • E. Decompression sickness from nitrogen bubble formation
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Nitrogen dissolving in neuronal membranes producing narcotic effect

    At 100 feet the ambient pressure is about 4 atmospheres, so inspired PN2 rises greatly and nitrogen dissolves in lipid-rich neuronal membranes, producing narcosis with euphoria and impaired motor control (nitrogen narcosis). Oxygen toxicity typically causes convulsions and is more likely with high PO2 breathing mixtures, while CO2 retention and decompression sickness on ascent are different syndromes.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Pulmonary Ventilation, Pulmonary Circulation, and Gas Exchange

  6. A diver holds his breath for 90 seconds. The strongest stimulus that forces him to breathe arises from:

    • A. Hypoxemia stimulating peripheral chemoreceptors
    • B. Hypercapnia stimulating central chemoreceptors
    • C. Metabolic acidosis stimulating carotid bodies
    • D. Hypoxia stimulating central chemoreceptors
    • E. Increased pulmonary stretch receptor discharge
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Hypercapnia stimulating central chemoreceptors

    During breath-holding PaCO2 rises steadily and is the dominant chemical drive to breathe via central chemoreceptors sensing H+ in cerebrospinal fluid (the breakpoint). Hypoxia contributes only when PaO2 falls very low, because peripheral chemoreceptors respond weakly until PO2 is below about 60 mmHg. The urge is therefore hypercapnic, not primarily hypoxic.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Pulmonary Ventilation, Pulmonary Circulation, and Gas Exchange

  7. A medical student performs spirometry and finds FEV1/FVC ratio 55%. Which condition is most likely?

    • A. Normal
    • B. Obstructive lung disease
    • C. Restrictive lung disease
    • D. Pulmonary embolism
    • E. Respiratory muscle weakness
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Obstructive lung disease

    An FEV1/FVC ratio of 55% (below the 70% threshold) indicates obstructive lung disease, where expiratory airflow is limited so FEV1 falls disproportionately while FVC is relatively preserved. In restrictive disease both volumes fall together and the ratio is normal or increased. Bronchial obstruction from asthma, chronic bronchitis, or emphysema is the usual cause.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Pulmonary Ventilation, Pulmonary Circulation, and Gas Exchange

  8. A military pilot rapidly ascends to high altitude without supplemental oxygen. After a few minutes, he develops confusion and impaired judgment. Which mechanism best explains this?

    • A. Reduced barometric pressure - low alveolar PO2 hypoxia
    • B. Increased alveolar ventilation respiratory acidosis
    • C. High inspired PO2 oxygen toxicity in neurons
    • D. High barometric pressure nitrogen narcosis left shift of
    • E. Excess O2 binding to Hb O-Hb curve
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Reduced barometric pressure - low alveolar PO2 hypoxia

    Rapid ascent reduces barometric pressure, so inspired PO2 (FiO2 x (PB - PH2O)) and alveolar PO2 fall, producing hypoxic hypoxia with dyspnoea, tachycardia, and impaired cognition. The mechanism is low alveolar PO2 from low ambient pressure, not CO poisoning, diffusion block, or shunt, although those cause hypoxaemia by other routes.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Pulmonary Ventilation, Pulmonary Circulation, and Gas Exchange

  9. A mountaineer ascends to 4000 m and develops hypoxemia. Which mechanism best explains this finding?

    • A. Alveolar ventilation increased at altitude
    • B. Reduced inspired partial pressure of oxygen
    • C. Right-to-left shunt
    • D. Ventilation-perfusion (V/Q) mismatch
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Reduced inspired partial pressure of oxygen

    At 4000 m barometric pressure is roughly 460 mmHg, so inspired PO2 falls from about 149 mmHg at sea level to about 87 mmHg, and alveolar PO2 falls accordingly, causing hypoxaemia. The fraction of O2 in air stays 21%; it is the reduced total pressure that lowers partial pressure. Diffusion limitation and shunt may worsen it but are not the primary cause.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Pulmonary Ventilation, Pulmonary Circulation, and Gas Exchange

  10. A patient with obstructive lung disease demonstrates an increased residual volume on spirometry. Which lung parameter is most affected?

    • A. Total lung capacity elevated due to air trapping
    • B. Functional residual capacity increased by bronchial constriction
    • C. Vital capacity-reduced by trapped air
    • D. Inspiratory capacity may be variably affected
    • E. Tidal volume- usually preserved until late disease
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Functional residual capacity increased by bronchial constriction

    Obstructive disease causes air trapping because narrowed airways close prematurely during expiration; residual volume and therefore functional residual capacity (FRC = ERV + RV) increase. The description 'increased by bronchial constriction' captures this air-trapping hyperinflation. Restrictive disease reduces FRC, and diffusion impairment does not itself raise residual volume.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Pulmonary Ventilation, Pulmonary Circulation, and Gas Exchange

  11. A ventilation-perfusion ratio (V/Q) of 0 means the blood leaving that unit is like:

    • A. Inspiratory air
    • B. Arterial blood
    • C. Venous blood
    • D. Mixed expired air
    • E. Alveolar dead-space air
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Venous blood

    A V/Q ratio of zero means perfusion without ventilation (shunt unit): blood passes unventilated alveoli unchanged, so effluent blood has the composition of mixed venous blood (PO2 about 40 mmHg, PCO2 about 46 mmHg). The opposite extreme, V/Q infinity (dead space), yields gas like inspired air. Normal overall V/Q is about 0.8.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Pulmonary Ventilation, Pulmonary Circulation, and Gas Exchange

  12. Before blood has passed one-third of the distance through the pulmonary capillaries, the pCO2 of pulmonary capillary blood changes to:

    • A. Less than atmospheric pCO2 (45 mmHg)
    • B. Equal to alveolar CO2 (40 mmHg)
    • C. More than interstitial CO2 (40 mmHg)
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Equal to alveolar CO2 (40 mmHg)

    CO2 diffuses about 20 times more readily than O2, so pulmonary capillary PCO2 equilibrates with alveolar PCO2 (about 40 mmHg) very early, well before blood has traversed one-third of the capillary. O2 equilibration also is normally complete by one-third, but CO2 is even faster. Capillary PCO2 does not remain at venous 46 mmHg throughout transit.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Pulmonary Ventilation, Pulmonary Circulation, and Gas Exchange

  13. CO2 rather than O2 normally controls respiration mainly because:

    • A. CO2 affects blood more proportionately
    • B. O2 diffuses more rapidly across the blood-brain barrier
    • C. Peripheral chemoreceptors are insensitive to O2 above 70 mmHg
    • D. Haemoglobin buffer destabilises O2
    • E. CO2 levels remain constant
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Peripheral chemoreceptors are insensitive to O2 above 70 mmHg

    Under normal conditions ventilation is controlled mainly by CO2 (via H+ at central chemoreceptors) because small changes in PaCO2 produce large ventilatory responses, whereas arterial PO2 can fall from 100 to about 70 mmHg with little change in ventilation due to the flat upper part of the Hb-O2 curve and weak peripheral chemoreceptor response. Only when PO2 falls below about 60 mmHg does hypoxic drive become powerful.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Pulmonary Ventilation, Pulmonary Circulation, and Gas Exchange

  14. During upright posture, the lung apex is relatively under perfused compared to the base because:

    • A. Arterial and venous pressures are lower than alveolar pressure at the apex
    • B. Pulmonary vascular resistance is lowest at the apex
    • C. Venous pressure is higher than alveolar pressure at the apex
    • D. Pulmonary arterial pressure increases more at the apex than at the base
    • E. Alveolar ventilation is greater than perfusion at the apex
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Arterial and venous pressures are lower than alveolar pressure at the apex

    In the upright lung, at the apex pulmonary arterial and venous pressures are low due to gravity and may both fall below alveolar pressure (Zone 1 conditions), so capillaries are compressed and perfusion is minimal relative to ventilation. At the base arterial and venous pressures exceed alveolar pressure (Zone 3) and flow is continuous. Low apex perfusion is therefore a hydrostatic pressure effect.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Pulmonary Ventilation, Pulmonary Circulation, and Gas Exchange

  15. Hypoxia increases ventilation primarily by stimulating the:

    • A. Pulmonary stretch receptors
    • B. Cerebral cortex
    • C. Central chemoreceptors
    • D. Medullary neurons directly
    • E. Aortic and carotid bodies
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: E. Aortic and carotid bodies

    Hypoxia stimulates ventilation almost exclusively via peripheral chemoreceptors in the carotid and aortic bodies, which sense falling arterial PO2 (especially below 60 mmHg) and increase afferent firing to the medullary respiratory centre. Central chemoreceptors respond to CO2/H+, not O2, and respond poorly to hypoxia (severe hypoxia depresses them).

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Pulmonary Ventilation, Pulmonary Circulation, and Gas Exchange

  16. If both vagi are cut, the effect on respiration is:

    • A. Increased rate
    • B. Decreased rate
    • C. Will no longer be influenced
    • D. Completely ceased
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Decreased rate

    The vagus carries stretch-receptor afferents from the lungs (Hering-Breuer inflation reflex) that terminate inspiration and set rate. Cutting both vagi removes this feedback, so inspirations become prolonged and breathing becomes slow and deep (decreased rate, increased tidal volume). Rate does not increase, and apnoea does not occur because central rhythm generation persists.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Pulmonary Ventilation, Pulmonary Circulation, and Gas Exchange

  17. In a patient with emphysema, lung compliance is markedly increased. What is the most likely functional consequence?

    • A. Enhanced elastic recoil aiding expiration
    • B. Greater inspiratory effort due to stiff lungs
    • C. Air trapping with expiratory difficulty
    • D. Reduction in residual volume below normal
    • E. Increased efficiency of gas exchange at alveolar level
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Air trapping with expiratory difficulty

    In emphysema alveolar wall destruction reduces elastic recoil and increases compliance; lungs inflate easily but deflate poorly. Airways collapse during expiration, trapping air, increasing residual volume, and making expiration difficult and prolonged. Inspiration is not primarily difficult; the problem is expiratory airflow limitation and hyperinflation, unlike surfactant deficiency where compliance falls.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Pulmonary Ventilation, Pulmonary Circulation, and Gas Exchange

  18. In an upright individual, the apex of the lung receives less perfusion than ventilation. Which consequence is most characteristic of this altered V/Q ratio?

    • A. Decreased alveolar PO2 with increased alveolar PCO2
    • B. Elevated alveolar PO2 with reduced alveolar PCO2
    • C. Alveolar gas tensions approximating mixed venous blood
    • D. Complete alveolar collapse due to inadequate ventilation
    • E. Increased physiologic dead space effect
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Elevated alveolar PO2 with reduced alveolar PCO2

    At the upright apex V/Q is high (about 3 or more) because ventilation continues but perfusion is low. Alveolar gas therefore approaches inspired composition: PO2 is elevated (toward 149 mmHg) and PCO2 is reduced (toward 0 mmHg) compared with the base. This is wasted (dead-space-like) ventilation, not shunt physiology.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Pulmonary Ventilation, Pulmonary Circulation, and Gas Exchange

  19. In chronic hypercapnia, as in long-standing COPD, CSF pH normalizes due to renal compensation. Ventilation is then maintained mainly by:

    • A. Central chemoreceptor response to CO2
    • B. Peripheral chemoreceptor response to O2
    • C. Irritant receptor activation in bronchi
    • D. Cortical voluntary control of breathing
    • E. J-receptor stimulation
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Peripheral chemoreceptor response to O2

    In chronic hypercapnia the kidneys retain bicarbonate and CSF pH is normalised, so central chemoreceptors become relatively insensitive to CO2 (reset). Ventilatory drive then depends mainly on the peripheral chemoreceptor hypoxic response to low PaO2. This is why uncontrolled high-flow O2 can suppress drive in some chronic COPD patients, although hypoxaemia must still be treated with controlled oxygen.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Pulmonary Ventilation, Pulmonary Circulation, and Gas Exchange

  20. Surfactant deficiency in a premature infant leads to:

    • A. Lower alveolar surface tension, preventing collapse
    • B. Reduced lung compliance, making inspiration difficult
    • C. Stabilization of alveoli during expiration
    • D. Increased tidal volume with effortless breathing
    • E. Enhanced alveolar ventilation due to easier expansion
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Reduced lung compliance, making inspiration difficult

    Surfactant reduces alveolar surface tension, especially at low lung volumes, keeping compliance normal and preventing atelectasis. Without it in a premature infant, surface tension is high, compliance falls, large pressures are needed to inflate the lungs, and respiratory distress (hyaline membrane disease) develops with difficult inspiration. Compliance is reduced, not increased.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Pulmonary Ventilation, Pulmonary Circulation, and Gas Exchange

  21. Which factor most strongly determines the efficiency of gas diffusion across the respiratory membrane?

    • A. Thickness of the respiratory membrane
    • B. Solubility of nitrogen in alveolar fluid
    • C. Number of alveoli ventilated per breath
    • D. Active transport of oxygen across type 1 pneumocytes
    • E. Viscosity of blood plasma in pulmonary capillaries
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Thickness of the respiratory membrane

    By Fick's law, diffusion rate is inversely proportional to membrane thickness (and directly to area, solubility, and pressure gradient, and inversely to square root of molecular weight). Thickness is the factor that most strongly limits efficiency when the respiratory membrane is thickened in fibrosis or oedema. Surface area loss also reduces DLCO, but thickness is the classic inverse determinant tested here.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Pulmonary Ventilation, Pulmonary Circulation, and Gas Exchange

  22. Which statement best distinguishes anatomical dead space from physiological dead space?

    • A. Anatomical dead space includes alveoli that are ventilated but not perfused
    • B. Physiological dead space equals anatomical dead space in normal lungs
    • C. Anatomical dead space is larger than physiological dead space in disease
    • D. Physiological dead space excludes the conducting airways
    • E. Anatomical dead space is reduced in obstructive airway disease
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Physiological dead space equals anatomical dead space in normal lungs

    Physiological dead space equals anatomical (conducting airway) dead space plus alveolar dead space (ventilated but unperfused alveoli). In normal lungs alveolar dead space is negligible, so physiological and anatomical dead spaces are essentially equal (about 150 mL). Only in disease with V/Q mismatch does physiological dead space exceed anatomical.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Pulmonary Ventilation, Pulmonary Circulation, and Gas Exchange

  23. Which statement best explains why central chemoreceptors are not directly stimulated by blood H+ ions?

    • A. H+ cannot cross the blood-brain barrier
    • B. CSF lacks buffering capacity
    • C. CO2 is impermeable to the blood-brain barrier
    • D. CSF has a lower pH than blood
    • E. Central chemoreceptors respond only to O2 tension
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. H+ cannot cross the blood-brain barrier

    Central chemoreceptors on the ventral medulla are separated from blood by the blood-brain barrier, which is poorly permeable to charged H+ and HCO3- ions. Blood H+ therefore cannot stimulate them directly; instead CO2 diffuses freely into CSF, where it forms H+ that excites the receptors. This is why arterial PCO2 is the dominant regulator of ventilation via the central mechanism.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Pulmonary Ventilation, Pulmonary Circulation, and Gas Exchange
