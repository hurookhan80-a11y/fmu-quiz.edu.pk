The microcirculation is the network of tiny vessels where exchange between blood and tissues takes place. Fluid filters out of the capillaries into the interstitial space and most of it is reabsorbed, while the remainder enters the lymphatic vessels as lymph. Lymph flows through nodes, where it is filtered, before returning to the blood.

  1. At the arterial end of a skeletal muscle capillary the capillary hydrostatic pressure is 30 mmHg, the interstitial fluid hydrostatic pressure is -3 mmHg, the plasma colloid osmotic pressure is 28 mmHg, and the interstitial fluid colloid osmotic pressure is 8 mmHg. What is the net filtration pressure across the capillary wall?

    • A. 13 mmHg outward (filtration)
    • B. 7 mmHg outward (filtration)
    • C. 7 mmHg inward (reabsorption)
    • D. 3 mmHg outward (filtration)
    • E. Zero; filtration equals reabsorption
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. 13 mmHg outward (filtration)

    Net filtration pressure = (Pc - Pi) - (πc - πi) = (30 - (-3)) - (28 - 8) = 33 - 20 = +13 mmHg. The positive sign means the net force drives fluid OUT of the capillary (filtration) at the arterial end. At the venous end the capillary pressure falls to about 10 mmHg, making the net pressure negative and favoring reabsorption.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 16: The Microcirculation and the Lymphatic System: Capillary Fluid Exchange, Interstitial Fluid, and Lymph Flow

  2. Which type of capillary has large intercellular gaps that allow plasma proteins and even cells to pass freely, and is found in the liver, spleen, and bone marrow?

    • A. Continuous capillaries
    • B. Fenestrated capillaries
    • C. Sinusoidal (discontinuous) capillaries
    • D. Capillaries with tight junctions only
    • E. Lymphatic capillaries
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Sinusoidal (discontinuous) capillaries

    Sinusoidal (discontinuous) capillaries have large gaps between endothelial cells and a discontinuous basement membrane, making them the most permeable capillaries in the body. They occur in the liver, spleen, and bone marrow where free exchange of proteins and cells is needed. Continuous capillaries (muscle, brain) are the least permeable; fenestrated capillaries (glomeruli, intestinal villi, endocrine glands) are intermediate.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 16: The Microcirculation and the Lymphatic System: Capillary Fluid Exchange, Interstitial Fluid, and Lymph Flow

  3. Which plasma protein is the chief contributor to plasma colloid osmotic (oncotic) pressure?

    • A. Fibrinogen
    • B. Albumin
    • C. Alpha-globulin
    • D. Gamma-globulin
    • E. Prothrombin
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Albumin

    Albumin, the smallest and most abundant plasma protein, contributes about 70-80% of plasma colloid osmotic pressure because osmotic pressure depends on the NUMBER of particles, and albumin molecules far outnumber the larger globulins and fibrinogen. This is why hypoalbuminemia in liver failure, nephrotic syndrome, or malnutrition causes edema.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 16: The Microcirculation and the Lymphatic System: Capillary Fluid Exchange, Interstitial Fluid, and Lymph Flow

  4. A 6-year-old boy presents with marked periorbital and ankle edema. Urine shows massive proteinuria and blood shows severe hypoalbuminemia, with otherwise normal cardiac function. What is the primary mechanism of his edema?

    • A. Increased capillary hydrostatic pressure
    • B. Lymphatic obstruction
    • C. Increased capillary permeability
    • D. Decreased plasma colloid osmotic pressure
    • E. Increased interstitial hydrostatic pressure
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Decreased plasma colloid osmotic pressure

    In nephrotic syndrome, heavy urinary protein loss causes hypoalbuminemia, which lowers plasma colloid osmotic pressure. With less oncotic force holding fluid inside capillaries, net filtration increases and fluid accumulates in the interstitium. This is the classic low-oncotic-pressure cause of edema, distinct from the high capillary pressure of heart failure.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 16: The Microcirculation and the Lymphatic System: Capillary Fluid Exchange, Interstitial Fluid, and Lymph Flow

  5. A woman develops chronic non-pitting swelling of the arm two years after mastectomy with axillary lymph node removal. What is the mechanism of this edema?

    • A. Increased capillary hydrostatic pressure
    • B. Decreased plasma proteins
    • C. Blocked lymphatic drainage
    • D. Increased capillary permeability
    • E. Arteriolar dilation
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Blocked lymphatic drainage

    Removal of axillary lymph nodes blocks lymphatic drainage from the arm. Protein-rich fluid that normally returns to blood via lymphatics accumulates in the interstitium, producing lymphedema. Because the fluid is protein-rich, it becomes non-pitting over time as fibrosis develops. Filariasis (elephantiasis) causes edema by the same mechanism.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 16: The Microcirculation and the Lymphatic System: Capillary Fluid Exchange, Interstitial Fluid, and Lymph Flow

  6. A patient with extensive thermal burns develops rapid generalized edema within hours. Which Starling force change best explains this?

    • A. Decreased capillary hydrostatic pressure
    • B. Increased capillary permeability to proteins
    • C. Increased plasma colloid osmotic pressure
    • D. Decreased interstitial colloid osmotic pressure
    • E. Decreased venous pressure
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Increased capillary permeability to proteins

    Burns release histamine, bradykinin, and other mediators that widen intercellular gaps in capillaries, letting plasma proteins leak into the interstitium. Interstitial colloid osmotic pressure rises while plasma oncotic pressure falls, so fluid pours out of capillaries. This is why burn edema fluid is protein-rich and why plasma volume falls dangerously in major burns.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 16: The Microcirculation and the Lymphatic System: Capillary Fluid Exchange, Interstitial Fluid, and Lymph Flow

  7. A 68-year-old man with chronic left heart failure develops pitting ankle edema that worsens through the day. Which change in Starling forces is primarily responsible?

    • A. Increased capillary hydrostatic pressure from venous congestion
    • B. Decreased plasma colloid osmotic pressure
    • C. Blocked lymphatic vessels
    • D. Increased capillary permeability
    • E. Decreased interstitial hydrostatic pressure
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Increased capillary hydrostatic pressure from venous congestion

    In heart failure the failing pump cannot handle venous return, so venous and capillary hydrostatic pressures rise. The increased outward hydrostatic force raises net filtration pressure, pushing fluid into the interstitium. The edema is pitting and gravity-dependent (ankles by evening), the hallmark of raised capillary hydrostatic pressure.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 16: The Microcirculation and the Lymphatic System: Capillary Fluid Exchange, Interstitial Fluid, and Lymph Flow

  8. In resting skeletal muscle, blood flow through the capillary bed switches on and off every few seconds (vasomotion). This intermittent flow is controlled by:

    • A. Venous valves
    • B. The thoracic duct pump
    • C. Sympathetic nerves to veins
    • D. Precapillary sphincters
    • E. Lymphatic smooth muscle
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Precapillary sphincters

    Precapillary sphincters are rings of smooth muscle at the entrance of true capillaries from metarterioles. They open and close periodically (vasomotion), so at rest only a fraction of capillaries are perfused at any moment. During exercise or when local metabolites accumulate, the sphincters relax and the full capillary bed opens to increase exchange surface area.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 16: The Microcirculation and the Lymphatic System: Capillary Fluid Exchange, Interstitial Fluid, and Lymph Flow

  9. Using the Guyton capsule method, the interstitial fluid hydrostatic pressure in loose subcutaneous tissue is normally about:

    • A. +6 mmHg
    • B. 0 mmHg
    • C. -3 mmHg
    • D. +12 mmHg
    • E. -15 mmHg
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. -3 mmHg

    Interstitial fluid hydrostatic pressure is normally slightly subatmospheric, about -3 mmHg in loose tissues such as subcutaneous tissue. This slight suction is created by lymphatic pumping, which continuously removes fluid and protein from the interstitium. When edema develops, interstitial pressure rises toward positive values, which then acts as a safety factor opposing further filtration.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 16: The Microcirculation and the Lymphatic System: Capillary Fluid Exchange, Interstitial Fluid, and Lymph Flow

  10. The thoracic duct returns lymph to the venous blood at the junction of the left subclavian and internal jugular veins. Which regions does it NOT drain?

    • A. The right upper limb and the right side of the head and neck
    • B. Both lower limbs
    • C. The left upper limb
    • D. The abdomen and pelvis
    • E. The left side of the thorax
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. The right upper limb and the right side of the head and neck

    The thoracic duct drains the entire body below the diaphragm plus the left upper body. The right upper limb, right side of the head and neck, and right thorax drain via the much smaller right lymphatic duct into the right venous angle. This asymmetry is clinically relevant in thoracic duct injury, which causes chylothorax on the left side.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 16: The Microcirculation and the Lymphatic System: Capillary Fluid Exchange, Interstitial Fluid, and Lymph Flow

  11. Forward movement of lymph through lymphatic vessels depends on all of the following EXCEPT:

    • A. One-way valves in lymphatic vessels
    • B. Compression by surrounding skeletal muscle
    • C. Intrinsic rhythmic contraction of lymphatic smooth muscle
    • D. Respiratory movements
    • E. High hydrostatic pressure generated by the heart
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: E. High hydrostatic pressure generated by the heart

    Lymph has no central pump like the heart. It moves forward because one-way valves prevent backflow while external compression (skeletal muscle contraction, arterial pulsation, breathing) and intrinsic lymphatic smooth muscle contractions squeeze it centrally. Lymphatic pressures are very low, so high pressure generated by the heart plays no direct role in lymph propulsion.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 16: The Microcirculation and the Lymphatic System: Capillary Fluid Exchange, Interstitial Fluid, and Lymph Flow

  12. In a healthy adult, a small net excess of about 2 mL/min of fluid filters out of all systemic capillaries and is not reabsorbed at venous ends. What happens to most of this fluid?

    • A. It is reabsorbed at the venous ends of capillaries
    • B. It is returned to the blood by the lymphatic system
    • C. It is excreted by the kidneys
    • D. It evaporates from the skin
    • E. It is converted to intracellular fluid
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. It is returned to the blood by the lymphatic system

    Although most filtered fluid is reabsorbed at venous capillary ends, a small net excess of about 2 mL/min (roughly 3 liters per day) remains in the interstitium. The lymphatic system collects this fluid, along with leaked plasma proteins, and returns it to the venous circulation. If lymph flow fails, this fluid accumulates and edema develops even when Starling forces are normal.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 16: The Microcirculation and the Lymphatic System: Capillary Fluid Exchange, Interstitial Fluid, and Lymph Flow

  13. Dilation of the arterioles supplying a capillary bed will increase net capillary filtration mainly because it:

    • A. Decreases plasma colloid osmotic pressure
    • B. Increases capillary permeability to water
    • C. Raises capillary hydrostatic pressure
    • D. Blocks lymphatic drainage
    • E. Lowers interstitial hydrostatic pressure
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Raises capillary hydrostatic pressure

    Arterioles are the main resistance vessels. When they dilate, more arterial pressure is transmitted downstream, raising capillary hydrostatic pressure and therefore net filtration pressure. This is why inflammatory vasodilation contributes to swelling, and why potent arteriolar dilators can cause ankle edema as a side effect.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 16: The Microcirculation and the Lymphatic System: Capillary Fluid Exchange, Interstitial Fluid, and Lymph Flow

  14. Fenestrated capillaries, with pores through their endothelial cells, are characteristically found in all of the following EXCEPT:

    • A. Renal glomeruli
    • B. Intestinal villi
    • C. Endocrine glands
    • D. Skeletal muscle
    • E. Choroid plexus
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Skeletal muscle

    Fenestrated capillaries permit rapid exchange of water and small solutes through endothelial pores (fenestrae), suiting tissues with high fluid or hormone transport: glomeruli, intestinal villi, endocrine glands, and choroid plexus. Skeletal muscle has continuous capillaries with tight intercellular clefts, which are far less permeable.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 16: The Microcirculation and the Lymphatic System: Capillary Fluid Exchange, Interstitial Fluid, and Lymph Flow

  15. Most interstitial fluid is immobilized in a proteoglycan gel. The small fraction of free-flowing interstitial fluid moves through tiny rivulets called:

    • A. Lymphatic sinuses
    • B. Tissue channels
    • C. Precapillary sphincters
    • D. Fenestrae
    • E. Intercellular clefts
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Tissue channels

    The interstitium is mostly a gel of collagen and proteoglycans that traps fluid and resists flow. Free fluid exists only in small tissue channels between cells. When large volumes filter rapidly (as in edema), fluid splits the gel apart and creates large free-fluid spaces, after which edema fluid flows easily — the reason pitting edema displaces so readily under finger pressure.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 16: The Microcirculation and the Lymphatic System: Capillary Fluid Exchange, Interstitial Fluid, and Lymph Flow

  16. When capillary hydrostatic pressure rises, safety factors automatically oppose edema formation. Which of the following is one of these safety factors?

    • A. Decreased lymph flow
    • B. Increased interstitial protein concentration
    • C. Washout of interstitial proteins by increased lymph flow, lowering interstitial oncotic pressure
    • D. Increased capillary permeability
    • E. Decreased interstitial hydrostatic pressure
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Washout of interstitial proteins by increased lymph flow, lowering interstitial oncotic pressure

    The three safety factors against edema are: (1) increased lymph flow carrying fluid away, (2) washout of interstitial proteins which lowers interstitial colloid osmotic pressure, and (3) rising interstitial hydrostatic pressure opposing further filtration. Together they can compensate for a capillary pressure rise of about 15-20 mmHg before visible edema appears.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 16: The Microcirculation and the Lymphatic System: Capillary Fluid Exchange, Interstitial Fluid, and Lymph Flow

  17. A healthy shopkeeper who stands all day develops mild ankle swelling by evening that disappears overnight. The most likely mechanism is:

    • A. Lymphatic valve failure
    • B. Gravity-induced rise in venous and capillary hydrostatic pressure in the legs
    • C. Decreased plasma albumin from standing
    • D. Allergic capillary leak
    • E. Right heart failure
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Gravity-induced rise in venous and capillary hydrostatic pressure in the legs

    Prolonged standing raises venous pressure in the legs by the full hydrostatic column of blood, which transmits to capillaries and increases net filtration. The edema is mild because safety factors compensate, and it resolves overnight when the legs are elevated and venous pressure falls. Persistent or severe edema would suggest venous valve incompetence or cardiac disease.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 16: The Microcirculation and the Lymphatic System: Capillary Fluid Exchange, Interstitial Fluid, and Lymph Flow

  18. Capillary hydrostatic pressure is highest at which point along the capillary?

    • A. The arterial end (about 30-35 mmHg)
    • B. The middle of the capillary
    • C. The venous end (about 10-12 mmHg)
    • D. It is uniform along the entire capillary
    • E. The precapillary sphincter
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. The arterial end (about 30-35 mmHg)

    Blood loses pressure as it flows through the high-resistance capillary, so hydrostatic pressure falls from about 30-35 mmHg at the arterial end to about 10-12 mmHg at the venous end (mean about 17 mmHg). This gradient explains why filtration dominates at the arterial end and reabsorption at the venous end — the basis of the Starling equilibrium across the capillary bed.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 16: The Microcirculation and the Lymphatic System: Capillary Fluid Exchange, Interstitial Fluid, and Lymph Flow

  19. Why is the lymphatic return of plasma proteins to the blood physiologically essential?

    • A. To oxygenate the tissues
    • B. To maintain blood glucose
    • C. To prevent accumulation of protein in the interstitium, which would raise interstitial oncotic pressure and cause edema
    • D. To filter bacteria from blood
    • E. To regulate body temperature
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. To prevent accumulation of protein in the interstitium, which would raise interstitial oncotic pressure and cause edema

    Small amounts of plasma protein continuously leak through capillary walls. Without lymphatic return, protein would accumulate in the interstitium, raising interstitial colloid osmotic pressure and pulling more fluid out of capillaries — a vicious cycle ending in severe edema. The lymphatics are the only route for this leaked protein to re-enter the circulation.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 16: The Microcirculation and the Lymphatic System: Capillary Fluid Exchange, Interstitial Fluid, and Lymph Flow

  20. A child with kwashiorkor (severe protein-energy malnutrition) develops generalized edema despite adequate calorie intake. The edema is best explained by:

    • A. Heart failure from anemia
    • B. Lymphatic filariasis
    • C. Increased capillary permeability from infection
    • D. Sodium retention by the kidneys
    • E. Decreased hepatic albumin synthesis causing low plasma oncotic pressure
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: E. Decreased hepatic albumin synthesis causing low plasma oncotic pressure

    In kwashiorkor, dietary protein deficiency impairs hepatic albumin synthesis, producing hypoalbuminemia and low plasma colloid osmotic pressure. Net capillary filtration rises and edema develops even with normal cardiac function. The same mechanism causes edema in chronic liver disease and nephrotic syndrome — all low-oncotic-pressure edemas.
