Cardiac muscle is the involuntary, striated muscle of the heart wall, and its cells are joined so the heart contracts as one unit. The heart works as two pumps: the right side sends blood to the lungs and the left side sends it to the body. One-way valves between the chambers and at the exits stop blood flowing backwards.

  1. The plateau phase in cardiac action potentials is due to:

    • A. Rapid Na+ influx + rapid K+ efflux
    • B. Slow Ca2+ influx + delayed K+ channel opening
    • C. Immediate Na+ channel inactivation
    • D. Increased Cl- permeability
    • E. Na+-K+ Pump activation
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Slow Ca2+ influx + delayed K+ channel opening

    The plateau of the ventricular action potential is maintained by a balance between inward Ca2+ current through slow L-type Ca2+ channels and a delayed, reduced outward K+ current. This prolongs depolarisation to about 200-300 ms, allowing sustained contraction and a long refractory period that prevents tetany. Rapid Na+ influx accounts only for phase 0, and immediate Na+ inactivation or Cl- changes do not create the plateau.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 9: Cardiac Muscle; The Heart as a Pump and Function of the Heart Valves

  2. A 60-year-old man with long-standing uncontrolled hypertension undergoes haemodynamic evaluation. Despite a marked increase in left ventricular systolic pressure and myocardial oxygen consumption, only a modest increase in stroke work is observed. Which parameter is most likely impaired?

    • A. Cardiac reserve
    • B. Cardiac index
    • C. Cardiac efficiency
    • D. Ejection fraction
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Cardiac efficiency

    Cardiac efficiency is the ratio of stroke work output to total energy (oxygen) input. In pressure-overloaded hypertrophy the ventricle consumes much more O2 to generate high pressure but stroke work rises only modestly, so efficiency falls. Cardiac index normalises output to body size, ejection fraction is stroke volume/end-diastolic volume, and cardiac reserve is the maximal output above rest.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 9: Cardiac Muscle; The Heart as a Pump and Function of the Heart Valves

  3. A 65-year-old woman with diastolic dysfunction undergoes Doppler echocardiography. After closure of the aortic valve, left ventricular pressure falls rapidly while ventricular volume remains unchanged until the mitral valve opens. Which phase is being assessed?

    • A. Rapid ejection
    • B. Isovolumetric relaxation
    • C. Atrial contraction
    • D. Reduced ejection
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Isovolumetric relaxation

    The Wiggers diagram period between aortic valve closure and mitral valve opening, when both valves are closed and ventricular volume is constant while pressure falls rapidly, is isovolumetric relaxation. It is not ejection or filling, and volume does not change because no valve is open. This phase ends when ventricular pressure falls below atrial pressure and the mitral valve opens.

  4. A 68-year-old man develops an acute myocardial infarction due to complete occlusion of the left anterior descending artery. During systole, the infarcted left ventricular wall bulges outward instead of contracting, reducing stroke volume. This abnormal movement is best explained by:

    • A. Increased sympathetic stimulation of the infarcted myocardium
    • B. Stretching of the non-contractile infarcted myocardium by contraction of the surrounding healthy muscle
    • C. Coronary vasodilation causing ventricular wall thinning
    • D. Increased ventricular filling due to elevated venous return
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Stretching of the non-contractile infarcted myocardium by contraction of the surrounding healthy muscle

    After transmural infarction the necrotic segment cannot contract; during systole pressure generated by surrounding healthy muscle bulges and stretches the soft infarcted area outward (paradoxical bulging), wasting stroke work. It does not contract more forcefully, and the effect is mechanical stretching, not electrical re-entry or valve failure.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 9: Cardiac Muscle; The Heart as a Pump and Function of the Heart Valves

  5. A patient receives an intravenous infusion of cardiotropic drug. The subsequent PV loop demonstrates a lower end-systolic volume with increased stroke volume, while end-diastolic volume remains unchanged. Which mechanism best explains this change?

    • A. Increased preload
    • B. Increased myocardial contractility
    • C. Increased afterload
    • D. Reduced venous return
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Increased myocardial contractility

    On a pressure-volume loop, ejecting to a lower end-systolic volume at unchanged end-diastolic volume (preload) and similar afterload indicates increased contractility (inotropy), shifting the end-systolic pressure-volume relation upward and leftward. Reduced afterload alone would not be described as a drug PV loop change without pressure data, and increased preload would raise end-diastolic volume.

  6. A patient with severe aortic stenosis has a prolonged interval between mitral valve closure and aortic valve opening. Which phase of the cardiac cycle is most likely prolonged?

    • A. Rapid ventricular filling
    • B. Isovolumetric contraction
    • C. Ventricular ejection
    • D. Atrial systole
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Isovolumetric contraction

    The interval between mitral valve closure and aortic valve opening, when both valves are closed and ventricular volume is constant while pressure rises, is isovolumetric contraction. In severe aortic stenosis ventricular pressure must rise much higher to open the stenotic valve, so this phase is prolonged. It is not ejection, which begins only after the aortic valve opens.

  7. A patient's Wiggers diagram shows a prominent 'a' wave in the left atrial pressure curve occurring immediately after the P wave on the ECG. At this moment, which change in left ventricular volume is most likely?

    • A. Left ventricular volume is rapidly decreasing due to ventricular ejection.
    • B. Left ventricular volume reaches its maximum because atrial systole completes ventricular filling.
    • C. Left ventricular volume remains constant during isovolumetric contraction.
    • D. Left ventricular volume decreases because the mitral valve has closed.
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Left ventricular volume reaches its maximum because atrial systole completes ventricular filling.

    The 'a' wave of the atrial pressure curve is produced by atrial contraction (atrial systole shortly after the ECG P wave). At this moment atrial pressure rises and the final increment of blood enters the ventricle, so left ventricular volume reaches its maximum at end-diastole just before mitral closure. Ventricular contraction produces the subsequent 'c' wave.

  8. An elderly woman presents with progressive dyspnea and palpitations. Examination reveals a low-pitched, rumbling, mid-diastolic murmur best heard at the apex with the bell of the stethoscope. What best explains this abnormal sound?

    • A. Delayed closure of the aortic valve from LV hypertrophy
    • B. Turbulent flow across a narrowed mitral valve during diastolic filling
    • C. Incomplete closure of the mitral valve causing systolic backflow
    • D. Rapid ventricular filling producing a third heart sound
    • E. Calcification of the aortic valve producing an ejection systolic murmur
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Turbulent flow across a narrowed mitral valve during diastolic filling

    A low-pitched rumbling mid-diastolic murmur at the apex with progressive dyspnoea and palpitations (often atrial fibrillation) is mitral stenosis. Turbulent flow across the narrowed mitral valve during diastolic filling produces the murmur, best heard with the bell in left lateral position. Systolic murmurs, aortic stenosis (ejection systolic), and mitral regurgitation (pansystolic) have different timing and character.

  9. Dicrotic notch on the aortic pressure curve is due to:

    • A. Closure of mitral valve
    • B. Closure of tricuspid valve
    • C. Closure of aortic valve
    • D. Closure of pulmonary valve
    • E. Rapid filling of left ventricle
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Closure of aortic valve

    The dicrotic (incisura) notch on the aortic pressure tracing occurs at aortic valve closure: forward flow briefly reverses, the valve snaps shut, and elastic recoil produces a small secondary pressure rise (dicrotic wave). It marks the end of systole and start of diastole, not mitral closure or opening of the aortic valve.

  10. Digitalis increases cardiac contractility primarily by which mechanism?

    • A. Inhibition of Na/K-ATPase intracellular Ca2+
    • B. Activation of B-adrenergic receptors
    • C. Stimulation of sarcoplasmic reticulum Ca2+ pump (SERCA)
    • D. Inhibition of L-type Ca2+ channels
    • E. Opening of ATP-sensitive K+ channels
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Inhibition of Na/K-ATPase intracellular Ca2+

    Digitalis (cardiac glycosides) inhibits the sarcolemmal Na+/K+-ATPase, raising intracellular Na+. The reduced Na+ gradient impairs forward Na+/Ca2+ exchange (and may reverse it), so intracellular Ca2+ rises and more Ca2+ is available to troponin, increasing contractility. The primary step is pump inhibition; Ca2+ accumulation is secondary via the exchanger.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 9: Cardiac Muscle; The Heart as a Pump and Function of the Heart Valves

  11. Digoxin given in cardiac failure serves to strengthen cardiac myocytes by blocking Na – K pump as ;

    • A. It Inhibits Sodium Calcium exchange.
    • B. It promotes ATP intracellular deposit
    • C. It enhances intracellular potassium
    • D. It inhibits Calcium intracellular reserve
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. It Inhibits Sodium Calcium exchange.

    Digoxin blocks the Na+-K+ pump, intracellular Na+ rises, and the Na+-Ca2+ exchanger extrudes less Ca2+ (its Ca2+ removal is inhibited/reduced). The resulting rise in cytosolic Ca2+ strengthens contraction. Stating simply 'it inhibits sodium-calcium exchange' is imprecise: the exchanger is not directly blocked, its driving gradient is reduced. The intended mechanism is pump blockade leading secondarily to reduced Ca2+ extrusion.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 9: Cardiac Muscle; The Heart as a Pump and Function of the Heart Valves

  12. During analysis of a Wiggers diagram, the QRS complex coincides with the beginning of the 'c' wave on the atrial pressure tracing. Which event best explains this finding?

    • A. Atrial contraction transiently increases atrial pressure while completing ventricular filling.
    • B. Early ventricular systole causes closure and bulging of the atrioventricular valve into the atrium, transiently increasing atrial pressure.
    • C. Opening of the aortic valve allows ventricular ejection, producing the c wave.
    • D. Ventricular relaxation lowers ventricular pressure below atrial pressure, initiating rapid ventricular filling.
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Early ventricular systole causes closure and bulging of the atrioventricular valve into the atrium, transiently increasing atrial pressure.

    On the Wiggers/jugular-atrial pressure diagram the 'c' wave begins at the start of ventricular systole (with the QRS complex): ventricular pressure rises, the AV valves close and bulge into the atria, transiently raising atrial pressure. The 'a' wave precedes it (atrial contraction) and the 'v' wave occurs later during atrial filling against closed AV valves.

  13. In myocardial ischaemia, disruption of which structure most directly impairs electrical spread between cardiac muscle cells?

    • A. Z line
    • B. Gap junctions
    • C. Sarcoplasmic reticulum
    • D. T tubule
    • E. Desmosome
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Gap junctions

    Cardiac myocytes are electrically coupled by gap junctions concentrated in intercalated discs, which allow ions and action potentials to pass directly from cell to cell so the myocardium acts as a functional syncytium. Ischaemic disruption of gap junctions most directly impairs electrical spread, causing conduction slowing and arrhythmia. Desmosomes provide mechanical, not electrical, coupling.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 9: Cardiac Muscle; The Heart as a Pump and Function of the Heart Valves

  14. V wave in jugular venous pulse is prominent in which condition?

    • A. Aortic stenosis
    • B. Aortic regurgitation
    • C. Mitral stenosis
    • D. Mitral regurgitation
    • E. Tricuspid regurgitation
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: E. Tricuspid regurgitation

    In tricuspid regurgitation systolic ejection of blood backward into the right atrium produces a large, prominent 'v' wave in the jugular venous pulse (often fused c-v wave), with rapid y descent. Other causes of a large v wave are less direct; tricuspid stenosis produces a prominent a wave, and normal JVP has small v waves.

  15. Normal splitting of the second heart sound (S2) occurs because:

    • A. Pulmonary valve closes before aortic valve
    • B. Both valves close exactly together always
    • C. Mitral valve closes after tricuspid valve
    • D. Aortic valve closes before pulmonary valve
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Aortic valve closes before pulmonary valve

    A2 precedes P2 because left-sided pressures close the aortic valve earlier; inspiration delays P2 further, widening the split. Reversed order would be abnormal (paradoxical splitting).

  16. The heart acts as a functional syncytium mainly because cardiac muscle cells are connected by:

    • A. Gap junctions (in intercalated discs)
    • B. Tight junctions only
    • C. Desmosomes alone without gap junctions
    • D. Synaptic vesicles
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Gap junctions (in intercalated discs)

    Intercalated discs contain gap junctions that allow ions and action potentials to spread cell-to-cell, so atria and ventricles each contract as a syncytium. Desmosomes provide mechanical strength but do not conduct the impulse.

  17. Maximum aortic pressure (about 120 mmHg) is reached during which phase of the cardiac cycle?

    • A. Isovolumetric relaxation
    • B. Atrial diastole
    • C. Ejection phase of ventricular systole
    • D. Isovolumetric contraction before valve opening
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Ejection phase of ventricular systole

    During ventricular ejection, blood driven into the aorta raises aortic pressure to its systolic peak (~120 mmHg). Pressure falls during diastole to about 80 mmHg as blood runs off to the periphery.

  18. Afterload of the left ventricle is increased mainly by an increase in:

    • A. End-diastolic volume alone (preload)
    • B. Venous compliance
    • C. Heart rate alone
    • D. Arterial (aortic) pressure against which the ventricle ejects
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Arterial (aortic) pressure against which the ventricle ejects

    Afterload is the load/pressure the ventricle must overcome to eject blood, determined chiefly by aortic/arterial pressure. End-diastolic volume determines preload, not afterload — this corrects the recall note, where textbook physiology prevails.

  19. In the ventricular action potential, maximum Ca2+ influx occurs during which phase?

    • A. Plateau phase (phase 2)
    • B. Phase 0 upstroke (Na+ influx)
    • C. Phase 4 resting potential
    • D. Phase 3 rapid repolarization (K+ efflux)
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Plateau phase (phase 2)

    Phase 2 plateau is maintained by Ca2+ influx through L-type calcium channels balanced by K+ efflux, and this Ca2+ triggers contraction. Phase 0 is rapid Na+ influx in working ventricular myocytes.

  20. The prolonged plateau phase of the ventricular muscle action potential is primarily caused by:

    • A. Slow Na+ influx through funny (If) channels
    • B. Cl- influx through ligand-gated channels
    • C. Rapid Na+ influx through fast voltage-gated channels
    • D. Rapid K+ efflux through inward rectifier channels
    • E. Slow Ca2+ influx through L-type calcium channels balancing K+ efflux
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: E. Slow Ca2+ influx through L-type calcium channels balancing K+ efflux

    The plateau (phase 2) of the cardiac action potential results from slow, prolonged Ca2+ entry through L-type (slow) calcium channels, which counterbalances K+ efflux. This extends the action potential to 0.2-0.3 seconds (vs 1-5 ms in skeletal muscle) and makes the refractory period nearly as long as the contraction, preventing tetanization.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 9: Cardiac Muscle; The Heart as a Pump and Function of the Heart Valves

  21. A 62-year-old man with heart failure is started on digoxin. The drug increases myocardial contractility mainly by:

    • A. Inhibiting the Na+/K+ ATPase, raising intracellular Na+ and thereby intracellular Ca2+ via the Na+/Ca2+ exchanger
    • B. Opening ATP-sensitive K+ channels
    • C. Blocking L-type calcium channels
    • D. Blocking cardiac beta-1 adrenergic receptors
    • E. Stimulating muscarinic receptors
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Inhibiting the Na+/K+ ATPase, raising intracellular Na+ and thereby intracellular Ca2+ via the Na+/Ca2+ exchanger

    Digoxin inhibits the Na+/K+ ATPase, so intracellular Na+ rises. This reduces Ca2+ extrusion by the Na+/Ca2+ exchanger (which uses the Na+ gradient), raising cytosolic Ca2+ available to troponin C. More Ca2+ means stronger actin-myosin interaction — a positive inotropic effect, the basis of digitalis therapy in heart failure.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 9: Cardiac Muscle; The Heart as a Pump and Function of the Heart Valves

  22. Atrial and ventricular muscle masses contract as separate functional syncytia because:

    • A. The SA node fires too rapidly for joint contraction
    • B. Gap junctions are absent in cardiac muscle
    • C. The fibrous atrioventricular septum electrically insulates the atria from the ventricles
    • D. Purkinje fibers conduct too slowly to synchronize them
    • E. Coronary blood flow physically separates them
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. The fibrous atrioventricular septum electrically insulates the atria from the ventricles

    Intercalated discs with gap junctions let each chamber mass behave as one syncytium, but the fibrous skeleton of the heart blocks direct electrical spread between atria and ventricles. The AV node is the only conducting bridge, imposing the delay that lets atria finish emptying before ventricles contract.

  23. The first heart sound (S1) is produced by:

    • A. Closure of the atrioventricular (mitral and tricuspid) valves at the onset of ventricular systole
    • B. Closure of the semilunar valves
    • C. Rapid ventricular filling
    • D. Opening of the aortic valve
    • E. Atrial contraction against a stiff ventricle
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Closure of the atrioventricular (mitral and tricuspid) valves at the onset of ventricular systole

    When ventricular pressure exceeds atrial pressure at the start of systole, the AV valves snap shut, and the sudden deceleration of blood plus valve vibration produces S1. It marks the beginning of isovolumetric contraction. S2, by contrast, is semilunar valve closure at the end of systole.

  24. During the cardiac cycle, all four heart valves are closed during:

    • A. Atrial systole
    • B. Rapid ventricular filling
    • C. Isovolumetric contraction and isovolumetric relaxation
    • D. Slow (reduced) ejection
    • E. Rapid ejection phase
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Isovolumetric contraction and isovolumetric relaxation

    In isovolumetric contraction, ventricular pressure is rising but has not yet exceeded arterial pressure (semilunar closed) while already exceeding atrial pressure (AV closed) — so volume is constant. The mirror image occurs in isovolumetric relaxation. These are the only phases with no valve open and no change in ventricular volume.

  25. A patient's end-diastolic volume is 120 mL and end-systolic volume is 50 mL. The ejection fraction is approximately:

    • A. 50%
    • B. 42%
    • C. 70%
    • D. 58%
    • E. 33%
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. 58%

    Stroke volume = EDV − ESV = 120 − 50 = 70 mL. Ejection fraction = SV/EDV = 70/120 ≈ 58%. A normal resting EF is roughly 55-70%; a falling EF is a hallmark of systolic heart failure.

  26. Acute hemorrhage reduces stroke volume primarily because:

    • A. Decreased venous return lowers end-diastolic volume, reducing stretch-mediated contraction (Frank-Starling mechanism)
    • B. Afterload increases sharply
    • C. The valves fail to close
    • D. Myocardial contractility increases
    • E. Heart rate falls reflexly
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Decreased venous return lowers end-diastolic volume, reducing stretch-mediated contraction (Frank-Starling mechanism)

    Blood loss lowers venous return and therefore end-diastolic volume (preload). By the Frank-Starling law, less diastolic stretch means less forceful systolic contraction, so stroke volume falls. This is heterometric regulation — output matched to input via fiber length, independent of nerves.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 9: Cardiac Muscle; The Heart as a Pump and Function of the Heart Valves

  27. The aortic valve opens when:

    • A. Left ventricular pressure exceeds aortic diastolic pressure
    • B. Ventricular pressure falls to zero
    • C. The mitral valve closes
    • D. Atrial pressure exceeds ventricular pressure
    • E. The SA node fires
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Left ventricular pressure exceeds aortic diastolic pressure

    During isovolumetric contraction, LV pressure climbs steeply; the moment it exceeds the diastolic pressure in the aorta (≈80 mmHg), the aortic valve is forced open and rapid ejection begins. Valve opening is purely pressure-driven — no muscle controls the leaflets.

  28. Most (about 70%) of ventricular filling occurs:

    • A. During the first third of diastole (rapid filling phase), before the atria contract
    • B. During ventricular systole
    • C. During isovolumetric relaxation
    • D. Only during exercise
    • E. During atrial systole
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. During the first third of diastole (rapid filling phase), before the atria contract

    When the AV valves open, blood that accumulated in the atria during systole rushes into the relaxed ventricles — the rapid filling phase accounts for most of diastolic filling. Atrial systole (the atrial kick) tops up only the final 20-30%, which is why its loss in atrial fibrillation is tolerated by normal hearts but dangerous in stiff ventricles.

  29. In excitation-contraction coupling of cardiac muscle, the immediate trigger for massive Ca2+ release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum is:

    • A. Acetylcholine binding to muscarinic receptors
    • B. ATP hydrolysis by myosin
    • C. A small Ca2+ entry through L-type channels via T-tubules, causing Ca2+-induced Ca2+ release
    • D. Na+ entry through fast channels
    • E. K+ efflux during repolarization
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. A small Ca2+ entry through L-type channels via T-tubules, causing Ca2+-induced Ca2+ release

    Depolarization travels down T-tubules and opens L-type Ca2+ channels; the small entering Ca2+ binds ryanodine receptors on the sarcoplasmic reticulum, triggering a much larger Ca2+ release (Ca2+-induced Ca2+ release). This released Ca2+ binds troponin C and initiates contraction. The extensive T-tubule system is essential because cardiac cells are large.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 9: Cardiac Muscle; The Heart as a Pump and Function of the Heart Valves

  30. Unlike skeletal muscle, cardiac muscle cannot be tetanized because:

    • A. It has no sarcoplasmic reticulum
    • B. Its absolute refractory period lasts nearly as long as the mechanical contraction itself
    • C. Gap junctions block summation of stimuli
    • D. It lacks troponin
    • E. It fatigues too rapidly
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Its absolute refractory period lasts nearly as long as the mechanical contraction itself

    The long plateau keeps the ventricular fiber refractory for 0.25-0.30 s — essentially the whole contraction time. A second stimulus therefore cannot summate with the first, so sustained tetanic contraction (which would stop the heart as a pump) is impossible. This is a vital protective design of cardiac muscle.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 9: Cardiac Muscle; The Heart as a Pump and Function of the Heart Valves

  31. The preferential energy substrate of the healthy heart is:

    • A. Glucose
    • B. Ketone bodies exclusively
    • C. Amino acids
    • D. Fatty acids
    • E. Lactate exclusively
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Fatty acids

    Cardiac muscle is an aerobic, fatigue-resistant tissue that derives most of its ATP from oxidation of fatty acids (with lactate and glucose as secondary fuels). This differs from the brain's glucose dependence and explains why the heart extracts ~70% of delivered oxygen even at rest — it has little anaerobic reserve.

  32. Loss of the atrial kick in atrial fibrillation reduces cardiac output most dangerously in patients with:

    • A. Stiff, poorly compliant ventricles (e.g., ventricular hypertrophy), where atrial systole contributes up to 30% of filling
    • B. Mitral regurgitation
    • C. Chronic anemia
    • D. Isolated aortic stenosis with normal compliance
    • E. Hyperthyroidism
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Stiff, poorly compliant ventricles (e.g., ventricular hypertrophy), where atrial systole contributes up to 30% of filling

    Atrial systole normally tops up ventricular filling by 20-30%. In a stiff, hypertrophied ventricle that fills poorly during passive diastole, this atrial contribution becomes critical — losing it in AF can drop cardiac output sharply and precipitate heart failure, whereas compliant ventricles tolerate the loss.

  33. Coronary blood flow to the left ventricle occurs mainly during:

    • A. Diastole, when the relaxing myocardium no longer compresses the intramyocardial vessels
    • B. Equally in systole and diastole
    • C. Atrial systole
    • D. Isovolumetric contraction
    • E. Ventricular systole
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Diastole, when the relaxing myocardium no longer compresses the intramyocardial vessels

    During systole, contracting left ventricular muscle squeezes its own intramyocardial coronary vessels nearly shut, so little flow occurs despite high aortic pressure. In diastole the muscle relaxes, vessels reopen, and stored aortic elastic energy drives flow — which is why tachycardia (shortened diastole) threatens coronary perfusion.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 9: Cardiac Muscle; The Heart as a Pump and Function of the Heart Valves

  34. Aortic regurgitation typically produces a wide pulse pressure because:

    • A. Venous return falls
    • B. Heart rate decreases markedly
    • C. Arterial compliance increases
    • D. Stroke volume falls
    • E. A large stroke volume is ejected while blood runs back into the ventricle during diastole, lowering diastolic pressure
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: E. A large stroke volume is ejected while blood runs back into the ventricle during diastole, lowering diastolic pressure

    In aortic regurgitation the ventricle ejects a big stroke volume (raising systolic pressure), but during diastole blood leaks back through the incompetent valve, so aortic diastolic pressure collapses. High systolic minus low diastolic gives the characteristic wide (bounding) pulse pressure, e.g., 160/60.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 9: Cardiac Muscle; The Heart as a Pump and Function of the Heart Valves

  35. The maximum rate of ventricular pressure rise (dP/dt max) during isovolumetric contraction is used clinically as an index of:

    • A. Afterload
    • B. Valve competence
    • C. Preload
    • D. Heart rate
    • E. Myocardial contractility
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: E. Myocardial contractility

    Because no blood leaves the ventricle during isovolumetric contraction, how fast pressure rises reflects purely how vigorously the muscle is contracting — independent of load. A higher dP/dt max means greater contractility (homeometric regulation), as seen with sympathetic stimulation or positive inotropes.

  36. In mitral stenosis, cardiac output falls during exercise mainly because:

    • A. Pericardial pressure rises
    • B. The aortic valve cannot open
    • C. Afterload rises sharply
    • D. Contractility is directly depressed by the lesion
    • E. The stenotic valve limits ventricular filling, reducing end-diastolic volume and therefore stroke volume
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: E. The stenotic valve limits ventricular filling, reducing end-diastolic volume and therefore stroke volume

    A narrowed mitral orifice restricts diastolic flow from left atrium to ventricle, so end-diastolic volume cannot rise to meet demand. By the Frank-Starling mechanism, the reduced preload yields a reduced stroke volume — the ventricle simply cannot be filled, however hard it tries to pump.

  37. Isovolumetric relaxation ends when:

    • A. The semilunar valves open
    • B. Atrial systole begins
    • C. The SA node fires
    • D. Aortic pressure exceeds ventricular pressure
    • E. Ventricular pressure falls below atrial pressure, allowing the AV valves to open
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: E. Ventricular pressure falls below atrial pressure, allowing the AV valves to open

    After the semilunar valves close, the relaxing ventricle's pressure plummets with all valves shut. The moment ventricular pressure drops just below atrial pressure, the AV valves are pushed open and rapid filling begins — ending isovolumetric relaxation. Valve motion is entirely driven by these pressure gradients.

  38. Sympathetic stimulation increases cardiac output by all of the following EXCEPT:

    • A. Prolonging the action potential plateau
    • B. Increasing venous return via venoconstriction
    • C. Increasing heart rate
    • D. Increasing myocardial contractility
    • E. Increasing AV nodal conduction velocity
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Prolonging the action potential plateau

    Sympathetic (beta-1) activation raises heart rate, contractility, conduction velocity, and venous return — but it SHORTENS, not prolongs, the action potential and systole. This shortening is essential: at high heart rates diastole must be preserved for filling, so systole is abbreviated, not extended.

  39. A 55-year-old man has severe aortic stenosis. His pulse pressure is expected to be:

    • A. Equal to the mean arterial pressure
    • B. Narrowed, because the fixed obstruction limits stroke volume
    • C. Unchanged from normal
    • D. Widened
    • E. Reversed (diastolic higher than systolic)
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Narrowed, because the fixed obstruction limits stroke volume

    The stenotic aortic valve throttles ejection, so stroke volume — and hence the systolic pressure surge — falls, while diastolic pressure is maintained. A small stroke volume into the arteries gives a narrow pulse pressure (e.g., 100/80), the opposite of the wide pulse of aortic regurgitation.

  40. An increase in ventricular end-diastolic volume stretches cardiac muscle fibres. According to the Frank-Starling mechanism, the immediate result is:

    • A. Decreased stroke volume because the ventricle is overstretched at normal volumes
    • B. Complete inhibition of the next contraction
    • C. Increased force of contraction and increased stroke volume
    • D. No change in stroke volume because preload does not affect the heart
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Increased force of contraction and increased stroke volume

    Within physiological limits, greater filling stretches fibres toward their optimal length and increases calcium sensitivity, so the ventricle ejects a larger stroke volume and matches output to venous return. Only at extreme, pathological distension does overstretch reduce performance; normal preload changes do affect stroke volume.
