Home › Physiology MCQs › Electrocardiographic Interpretation of Cardiac Muscle and Coronary Blood Flow Abnormalities: Vectorial Analysis

Vectorial analysis interprets the electrocardiogram by treating the heart's electrical activity as a single moving vector. The direction and size of this vector at each moment determine the shape of the waves recorded in each lead. Damage to heart muscle or reduced coronary blood flow changes the vector, producing characteristic changes in the tracing.

  1. In left ventricular hypertrophy, the mean QRS axis typically:

    • A. Shifts left beyond -30° because the increased left ventricular mass pulls the vector leftward
    • B. Becomes indeterminate
    • C. Shifts right beyond +100°
    • D. Remains fixed at +59°
    • E. Reverses by exactly 180°
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Shifts left beyond -30° because the increased left ventricular mass pulls the vector leftward

    The mean QRS vector follows the bulk of ventricular mass. A hypertrophied left ventricle generates a larger leftward electrical force, dragging the axis beyond −30° (left axis deviation). Right ventricular hypertrophy does the mirror image, pushing the axis beyond +100°.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 12: Electrocardiographic Interpretation of Cardiac Muscle and Coronary Blood Flow Abnormalities: Vectorial Analysis

  2. A current of injury producing ST-segment deviation occurs when:

    • A. Pericarditis is present
    • B. Electrodes are misplaced
    • C. Ischemic tissue remains partially depolarized during diastole, creating a voltage difference with normal tissue
    • D. The SA node fails
    • E. Tissue is fully repolarized
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Ischemic tissue remains partially depolarized during diastole, creating a voltage difference with normal tissue

    Injured myocardium cannot fully repolarize, so it stays relatively negative (depolarized) during electrical diastole while healthy tissue returns to a negative resting state — actually the injured zone is LESS negative, creating a voltage gradient. This injury current shifts the ST segment: elevation in leads facing the injury, depression in opposite leads.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 12: Electrocardiographic Interpretation of Cardiac Muscle and Coronary Blood Flow Abnormalities: Vectorial Analysis

  3. Transmural myocardial infarction typically produces:

    • A. ST-segment elevation in leads facing the infarcted wall
    • B. ST depression only
    • C. Peaked T waves only
    • D. Shortened QRS
    • E. No ECG change at all
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. ST-segment elevation in leads facing the infarcted wall

    When infarction spans the full wall thickness, the injury current flows outward toward the overlying electrodes during systole, elevating the ST segment in those leads. This is the ECG signature of STEMI and the trigger for urgent reperfusion. Subendocardial (non-transmural) ischemia instead gives ST depression.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 12: Electrocardiographic Interpretation of Cardiac Muscle and Coronary Blood Flow Abnormalities: Vectorial Analysis

  4. A pathologic Q wave suggesting old myocardial infarction is defined as:

    • A. A Q wave confined to aVR
    • B. Any visible q wave
    • C. A QRS wider than 0.12 s
    • D. An absent Q in V6
    • E. A Q wave wider than 0.04 s or deeper than 25% of the R wave
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: E. A Q wave wider than 0.04 s or deeper than 25% of the R wave

    Dead myocardium cannot depolarize, so the electrode over an old infarct records the receding wave from the opposite wall as a deep negative deflection. A Q wider than one small square (0.04 s) or deeper than a quarter of the R wave marks pathologic necrosis, distinguishing it from tiny normal septal q waves.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 12: Electrocardiographic Interpretation of Cardiac Muscle and Coronary Blood Flow Abnormalities: Vectorial Analysis

  5. Occlusion of the left anterior descending coronary artery most commonly infarcts the:

    • A. Lateral wall only
    • B. Anterior wall and anterior septum (leads V1-V4)
    • C. Posterior wall
    • D. Inferior wall
    • E. Right ventricle alone
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Anterior wall and anterior septum (leads V1-V4)

    The LAD supplies the anterior wall and anterior two-thirds of the septum — the largest myocardial territory, reflected in leads V1-V4. Its occlusion causes anterior MI, the most dangerous because of the large muscle mass at risk and the high incidence of pump failure and malignant arrhythmias.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 12: Electrocardiographic Interpretation of Cardiac Muscle and Coronary Blood Flow Abnormalities: Vectorial Analysis

  6. Inferior wall myocardial infarction (leads II, III, aVF) most often results from occlusion of the:

    • A. Posterior descending in every person
    • B. Left circumflex
    • C. Right coronary artery
    • D. Left main
    • E. Left anterior descending
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Right coronary artery

    The right coronary artery supplies the inferior wall in most people, seen in leads II, III, and aVF. Because the RCA also feeds the SA and AV nodes, inferior MI may complicate with sinus bradycardia or AV block — a useful bedside clue to the culprit artery.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 12: Electrocardiographic Interpretation of Cardiac Muscle and Coronary Blood Flow Abnormalities: Vectorial Analysis

  7. Right bundle branch block is characterized by:

    • A. Short PR interval
    • B. ST elevation in all leads
    • C. QRS wider than 0.12 s with an rsR' pattern in V1
    • D. Delta wave
    • E. Narrow QRS complex
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. QRS wider than 0.12 s with an rsR' pattern in V1

    With the right bundle blocked, the left ventricle depolarizes normally via Purkinje fibers while the right ventricle is activated late by slow muscle-to-muscle spread — producing a wide QRS with the classic rsR' (rabbit ears) in V1 overlying the right ventricle and a broad S in V6.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 12: Electrocardiographic Interpretation of Cardiac Muscle and Coronary Blood Flow Abnormalities: Vectorial Analysis

  8. The QRS is widened in bundle branch block because:

    • A. The SA node fires twice
    • B. Repolarization is prolonged
    • C. One ventricle is depolarized by slow cell-to-cell spread instead of the rapid Purkinje system
    • D. The atrial rate increases
    • E. The AV node is completely blocked
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. One ventricle is depolarized by slow cell-to-cell spread instead of the rapid Purkinje system

    Normally Purkinje fibers flash-depolarize each ventricle in ~0.08 s. When a bundle is blocked, the affected ventricle can only be reached by impulse creeping from cell to cell through ordinary muscle — far slower — so total ventricular depolarization time (QRS width) exceeds 0.12 s.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 12: Electrocardiographic Interpretation of Cardiac Muscle and Coronary Blood Flow Abnormalities: Vectorial Analysis

  9. A 58-year-old man has crushing chest pain. ECG shows ST elevation in V1-V4. The most likely site of coronary occlusion is the:

    • A. Posterior descending artery
    • B. Left anterior descending artery
    • C. Left circumflex artery
    • D. Right coronary artery
    • E. SA nodal artery
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Left anterior descending artery

    ST elevation in V1-V4 localizes the injury to the anterior wall and septum — the LAD territory. This is a STEMI equivalent demanding immediate reperfusion (primary PCI or thrombolysis), because every minute of delay enlarges the infarct.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 12: Electrocardiographic Interpretation of Cardiac Muscle and Coronary Blood Flow Abnormalities: Vectorial Analysis

  10. Subendocardial ischemia typically produces:

    • A. Shortened QT interval
    • B. ST elevation
    • C. Tall peaked T waves only
    • D. Pathologic Q waves within minutes
    • E. ST-segment depression and T-wave inversion in the affected leads
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: E. ST-segment depression and T-wave inversion in the affected leads

    When only the inner (subendocardial) layer is ischemic, the injury vector points away from the overlying epicardial electrode, depressing the ST segment rather than elevating it. This pattern defines NSTEMI/unstable angina — still an acute coronary syndrome, managed without emergent PCI unless high-risk features exist.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 12: Electrocardiographic Interpretation of Cardiac Muscle and Coronary Blood Flow Abnormalities: Vectorial Analysis

  11. Left bundle branch block shows:

    • A. Delta waves
    • B. rsR' pattern in V1
    • C. Narrow QRS complex
    • D. Sawtooth baseline
    • E. Wide QRS with broad monophasic R waves in V5-V6 and absent septal q waves
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: E. Wide QRS with broad monophasic R waves in V5-V6 and absent septal q waves

    With the left bundle blocked, the septum depolarizes right-to-left (reversing normal), and the massive left ventricle is activated late and slowly — giving a very wide QRS with a broad, notched monophasic R in lateral leads (V5-V6) and loss of the normal septal q. LBBB often masks MI changes.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 12: Electrocardiographic Interpretation of Cardiac Muscle and Coronary Blood Flow Abnormalities: Vectorial Analysis

  12. In right ventricular hypertrophy, the ECG typically shows:

    • A. Left axis deviation
    • B. Upright T wave in aVR
    • C. Normal axis
    • D. Right axis deviation beyond +100° with a tall R wave in V1
    • E. Short PR interval
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Right axis deviation beyond +100° with a tall R wave in V1

    Extra right ventricular mass pulls the mean vector rightward past +100°, and the dominant rightward forces inscribe a tall R wave in V1 (normally rS). Causes include chronic lung disease, pulmonary stenosis, and pulmonary hypertension — all raising right-sided pressures.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 12: Electrocardiographic Interpretation of Cardiac Muscle and Coronary Blood Flow Abnormalities: Vectorial Analysis

  13. Generalized low voltage on the ECG (small complexes in all leads) can be caused by:

    • A. Sinus tachycardia
    • B. Thin chest wall
    • C. Hyperkalemia
    • D. Ventricular hypertrophy
    • E. Pericardial effusion electrically insulating the heart
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: E. Pericardial effusion electrically insulating the heart

    Fluid around the heart short-circuits and dampens the electrical signals reaching the skin electrodes, shrinking all complexes. Low voltage is also seen in hypothyroidism, obesity, and COPD hyperinflation — all effectively increasing the distance or insulation between heart and electrodes.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 12: Electrocardiographic Interpretation of Cardiac Muscle and Coronary Blood Flow Abnormalities: Vectorial Analysis

  14. During the very early phase of transmural ischemia, before ST elevation develops, the ECG may show:

    • A. Flattened P waves
    • B. Pathologic Q waves
    • C. Complete heart block
    • D. Tall, peaked T waves (hyperacute T) in the affected leads
    • E. Prominent U waves
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Tall, peaked T waves (hyperacute T) in the affected leads

    Within minutes of coronary occlusion, the ischemic zone's potassium handling fails and the T wave becomes tall and peaked (hyperacute T) — the earliest ECG sign, preceding ST elevation. Recognizing it allows reperfusion before transmural necrosis completes.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 12: Electrocardiographic Interpretation of Cardiac Muscle and Coronary Blood Flow Abnormalities: Vectorial Analysis

  15. A patient with left circumflex artery occlusion would be expected to show ischemic changes best in leads:

    • A. V1 only
    • B. V1-V4
    • C. aVR only
    • D. I, aVL, V5-V6 (lateral wall)
    • E. II, III, aVF
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. I, aVL, V5-V6 (lateral wall)

    The circumflex artery wraps around to supply the lateral wall, reflected in leads I, aVL, V5-V6. Lateral MI may be electrically subtle (no dramatic anterior ST elevation) but still threatens significant muscle and the mitral apparatus.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 12: Electrocardiographic Interpretation of Cardiac Muscle and Coronary Blood Flow Abnormalities: Vectorial Analysis

  16. Lead I shows a predominantly negative QRS and lead aVF a predominantly positive QRS. The mean QRS axis is:

    • A. Normal
    • B. Leftward
    • C. Posterior
    • D. Indeterminate
    • E. Rightward (right axis deviation)
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: E. Rightward (right axis deviation)

    Lead I points left (0°); negativity there with positivity in aVF (+90°) places the vector in the lower-right quadrant (+90° to +180°) — right axis deviation. Think RVH, right bundle branch block, or lateral MI shifting forces rightward.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 12: Electrocardiographic Interpretation of Cardiac Muscle and Coronary Blood Flow Abnormalities: Vectorial Analysis

  17. T-wave inversion in the chest leads following chest pain most likely indicates:

    • A. Benign early repolarization
    • B. Digoxin effect only
    • C. Always a normal variant
    • D. Myocardial ischemia or evolving infarction
    • E. Pericarditis (which elevates ST diffusely)
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Myocardial ischemia or evolving infarction

    Inverted T waves reflect abnormal repolarization of ischemic but viable myocardium. Deep symmetric T-wave inversion (e.g., Wellens pattern in V2-V3) warns of critical LAD disease and impending anterior MI even after pain resolves — a do-not-miss send-up favorite.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 12: Electrocardiographic Interpretation of Cardiac Muscle and Coronary Blood Flow Abnormalities: Vectorial Analysis

  18. The injury potential in acute myocardial infarction is recorded during:

    • A. Atrial systole
    • B. Electrical diastole (the TQ segment), when injured tissue remains depolarized relative to healthy tissue
    • C. Systole only
    • D. It is never recorded
    • E. The PR interval
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Electrical diastole (the TQ segment), when injured tissue remains depolarized relative to healthy tissue

    The injury potential is a diastolic phenomenon: during the TQ interval (electrical diastole), partially depolarized injured tissue sits at a less negative voltage than healthy resting tissue, so current flows between them. This baseline shift is what we read as ST deviation on the surface ECG.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 12: Electrocardiographic Interpretation of Cardiac Muscle and Coronary Blood Flow Abnormalities: Vectorial Analysis

  19. A 65-year-old hypertensive man has an ECG showing left axis deviation of -50° with tall R waves in V5-V6. The most likely diagnosis is:

    • A. Right ventricular hypertrophy
    • B. Acute anterior MI
    • C. Normal variant
    • D. Left ventricular hypertrophy
    • E. Right bundle branch block
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Left ventricular hypertrophy

    Chronic pressure overload thickens the left ventricle, increasing leftward voltage (tall R in V5-V6, deep S in V1) and dragging the axis left beyond −30°. This ECG pattern in a hypertensive patient is LVH until proven otherwise and predicts heart failure risk.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 12: Electrocardiographic Interpretation of Cardiac Muscle and Coronary Blood Flow Abnormalities: Vectorial Analysis

  20. In Prinzmetal (variant) angina due to coronary vasospasm, the ECG during pain typically shows:

    • A. Pathologic Q waves
    • B. Complete heart block
    • C. Transient ST-segment elevation that resolves when the spasm relaxes
    • D. ST depression
    • E. No ECG change
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Transient ST-segment elevation that resolves when the spasm relaxes

    Prinzmetal angina is transient coronary vasospasm, not plaque rupture. During the spasm the myocardium suffers transmural ischemia (ST elevation, as in STEMI), but when the spasm relaxes the ECG normalizes — distinguishing it from fixed occlusion, though it still risks arrhythmia and MI.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 12: Electrocardiographic Interpretation of Cardiac Muscle and Coronary Blood Flow Abnormalities: Vectorial Analysis
