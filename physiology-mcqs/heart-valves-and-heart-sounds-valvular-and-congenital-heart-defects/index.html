Home › Physiology MCQs › Heart Valves and Heart Sounds; Valvular and Congenital Heart Defects

The heart contains four one-way valves that keep blood moving in the correct direction. The sounds heard with a stethoscope come mainly from the closing of these valves, first the atrioventricular valves and then the semilunar valves. When a valve is narrowed or leaky, or when the heart forms abnormally before birth, blood flow and heart sounds change.

  1. The first heart sound (S1) is produced mainly by:

    • A. Rapid ventricular filling in early diastole
    • B. Closure of the mitral and tricuspid valves
    • C. Opening of the semilunar valves
    • D. Closure of the aortic and pulmonary valves
    • E. Atrial contraction against a stiff ventricle
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Closure of the mitral and tricuspid valves

    S1 marks the beginning of systole and is caused by closure of the atrioventricular (mitral and tricuspid) valves as ventricular pressure rises above atrial pressure. Turbulence from the sudden valve closure and tensing of the chordae tendineae also contribute. S2, in contrast, is caused by closure of the semilunar (aortic and pulmonary) valves.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 23: Heart Valves and Heart Sounds; Valvular and Congenital Heart Defects

  2. A 22-year-old athlete has a second heart sound heard as two components during inspiration but as a single sound during expiration. This finding is best explained by:

    • A. Mitral valve prolapse
    • B. Left bundle branch block
    • C. Delayed aortic valve closure
    • D. Atrial septal defect
    • E. Increased venous return to the right heart during inspiration delaying pulmonary valve closure
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: E. Increased venous return to the right heart during inspiration delaying pulmonary valve closure

    This is physiological (normal) splitting of S2. During inspiration, increased venous return prolongs right ventricular ejection, delaying pulmonary valve closure (P2) so it separates from aortic closure (A2); during expiration the components fuse. It is a normal finding, especially prominent in young people, and should not be confused with the fixed splitting of atrial septal defect.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 23: Heart Valves and Heart Sounds; Valvular and Congenital Heart Defects

  3. A 6-year-old girl has a widely split second heart sound that does NOT vary with respiration. The most likely diagnosis is:

    • A. Mitral regurgitation
    • B. Atrial septal defect
    • C. Ventricular septal defect
    • D. Aortic stenosis
    • E. Patent ductus arteriosus
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Atrial septal defect

    Fixed splitting of S2 is characteristic of atrial septal defect. The left-to-right shunt keeps right ventricular volume constantly high, so pulmonary valve closure is always delayed and the split does not change with respiration. The associated murmur is a systolic ejection murmur from increased pulmonary blood flow, not from flow through the defect itself.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 23: Heart Valves and Heart Sounds; Valvular and Congenital Heart Defects

  4. In severe aortic stenosis the second heart sound may show paradoxical (reversed) splitting. The mechanism is:

    • A. Early aortic valve closure
    • B. Delayed right ventricular ejection
    • C. Prolonged atrial systole
    • D. Delayed left ventricular ejection prolonging aortic valve closure so A2 follows P2
    • E. Early pulmonary valve closure
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Delayed left ventricular ejection prolonging aortic valve closure so A2 follows P2

    In severe aortic stenosis, prolonged left ventricular ejection delays aortic valve closure (A2) so it occurs AFTER pulmonary closure (P2) — the reverse of normal. On inspiration, P2 moves later toward the delayed A2, so the split narrows with inspiration (hence 'paradoxical'). The same pattern occurs in left bundle branch block, which delays left ventricular activation.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 23: Heart Valves and Heart Sounds; Valvular and Congenital Heart Defects

  5. A 70-year-old man presents with exertional syncope and angina. Examination reveals a harsh crescendo-decrescendo systolic murmur at the right second intercostal space radiating to the carotids, with a slow-rising, low-volume carotid pulse. The most likely diagnosis is:

    • A. Aortic regurgitation
    • B. Mitral stenosis
    • C. Aortic stenosis
    • D. Mitral valve prolapse
    • E. Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Aortic stenosis

    This is the classic triad of severe aortic stenosis — angina, syncope, and dyspnea — with a systolic ejection murmur at the aortic area radiating to the neck and pulsus parvus et tardus (small, slow-rising pulse). The murmur is crescendo-decrescendo because flow across the narrowed valve rises and then falls during systole.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 23: Heart Valves and Heart Sounds; Valvular and Congenital Heart Defects

  6. A 35-year-old woman with a history of rheumatic fever has a loud S1, an opening snap, and a low-pitched diastolic rumble at the apex. These findings indicate:

    • A. Tricuspid regurgitation
    • B. Mitral regurgitation
    • C. Aortic regurgitation
    • D. Aortic stenosis
    • E. Mitral stenosis
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: E. Mitral stenosis

    Mitral stenosis produces a loud S1 (the stiff valve snaps shut), an opening snap in early diastole (sudden tensing of the stenotic leaflets as they are forced open), and a diastolic rumble from turbulent flow across the narrowed mitral valve, best heard at the apex with the bell. Rheumatic fever is by far the most common cause of mitral stenosis.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 23: Heart Valves and Heart Sounds; Valvular and Congenital Heart Defects

  7. A 55-year-old man has a holosystolic (pansystolic) murmur at the apex radiating to the axilla. The most likely lesion is:

    • A. Aortic stenosis
    • B. Mitral stenosis
    • C. Mitral regurgitation
    • D. Aortic regurgitation
    • E. Tricuspid stenosis
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Mitral regurgitation

    Mitral regurgitation drives blood backward from the left ventricle into the left atrium throughout systole, producing a holosystolic murmur at the apex that radiates to the axilla. It differs from the ejection murmur of aortic stenosis, which is crescendo-decrescendo, heard at the base, and radiates to the carotids.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 23: Heart Valves and Heart Sounds; Valvular and Congenital Heart Defects

  8. A 40-year-old man has a bounding pulse, a wide pulse pressure, and an early diastolic decrescendo murmur at the left sternal border. The diagnosis is:

    • A. Mitral stenosis
    • B. Pulmonary stenosis
    • C. Aortic regurgitation
    • D. Aortic stenosis
    • E. Mitral regurgitation
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Aortic regurgitation

    Aortic regurgitation allows blood to leak back into the left ventricle during diastole, producing a high systolic and low diastolic pressure (wide pulse pressure), a bounding water-hammer pulse, and an early diastolic decrescendo murmur at the left sternal edge. Chronic aortic regurgitation causes left ventricular volume overload with progressive dilation.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 23: Heart Valves and Heart Sounds; Valvular and Congenital Heart Defects

  9. A 2-month-old infant has a continuous 'machinery' murmur at the left infraclavicular area. The most likely diagnosis is:

    • A. Patent ductus arteriosus
    • B. Ventricular septal defect
    • C. Tetralogy of Fallot
    • D. Coarctation of the aorta
    • E. Atrial septal defect
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Patent ductus arteriosus

    Patent ductus arteriosus produces a continuous murmur because blood flows from the high-pressure aorta into the lower-pressure pulmonary artery throughout both systole and diastole. It is best heard below the left clavicle and has a machinery-like quality, which distinguishes it from murmurs confined to systole or diastole.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 23: Heart Valves and Heart Sounds; Valvular and Congenital Heart Defects

  10. A 4-year-old boy has a harsh holosystolic murmur at the left lower sternal border. The most likely diagnosis is:

    • A. Atrial septal defect
    • B. Ventricular septal defect
    • C. Patent ductus arteriosus
    • D. Pulmonary stenosis
    • E. Mitral valve prolapse
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Ventricular septal defect

    A ventricular septal defect shunts blood from the high-pressure left ventricle to the right ventricle throughout systole, producing a holosystolic murmur at the left lower sternal border. Small defects produce loud murmurs because of the large pressure gradient; very large defects may be quieter as the pressures equalize.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 23: Heart Valves and Heart Sounds; Valvular and Congenital Heart Defects

  11. A 60-year-old man with heart failure has a low-pitched extra sound in early diastole, best heard at the apex with the bell of the stethoscope. This is:

    • A. An S3 (ventricular gallop)
    • B. An opening snap
    • C. An ejection click
    • D. A pericardial knock
    • E. An S4 (atrial gallop)
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. An S3 (ventricular gallop)

    S3 is an early diastolic sound caused by rapid ventricular filling and sudden tensing of the ventricular wall. In adults it is usually pathological, indicating volume overload or systolic heart failure (the 'ventricular gallop'). It is best heard with the bell at the apex with the patient in the left lateral position.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 23: Heart Valves and Heart Sounds; Valvular and Congenital Heart Defects

  12. An S4 heart sound (atrial gallop) is most commonly associated with:

    • A. Thyrotoxicosis
    • B. Decreased ventricular compliance, e.g., left ventricular hypertrophy in hypertension
    • C. Severe anemia
    • D. Acute mitral regurgitation
    • E. Volume overload of the ventricle
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Decreased ventricular compliance, e.g., left ventricular hypertrophy in hypertension

    S4 occurs in late diastole (presystole) when atrial contraction forces blood into a stiff, noncompliant ventricle, as in left ventricular hypertrophy from chronic hypertension or aortic stenosis. Unlike S3, an S4 is almost always pathological and reflects impaired diastolic filling rather than volume overload.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 23: Heart Valves and Heart Sounds; Valvular and Congenital Heart Defects

  13. Which of the following is NOT a component of tetralogy of Fallot?

    • A. Pulmonary stenosis
    • B. Ventricular septal defect
    • C. Overriding aorta
    • D. Atrial septal defect
    • E. Right ventricular hypertrophy
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Atrial septal defect

    Tetralogy of Fallot comprises four features: ventricular septal defect, pulmonary (right ventricular outflow) stenosis, an overriding aorta, and right ventricular hypertrophy. The degree of cyanosis depends on the severity of the pulmonary stenosis. An atrial septal defect is not part of the tetralogy.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 23: Heart Valves and Heart Sounds; Valvular and Congenital Heart Defects

  14. A systolic murmur that becomes LOUDER with the Valsalva maneuver (which decreases preload) is characteristic of:

    • A. Aortic stenosis
    • B. Mitral regurgitation
    • C. Hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy
    • D. Ventricular septal defect
    • E. Tricuspid regurgitation
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy

    In hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy, decreased preload reduces ventricular volume, bringing the hypertrophied septum and anterior mitral leaflet closer together and worsening the dynamic outflow obstruction — so the murmur intensifies. In aortic stenosis the murmur instead softens with decreased preload, and this opposite response helps distinguish the two conditions.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 23: Heart Valves and Heart Sounds; Valvular and Congenital Heart Defects

  15. A holosystolic murmur at the left lower sternal border that INCREASES with inspiration is characteristic of:

    • A. Tricuspid regurgitation
    • B. Mitral regurgitation
    • C. Ventricular septal defect
    • D. Aortic regurgitation
    • E. Patent ductus arteriosus
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Tricuspid regurgitation

    This is Carvallo's sign. Right-sided murmurs (tricuspid regurgitation, pulmonary stenosis) intensify with inspiration because the increased venous return augments right-sided blood flow. Left-sided murmurs typically soften or remain unchanged with inspiration. Tricuspid regurgitation is most often secondary to pulmonary hypertension with right ventricular dilation.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 23: Heart Valves and Heart Sounds; Valvular and Congenital Heart Defects

  16. Pulsus parvus et tardus (a small, slow-rising arterial pulse) is characteristic of:

    • A. Severe anemia
    • B. Patent ductus arteriosus
    • C. Severe aortic stenosis
    • D. Aortic regurgitation
    • E. Hyperthyroidism
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Severe aortic stenosis

    In severe aortic stenosis the narrowed valve restricts ventricular ejection, producing an arterial pulse that rises slowly and has a low amplitude (parvus et tardus means 'small and late'). By contrast, aortic regurgitation produces a bounding, rapidly collapsing water-hammer pulse because of its wide pulse pressure.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 23: Heart Valves and Heart Sounds; Valvular and Congenital Heart Defects

  17. A mid-systolic click followed by a late systolic murmur at the apex, in a young woman with atypical chest pain, most likely represents:

    • A. Mitral stenosis
    • B. Atrial myxoma
    • C. Aortic stenosis
    • D. Acute pericarditis
    • E. Mitral valve prolapse
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: E. Mitral valve prolapse

    Mitral valve prolapse produces a mid-systolic click as the redundant leaflet billows into the left atrium and suddenly tenses, followed by a late systolic murmur when regurgitation occurs. It is common in young women, usually benign, and is associated with atypical chest pain and palpitations.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 23: Heart Valves and Heart Sounds; Valvular and Congenital Heart Defects

  18. The most common valvular lesion following rheumatic fever is:

    • A. Pulmonary regurgitation
    • B. Aortic regurgitation
    • C. Mitral stenosis
    • D. Aortic stenosis
    • E. Tricuspid stenosis
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Mitral stenosis

    Rheumatic fever most commonly damages the mitral valve, and mitral stenosis is its classic late valvular consequence, often presenting decades after the acute illness. The mitral valve is affected far more frequently than the aortic valve, while tricuspid and pulmonary involvement is rare.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 23: Heart Valves and Heart Sounds; Valvular and Congenital Heart Defects

  19. A 16-year-old boy has higher blood pressure in the arms than in the legs, with weak and delayed femoral pulses. The most likely diagnosis is:

    • A. Coarctation of the aorta
    • B. Takayasu arteritis
    • C. Valvular aortic stenosis
    • D. Patent ductus arteriosus
    • E. Renal artery stenosis
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Coarctation of the aorta

    Coarctation of the aorta — a narrowing usually just distal to the left subclavian artery — causes hypertension in the upper body but low pressure with weak, delayed pulses in the lower limbs. It is an important cause of secondary hypertension in young patients and may produce a systolic murmur heard over the back.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 23: Heart Valves and Heart Sounds; Valvular and Congenital Heart Defects

  20. A 30-year-old man with fever for three weeks develops a new murmur of aortic regurgitation. The most likely diagnosis is:

    • A. Myxomatous valve degeneration
    • B. Marfan syndrome
    • C. Infective endocarditis
    • D. Syphilitic aortitis
    • E. Acute rheumatic carditis
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Infective endocarditis

    Infective endocarditis classically presents with persistent fever and a new or changing regurgitant murmur as vegetations destroy the valve leaflets. Supporting clues include Osler nodes, Janeway lesions, and splinter hemorrhages. The diagnosis is confirmed with blood cultures and echocardiography showing vegetations.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 23: Heart Valves and Heart Sounds; Valvular and Congenital Heart Defects

  21. The second heart sound (S2) is produced by:

    • A. Closure of the mitral and tricuspid valves at the start of systole
    • B. Turbulent filling of the atria during atrial systole
    • C. Opening of the mitral valve in early diastole
    • D. Closure of the aortic and pulmonary valves at the end of systole
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Closure of the aortic and pulmonary valves at the end of systole

    S2 marks closure of the semilunar (aortic and pulmonary) valves when ventricular pressure falls below arterial pressure at the end of systole. S1, not S2, is caused by AV valve closure at the start of systole, and valve opening is normally silent.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Heart Valves and Heart Sounds; Valvular and Congenital Heart Defects
