Home › Physiology MCQs › Muscle Blood Flow and Cardiac Output During Exercise; the Coronary Circulation and Ischemic Heart Disease

During exercise, the working muscles need much more blood, so cardiac output rises and vessels in the active muscles widen. The heart itself receives its blood through the coronary arteries, and its own demand for oxygen also rises sharply. If a coronary artery is narrowed, the heart muscle may not receive enough blood during exertion, causing ischaemia.

  1. During maximal exercise, blood flow to active skeletal muscle can increase to about:

    • A. 20 times resting flow
    • B. 2 times resting flow
    • C. Unchanged from rest
    • D. Half of resting flow
    • E. 100 times resting flow
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. 20 times resting flow

    Metabolic vasodilation in exercising muscle is the most dramatic flow increase in the body — from about 4 mL/min/100g at rest to 80-100 mL/min/100g, roughly 20-fold. Combined with increased cardiac output and sympathetic vasoconstriction elsewhere, active muscle can receive over 80% of cardiac output during maximal exercise.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 21: Muscle Blood Flow and Cardiac Output During Exercise; the Coronary Circulation and Ischemic Heart Disease

  2. During heavy exercise, blood flow to the splanchnic organs and kidneys:

    • A. Increases markedly
    • B. Remains unchanged
    • C. Decreases due to sympathetic vasoconstriction, redirecting flow to muscle
    • D. Stops completely
    • E. Increases due to local metabolites
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Decreases due to sympathetic vasoconstriction, redirecting flow to muscle

    Sympathetic drive constricts splanchnic and renal arterioles during exercise, cutting their flow by half or more to prioritize muscle, heart, and brain. This redistribution is why vigorous exercise right after a heavy meal can cause cramps (gut ischemia) and why renal blood flow falls during marathon running.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 21: Muscle Blood Flow and Cardiac Output During Exercise; the Coronary Circulation and Ischemic Heart Disease

  3. The maximal cardiac output of an elite endurance athlete may reach:

    • A. 10 L/min
    • B. 30-35 L/min
    • C. 5 L/min
    • D. 100 L/min
    • E. 15 L/min
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. 30-35 L/min

    Training enlarges stroke volume (bigger, more compliant ventricles) and raises maximal heart rate, pushing cardiac output to 30-35 L/min — six to seven times resting. This extraordinary pump capacity, not muscle biochemistry alone, is the primary determinant of VO2 max and endurance performance.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 21: Muscle Blood Flow and Cardiac Output During Exercise; the Coronary Circulation and Ischemic Heart Disease

  4. The left anterior descending (LAD) coronary artery is clinically the most important coronary branch because:

    • A. It supplies the SA node
    • B. It is the smallest branch
    • C. It supplies the anterior left ventricle and anterior septum; its occlusion causes large anterior MI
    • D. It never develops atherosclerosis
    • E. It supplies the right atrium
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. It supplies the anterior left ventricle and anterior septum; its occlusion causes large anterior MI

    The LAD (anterior interventricular branch) perfuses the anterior LV wall and anterior two-thirds of the septum — the heart's main pumping chamber. Proximal LAD occlusion produces a large anterior infarction with high mortality, earning it the widow maker nickname. The RCA typically supplies the SA/AV nodes and inferior wall.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 21: Muscle Blood Flow and Cardiac Output During Exercise; the Coronary Circulation and Ischemic Heart Disease

  5. Blood flow in the left coronary artery is markedly phasic, falling to near zero during systole. This occurs because:

    • A. Systolic contraction compresses the intramyocardial vessels, especially in the subendocardium
    • B. The aortic valve closes
    • C. Vagal tone increases in systole
    • D. Coronary arteries have no smooth muscle
    • E. Blood flows backward in systole
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Systolic contraction compresses the intramyocardial vessels, especially in the subendocardium

    During systole, contracting myocardium squeezes its own vessels shut — most severely in the subendocardium where tissue pressure exceeds coronary perfusion pressure. Flow therefore occurs mainly in diastole when the muscle relaxes. Tachycardia shortens diastole disproportionately, which is why fast rates threaten coronary perfusion.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 21: Muscle Blood Flow and Cardiac Output During Exercise; the Coronary Circulation and Ischemic Heart Disease

  6. Compared with the left coronary artery, right coronary blood flow is less phasic (flows well in systole too) because:

    • A. The right coronary has no branches
    • B. Right ventricular systolic tissue pressure is much lower, so vessels are less compressed
    • C. The right coronary is larger
    • D. Vagal tone dilates it
    • E. It fills from the pulmonary artery
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Right ventricular systolic tissue pressure is much lower, so vessels are less compressed

    The right ventricle generates only about 25 mmHg systolic pressure versus about 120 mmHg on the left, so its contracting muscle barely compresses coronary vessels. Right coronary flow continues through systole, making the right ventricle relatively resistant to ischemia — right ventricular infarction usually requires proximal RCA occlusion.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 21: Muscle Blood Flow and Cardiac Output During Exercise; the Coronary Circulation and Ischemic Heart Disease

  7. The primary metabolic mediator matching coronary blood flow to myocardial oxygen demand is:

    • A. Norepinephrine
    • B. Endothelin
    • C. Adenosine
    • D. Histamine
    • E. Serotonin
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Adenosine

    When myocardial oxygen consumption rises, ATP breakdown releases adenosine into the interstitium, potently dilating coronary arterioles. Because the heart already extracts about 70% of delivered oxygen at rest, increased demand can only be met by increased flow — and adenosine is the signal that couples the two, supported by NO, K+, and H+.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 21: Muscle Blood Flow and Cardiac Output During Exercise; the Coronary Circulation and Ischemic Heart Disease

  8. At rest the heart extracts about 70-75% of the oxygen delivered by coronary blood (vs about 25% in most tissues). The physiological consequence is:

    • A. The heart needs no blood flow increase in exercise
    • B. Coronary flow can decrease in exercise
    • C. Increased oxygen demand must be met almost entirely by increasing coronary blood flow
    • D. The heart is immune to ischemia
    • E. Myoglobin is unnecessary
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Increased oxygen demand must be met almost entirely by increasing coronary blood flow

    Most tissues meet higher demand by extracting more oxygen, but the heart's extraction is already near-maximal at rest. Therefore any increase in cardiac work demands a proportional increase in coronary flow — the reason coronary artery disease first manifests during exertion, when flow cannot rise to match demand.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 21: Muscle Blood Flow and Cardiac Output During Exercise; the Coronary Circulation and Ischemic Heart Disease

  9. A 60-year-old man develops retrosternal pressure on climbing stairs, relieved within minutes by rest. ECG is normal at rest. This is most consistent with:

    • A. Stable angina from fixed coronary stenosis with exertional supply-demand mismatch
    • B. Acute myocardial infarction
    • C. Unstable angina
    • D. Prinzmetal angina
    • E. Pericarditis
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Stable angina from fixed coronary stenosis with exertional supply-demand mismatch

    Stable angina is exertional chest discomfort from a fixed atherosclerotic stenosis: demand outstrips the limited supply during effort, and rest restores balance. Pain is predictable, brief, and relieved by rest or nitrates. New rest pain, prolonged episodes, or changing pattern signal unstable angina — a pre-infarction emergency.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 21: Muscle Blood Flow and Cardiac Output During Exercise; the Coronary Circulation and Ischemic Heart Disease

  10. Which feature distinguishes unstable angina from stable angina?

    • A. Pain only on exertion
    • B. Pain at rest or with increasing frequency/severity, suggesting plaque disruption with thrombus
    • C. Relief by rest always
    • D. Normal coronary arteries
    • E. No risk of infarction
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Pain at rest or with increasing frequency/severity, suggesting plaque disruption with thrombus

    Unstable angina reflects plaque rupture or erosion with partial thrombosis: pain occurs at rest, lasts longer, and crescendos. It is part of the acute coronary syndrome spectrum with NSTEMI, distinguished only by the absence of troponin elevation. It demands urgent antiplatelet, anticoagulant, and often invasive management.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 21: Muscle Blood Flow and Cardiac Output During Exercise; the Coronary Circulation and Ischemic Heart Disease

  11. A 45-year-old woman has episodes of severe chest pain at rest, typically at night, with transient ST-segment elevation that resolves with sublingual nitroglycerin. Coronary angiography shows no fixed stenosis. Diagnosis:

    • A. Stable angina
    • B. Unstable angina from plaque rupture
    • C. Prinzmetal (vasospastic) angina
    • D. Aortic dissection
    • E. Esophageal spasm
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Prinzmetal (vasospastic) angina

    Prinzmetal angina is caused by focal coronary vasospasm, not fixed plaque: rest (often nocturnal) pain with transient ST elevation that normalizes as spasm releases. Calcium channel blockers prevent spasm; nonselective beta-blockers can worsen it by leaving alpha-mediated constriction unopposed.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 21: Muscle Blood Flow and Cardiac Output During Exercise; the Coronary Circulation and Ischemic Heart Disease

  12. The most sensitive and specific blood marker for myocardial necrosis is:

    • A. CK-MB
    • B. Myoglobin
    • C. LDH
    • D. Cardiac troponin I or T
    • E. AST
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Cardiac troponin I or T

    Cardiac troponins I and T are regulatory proteins unique to myocardium; they rise within 3-4 hours of infarction, peak at 24 hours, and remain elevated for 7-10 days. High-sensitivity assays detect even minor injury, making troponin the cornerstone for diagnosing MI and risk-stratifying acute coronary syndromes.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 21: Muscle Blood Flow and Cardiac Output During Exercise; the Coronary Circulation and Ischemic Heart Disease

  13. Sublingual nitroglycerin relieves angina primarily by:

    • A. Venodilation reducing preload and myocardial oxygen demand, plus coronary dilation
    • B. Increasing heart rate
    • C. Constricting arterioles
    • D. Increasing contractility
    • E. Thinning the blood
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Venodilation reducing preload and myocardial oxygen demand, plus coronary dilation

    Nitrates release NO, predominantly dilating veins: reduced venous return lowers ventricular wall tension (Laplace's law), cutting oxygen demand. They also dilate coronary arteries and collaterals, improving supply. The combination — less demand, more supply — aborts anginal pain within minutes.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 21: Muscle Blood Flow and Cardiac Output During Exercise; the Coronary Circulation and Ischemic Heart Disease

  14. Beta-blockers help angina and post-MI patients mainly by:

    • A. Dilating coronary arteries
    • B. Reducing heart rate, contractility, and blood pressure, thereby lowering myocardial oxygen demand
    • C. Dissolving thrombi
    • D. Increasing HDL
    • E. Blocking calcium channels
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Reducing heart rate, contractility, and blood pressure, thereby lowering myocardial oxygen demand

    Myocardial oxygen consumption is determined by heart rate, contractility, and wall tension. Beta-1 blockade reduces all three, rebalancing supply and demand. Post-MI, beta-blockers also suppress arrhythmias and remodeling, which is why they reduce mortality after infarction — benefits beyond mere symptom relief.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 21: Muscle Blood Flow and Cardiac Output During Exercise; the Coronary Circulation and Ischemic Heart Disease

  15. The earliest morphologic change in atherosclerosis is:

    • A. Calcified plaque
    • B. Thrombus formation
    • C. Endothelial injury with subintimal LDL accumulation and fatty streak formation
    • D. Medial hypertrophy
    • E. Aneurysm
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Endothelial injury with subintimal LDL accumulation and fatty streak formation

    Atherosclerosis begins with endothelial dysfunction/injury (from hypertension, smoking, oxidized LDL), allowing LDL into the intima where macrophages engulf it becoming foam cells — the fatty streak. Over decades, smooth muscle proliferation, fibrosis, and calcification build complex plaques that may rupture, triggering thrombosis and acute coronary syndromes.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 21: Muscle Blood Flow and Cardiac Output During Exercise; the Coronary Circulation and Ischemic Heart Disease

  16. During coronary hypoperfusion, the subendocardium suffers ischemia first because:

    • A. It has the highest oxygen extraction
    • B. It is supplied by veins
    • C. It is farthest from epicardial arteries AND subjected to the greatest systolic compressive forces
    • D. It has no capillaries
    • E. It contracts least
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. It is farthest from epicardial arteries AND subjected to the greatest systolic compressive forces

    The subendocardium is doubly disadvantaged: it lies at the distal end of the coronary tree and endures the highest intramyocardial tissue pressure during systole, which obliterates its vessels. Ischemia therefore progresses from endocardium to epicardium (the wavefront phenomenon) — the basis of subendocardial (non-transmural) infarction patterns.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 21: Muscle Blood Flow and Cardiac Output During Exercise; the Coronary Circulation and Ischemic Heart Disease

  17. Tachycardia is particularly dangerous in coronary artery disease because:

    • A. It shortens diastole, reducing coronary filling time while simultaneously increasing oxygen demand
    • B. It lowers blood pressure
    • C. It causes bradycardia
    • D. It dilates coronaries excessively
    • E. It has no effect on the heart
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. It shortens diastole, reducing coronary filling time while simultaneously increasing oxygen demand

    Fast rates hit the ischemic heart twice: diastole (when coronaries fill) shortens disproportionately, cutting supply, while increased rate and contractility raise demand. This supply-demand scissors is why controlling heart rate with beta-blockers is central to managing angina and why tachyarrhythmias can precipitate infarction.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 21: Muscle Blood Flow and Cardiac Output During Exercise; the Coronary Circulation and Ischemic Heart Disease

  18. Well-developed coronary collateral vessels are most likely to be found in:

    • A. Acute plaque rupture
    • B. Chronic stable coronary disease with gradual stenosis, allowing time for collateral growth
    • C. Prinzmetal angina
    • D. Normal hearts
    • E. Acute pericarditis
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Chronic stable coronary disease with gradual stenosis, allowing time for collateral growth

    Collaterals enlarge in response to chronic pressure gradients across developing stenoses over months to years (arteriogenesis). Sudden occlusion (as in acute MI from plaque rupture) strikes before collaterals exist, causing large infarcts — while gradual disease may be surprisingly well tolerated thanks to a rich collateral network.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 21: Muscle Blood Flow and Cardiac Output During Exercise; the Coronary Circulation and Ischemic Heart Disease

  19. The three major determinants of myocardial oxygen consumption are:

    • A. Blood glucose, cholesterol, and age
    • B. Heart rate, contractility, and ventricular wall tension
    • C. Coronary diameter, blood viscosity, and hematocrit
    • D. Preload only
    • E. Body temperature and pH
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Heart rate, contractility, and ventricular wall tension

    Myocardial work — and thus oxygen demand — is set by how fast the heart beats, how forcefully it contracts, and the wall tension it must generate (Laplace: pressure x radius / wall thickness). Every anti-anginal strategy (beta-blockers, nitrates, calcium blockers) works by reducing one or more of these three determinants.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 21: Muscle Blood Flow and Cardiac Output During Exercise; the Coronary Circulation and Ischemic Heart Disease

  20. A 55-year-old diabetic man develops crushing retrosternal pain radiating to the left arm with sweating and nausea, lasting 45 minutes and unrelieved by rest. ECG shows ST elevation in anterior leads. The most likely diagnosis and immediate concern are:

    • A. Stable angina; reassurance
    • B. Pericarditis; NSAIDs
    • C. Aortic dissection; surgery
    • D. Acute ST-elevation myocardial infarction; urgent reperfusion
    • E. Gastritis; antacids
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Acute ST-elevation myocardial infarction; urgent reperfusion

    Prolonged (over 20 min) crushing chest pain with diaphoresis unrelieved by rest plus ST elevation defines STEMI — complete coronary occlusion with transmural ischemia. Time is muscle: urgent reperfusion by primary PCI (or thrombolysis) within 120 minutes salvages myocardium. Diabetics may have atypical or silent symptoms, so vigilance is essential.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 21: Muscle Blood Flow and Cardiac Output During Exercise; the Coronary Circulation and Ischemic Heart Disease
