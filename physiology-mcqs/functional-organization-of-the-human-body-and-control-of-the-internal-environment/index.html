Functional Organization of the Human Body and Control of the "Internal Environment" MCQs for FMU 1st Year MBBS Physiology

Home › Physiology MCQs › Functional Organization of the Human Body and Control of the "Internal Environment"

The human body is organised into cells, tissues, organs, and systems that work together to keep the internal environment stable. Extracellular fluid bathes the cells, and its composition, temperature, and acidity are held within narrow limits. This stability, called homeostasis, is maintained by control systems that detect changes and correct them.

  1. In a healthy 70-kg adult man, total body water is approximately:

    • A. 28 liters
    • B. 35 liters
    • C. 42 liters
    • D. 49 liters
    • E. 56 liters
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. 42 liters

    Total body water is about 60% of body weight in the adult male, so 0.60 x 70 kg = 42 liters. In females it is closer to 50% because of a higher proportion of adipose tissue, which contains little water. Of the 42 L, about 28 L is intracellular fluid and 14 L is extracellular fluid.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 1: Functional Organization of the Human Body and Control of the "Internal Environment"

  2. About what fraction of total body water is contained inside the cells?

    • A. One-quarter
    • B. One-third
    • C. Two-thirds
    • D. Three-quarters
    • E. Nine-tenths
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Two-thirds

    Intracellular fluid makes up about 40% of body weight (28 of 42 liters), which is two-thirds of total body water. The remaining one-third (14 L) is extracellular fluid. This large intracellular reservoir is the fluid in which all cellular chemical reactions occur.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 1: Functional Organization of the Human Body and Control of the "Internal Environment"

  3. The largest subdivision of the extracellular fluid is the:

    • A. Plasma (about 3.5 L)
    • B. Interstitial fluid (about 10.5 L)
    • C. Transcellular fluid
    • D. Cerebrospinal fluid
    • E. Lymphatic fluid
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Interstitial fluid (about 10.5 L)

    Of the 14 L of extracellular fluid, roughly 10.5 L bathes the cells directly as interstitial fluid and only about 3.5 L is plasma inside blood vessels. Transcellular fluids (CSF, intraocular, joint, GI secretions) are a small special subdivision. The interstitial fluid is therefore the true 'internal environment' of Claude Bernard.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 1: Functional Organization of the Human Body and Control of the "Internal Environment"

  4. The most abundant cation in the extracellular fluid is:

    • A. Potassium
    • B. Sodium
    • C. Calcium
    • D. Magnesium
    • E. Hydrogen
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Sodium

    Sodium is the dominant extracellular cation at about 142 mEq/L, and it largely determines extracellular osmolarity and fluid volume. Potassium dominates inside the cell (about 140 mEq/L intracellular). This steep Na+/K+ asymmetry is maintained by the Na+/K+ ATPase pump.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 1: Functional Organization of the Human Body and Control of the "Internal Environment"

  5. The most abundant cation in the intracellular fluid is:

    • A. Sodium
    • B. Potassium
    • C. Calcium
    • D. Chloride
    • E. Bicarbonate
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Potassium

    Potassium is the principal intracellular cation (about 140 mEq/L), while intracellular Na+ is only about 14 mEq/L. Intracellular fluid also contains more magnesium, phosphate, and protein anions than extracellular fluid. The Na+/K+ ATPase continuously pumps 3 Na+ out and 2 K+ in to preserve this distribution.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 1: Functional Organization of the Human Body and Control of the "Internal Environment"

  6. A rise in arterial pressure stretches the carotid sinus baroreceptors, which reflexly slows the heart and dilates resistance vessels, bringing pressure back down. This is an example of:

    • A. Positive feedback
    • B. Feedforward control
    • C. Negative feedback
    • D. Accommodation
    • E. Denervation supersensitivity
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Negative feedback

    Negative feedback is the master control principle of homeostasis: the response opposes (reverses) the initiating change. Here, increased pressure triggers responses that lower pressure. Most homeostatic systems — blood pressure, blood glucose, osmolarity, body temperature — operate by negative feedback.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 1: Functional Organization of the Human Body and Control of the "Internal Environment"

  7. Which of the following operates by POSITIVE feedback?

    • A. Baroreceptor control of blood pressure
    • B. Blood clotting
    • C. ADH release during dehydration
    • D. Insulin release after a meal
    • E. Pupillary constriction to light
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Blood clotting

    In blood clotting, activated clotting factors activate still more clotting factors in a self-amplifying cascade — the response reinforces the stimulus. Other positive feedback examples are uterine contractions of labor (oxytocin) and the Na+ influx phase of the action potential. Positive feedback is rare in physiology because it can run away unless a climactic endpoint stops it.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 1: Functional Organization of the Human Body and Control of the "Internal Environment"

  8. A woman in labor has progressively stronger and more frequent uterine contractions. Each contraction stretches the cervix, stimulating more oxytocin release, which causes still stronger contractions. This illustrates:

    • A. Negative feedback maintaining a set point
    • B. Positive feedback driving toward a climax
    • C. Feedforward inhibition
    • D. Accommodation of stretch receptors
    • E. Failure of homeostasis
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Positive feedback driving toward a climax

    Labor is the classic positive feedback loop: the response (contraction) intensifies the stimulus (cervical stretch and oxytocin release). Unlike negative feedback, positive feedback amplifies the change and drives the system toward a climactic event — delivery of the baby — which then terminates the loop.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 1: Functional Organization of the Human Body and Control of the "Internal Environment"

  9. After acute hemorrhage, sympathetic discharge constricts arterioles, increases heart rate and contractility, and helps restore arterial pressure toward normal. The control architecture here is:

    • A. Positive feedback, because pressure keeps falling
    • B. Negative feedback, because responses oppose the pressure fall
    • C. Feedforward, because it anticipates blood loss
    • D. Open loop, because no receptor is involved
    • E. Trophic feedback via hormones only
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Negative feedback, because responses oppose the pressure fall

    Hemorrhage lowers arterial pressure; baroreceptor unloading triggers sympathetic responses that raise pressure back up — the correction opposes the disturbance, which is negative feedback. If this were positive feedback, pressure would spiral downward uncontrollably. Sympathetic activation is the rapid neural arm of cardiovascular homeostasis.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 1: Functional Organization of the Human Body and Control of the "Internal Environment"

  10. Dehydration raises plasma osmolarity, stimulating ADH (vasopressin) secretion. ADH increases water reabsorption in the collecting ducts, diluting the plasma; as osmolarity normalizes, ADH secretion falls. This best demonstrates:

    • A. Positive feedback with a climax
    • B. Negative feedback around a set point
    • C. Feedforward control
    • D. Trophic hormone cascade
    • E. Failure of the control loop
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Negative feedback around a set point

    Osmoreceptors defend plasma osmolarity near 285 mOsm/L (the set point). A rise in osmolarity evokes water conservation that lowers osmolarity, and the signal then switches off — the hallmark of negative feedback. Diabetes insipidus (ADH deficiency or resistance) shows what happens when this loop fails: massive dilute urine and hyperosmolarity.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 1: Functional Organization of the Human Body and Control of the "Internal Environment"

  11. Claude Bernard's concept of the 'milieu interieur' (internal environment) refers to the:

    • A. Fluid inside the cells
    • B. Extracellular fluid bathing the cells
    • C. Blood plasma only
    • D. Fluid in the GI lumen
    • E. Cerebrospinal fluid
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Extracellular fluid bathing the cells

    Bernard proposed that cells live in a stable internal sea — the extracellular (interstitial) fluid — whose composition the body keeps nearly constant despite a changing outside world. Walter Cannon later named this constancy 'homeostasis.' Plasma is part of it, but the interstitial fluid directly bathes the cells.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 1: Functional Organization of the Human Body and Control of the "Internal Environment"

  12. Which statement about body fluid osmolarity is correct in health?

    • A. ICF osmolarity is much higher than ECF osmolarity
    • B. ECF osmolarity is much higher than ICF osmolarity
    • C. ICF and ECF osmolarities are essentially equal
    • D. Plasma osmolarity is double interstitial osmolarity
    • E. Osmolarity cannot be compared across membranes
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. ICF and ECF osmolarities are essentially equal

    Cell membranes are freely permeable to water, so water shifts until intracellular and extracellular osmolarities equalize at about 285-295 mOsm/kg. A difference would draw water across the membrane and change cell volume. Sodium salts dominate ECF osmolarity while potassium salts dominate ICF osmolarity, but the totals match.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 1: Functional Organization of the Human Body and Control of the "Internal Environment"

  13. Failure of a negative feedback loop is best illustrated by:

    • A. Uterine contractions during labor
    • B. Type 1 diabetes mellitus from loss of insulin secretion
    • C. The blood clotting cascade
    • D. The upstroke of the neuronal action potential
    • E. The milk ejection reflex
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Type 1 diabetes mellitus from loss of insulin secretion

    In type 1 diabetes, autoimmune destruction of beta cells removes insulin — the effector arm of glucose negative feedback — so blood glucose rises unchecked. Labor, clotting, the AP upstroke, and milk ejection are all normal positive feedback processes, not failures. Disease often represents broken feedback control.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 1: Functional Organization of the Human Body and Control of the "Internal Environment"

  14. During the upstroke of the action potential, Na+ entry depolarizes the membrane, which opens more voltage-gated Na+ channels, admitting still more Na+. This self-reinforcing cycle is an example of:

    • A. Negative feedback stabilizing the membrane
    • B. Positive feedback (Hodgkin cycle)
    • C. Feedforward inhibition
    • D. Accommodation
    • E. Electrotonic spread
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Positive feedback (Hodgkin cycle)

    The Hodgkin cycle is positive feedback confined to a fraction of a millisecond: depolarization recruits more Na+ channels until the membrane potential approaches the Na+ equilibrium potential. It ends when Na+ channels inactivate. This is why the action potential, once triggered, proceeds explosively to completion.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 1: Functional Organization of the Human Body and Control of the "Internal Environment"

  15. In control-system terms, the 'set point' is best defined as the:

    • A. Maximum value a variable can reach
    • B. Reference value the system defends
    • C. Rate of change of the controlled variable
    • D. Delay between stimulus and response
    • E. Gain of the effector organ
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Reference value the system defends

    The set point is the target value (e.g., 37 C core temperature, 285 mOsm/L plasma osmolarity, ~100 mmHg mean arterial pressure). Receptors compare the actual value with the set point and generate an error signal that drives corrective responses. Fever raises the temperature set point; heat-loss responses then defend the new, higher value.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 1: Functional Organization of the Human Body and Control of the "Internal Environment"

  16. Which ion is normally maintained at a MUCH higher concentration in extracellular fluid than in intracellular fluid?

    • A. Potassium
    • B. Sodium
    • C. Magnesium
    • D. Phosphate
    • E. Hydrogen
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Sodium

    Extracellular Na+ (~142 mEq/L) is about ten times intracellular Na+ (~14 mEq/L), creating the gradient that powers secondary active transport and the action potential upstroke. Ca2+ and Cl- are also higher outside, while K+, Mg2+, phosphate, and protein anions concentrate inside. The Na+/K+ ATPase maintains these gradients continuously.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 1: Functional Organization of the Human Body and Control of the "Internal Environment"

  17. Salivation and a small early insulin release at the sight and smell of food, before any glucose reaches the blood, is an example of:

    • A. Negative feedback
    • B. Positive feedback
    • C. Feedforward control
    • D. Trophic stimulation
    • E. Conditioned inhibition
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Feedforward control

    Feedforward (anticipatory) control prepares the body for an expected disturbance before receptors detect any error — here the cephalic phase primes digestion. Similarly, heart rate rises as exercise begins, before metabolic demand actually changes. Feedforward is faster than feedback but less precise, so the two usually work together.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 1: Functional Organization of the Human Body and Control of the "Internal Environment"

  18. Which of the following does NOT operate principally by negative feedback?

    • A. TSH regulation by thyroid hormones
    • B. ACTH regulation by cortisol
    • C. Uterine contractions during labor
    • D. Blood glucose regulation by insulin
    • E. Arterial pressure regulation by baroreceptors
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Uterine contractions during labor

    Labor contractions amplify via oxytocin positive feedback, whereas thyroid, adrenal, glucose, and pressure systems all use negative feedback (rising hormone levels suppress their own releasing signals). Recognizing which loop is positive versus negative is a favorite examiner distinction.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 1: Functional Organization of the Human Body and Control of the "Internal Environment"

  19. The two general systems by which the body regulates the internal environment are the:

    • A. Somatic nervous system and immune system
    • B. Autonomic nervous system and endocrine system
    • C. Enteric nervous system and lymphatic system
    • D. Sympathetic and parasympathetic divisions only
    • E. Reticuloendothelial system and kidneys
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Autonomic nervous system and endocrine system

    Rapid, short-lived corrections (seconds) are made by the autonomic nervous system — e.g., baroreflex control of pressure — while slower, sustained adjustments (minutes to days) are hormonal, e.g., ADH, aldosterone, and thyroid hormone. Together they keep the composition of the extracellular fluid nearly constant.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 1: Functional Organization of the Human Body and Control of the "Internal Environment"

  20. After a carbohydrate-rich meal, rising blood glucose stimulates pancreatic insulin release; as glucose falls back toward fasting levels, insulin secretion declines. This pattern demonstrates:

    • A. Positive feedback driving glucose ever lower
    • B. Negative feedback with glucose as the controlled variable
    • C. Feedforward control by gut hormones alone
    • D. Loss of the set point mechanism
    • E. An open-loop hormonal response
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Negative feedback with glucose as the controlled variable

    Glucose is the controlled variable and insulin the effector: high glucose raises insulin, which lowers glucose, which then turns insulin off. Glucagon provides the counter-regulatory arm when glucose falls too low. The paired hormones keep fasting glucose within a narrow range — a dual negative feedback system.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 1: Functional Organization of the Human Body and Control of the "Internal Environment"
