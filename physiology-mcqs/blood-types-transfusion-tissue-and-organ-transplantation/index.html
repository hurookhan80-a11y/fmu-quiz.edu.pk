Home › Physiology MCQs › Blood Types; Transfusion; Tissue and Organ Transplantation

Human blood is grouped by the antigens present on red blood cells, notably the ABO and Rh systems. Transfusion must match donor and recipient blood, because mismatched blood can trigger a dangerous immune reaction. The same immune principles govern tissue and organ transplantation, where matching reduces the risk that the recipient rejects the graft.

  1. A person with blood group A has:

    • A. A antigens on red cells and anti-B antibodies in plasma
    • B. Neither antigen with both anti-A and anti-B antibodies
    • C. Both A and B antigens with no antibodies
    • D. B antigens on red cells and anti-A antibodies in plasma
    • E. Only Rh antigen
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. A antigens on red cells and anti-B antibodies in plasma

    In the ABO system, red cell antigens and plasma antibodies are reciprocal: group A carries A antigens with anti-B antibodies, group B carries B antigens with anti-A, group AB carries both antigens with neither antibody, and group O carries neither antigen with both antibodies. These naturally occurring IgM antibodies cause immediate agglutination on mismatched transfusion.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 36: Blood Types; Transfusion; Tissue and Organ Transplantation

  2. In transfusion of packed red cells, the universal donor and universal recipient are respectively:

    • A. Rh-negative and Rh-positive
    • B. A and B
    • C. AB and O
    • D. O and AB
    • E. B and A
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. O and AB

    Group O red cells bear no A or B antigens, so they are not attacked by any recipient's ABO antibodies (universal donor for red cells). Group AB recipients have neither anti-A nor anti-B, so they can receive any ABO red cells (universal recipient). Note the reverse applies to plasma: AB plasma (no antibodies) is the universal plasma donor.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 36: Blood Types; Transfusion; Tissue and Organ Transplantation

  3. The major crossmatch in blood transfusion tests:

    • A. Platelet compatibility
    • B. White cell antibodies
    • C. Recipient red cells against donor serum
    • D. Donor red cells against recipient serum for agglutination
    • E. Rh genotype
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Donor red cells against recipient serum for agglutination

    The major crossmatch mixes donor red cells with recipient serum — the critical test, because it detects whether the recipient's antibodies will attack the transfused cells. Agglutination means incompatibility. The minor crossmatch (recipient cells vs donor serum) is less important since donor antibodies are diluted in the recipient's circulation.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 36: Blood Types; Transfusion; Tissue and Organ Transplantation

  4. A 40-year-old man develops fever, flank pain, and dark urine minutes after starting a blood transfusion. The transfusion is stopped immediately. The most likely cause and mechanism are:

    • A. Allergic reaction to donor plasma proteins
    • B. Acute hemolytic reaction from ABO-incompatible red cells destroyed by recipient antibodies
    • C. Febrile reaction to white cell cytokines
    • D. Bacterial contamination causing rigors only
    • E. Volume overload
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Acute hemolytic reaction from ABO-incompatible red cells destroyed by recipient antibodies

    ABO-incompatible transfusion lets the recipient's preformed IgM antibodies bind donor red cells, activating complement and causing explosive intravascular hemolysis — with fever, pain, hemoglobinuria, hypotension, and possible renal failure and DIC. It is the most dangerous transfusion reaction and is prevented by meticulous ABO verification and crossmatching.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 36: Blood Types; Transfusion; Tissue and Organ Transplantation

  5. Hemolytic disease of the newborn (erythroblastosis fetalis) occurs when:

    • A. An Rh-negative mother carrying an Rh-positive fetus becomes sensitized and her IgG anti-D crosses the placenta in a later pregnancy
    • B. The mother is Rh-positive and the fetus Rh-negative
    • C. The fetus is group O
    • D. Both parents are Rh-negative
    • E. The mother is group AB
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. An Rh-negative mother carrying an Rh-positive fetus becomes sensitized and her IgG anti-D crosses the placenta in a later pregnancy

    Fetal Rh-positive red cells leaking into an Rh-negative mother's circulation sensitize her to produce IgG anti-D. In a subsequent Rh-positive pregnancy, these IgG antibodies cross the placenta and hemolyze fetal red cells, causing severe anemia, jaundice, and hydrops. The first pregnancy is usually spared because sensitization occurs mainly at delivery.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 36: Blood Types; Transfusion; Tissue and Organ Transplantation

  6. A 28-year-old Rh-negative woman has just delivered a healthy Rh-positive baby. To prevent hemolytic disease in her NEXT pregnancy, she should receive:

    • A. Anti-D (RhoGAM) immunoglobulin within 72 hours of delivery
    • B. Platelet transfusion
    • C. Intravenous iron
    • D. Whole blood transfusion
    • E. No treatment; the risk has passed
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Anti-D (RhoGAM) immunoglobulin within 72 hours of delivery

    Anti-D immunoglobulin given within 72 hours of delivery (and routinely at 28 weeks' gestation) destroys fetal Rh-positive cells in the maternal circulation before she can mount her own immune response — preventing sensitization. This passive immunization has made severe erythroblastosis fetalis rare.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 36: Blood Types; Transfusion; Tissue and Organ Transplantation

  7. Which parental combination carries NO risk of Rh hemolytic disease of the newborn?

    • A. Rh-negative mother, Rh-positive father
    • B. Any Rh-negative mother
    • C. Rh-negative mother, Rh-positive father with a previous affected child
    • D. Rh-negative mother, unknown father
    • E. Rh-positive mother, Rh-negative father
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: E. Rh-positive mother, Rh-negative father

    Risk requires an Rh-negative mother and an Rh-positive fetus. If the mother herself is Rh-positive, she cannot form anti-D antibodies, so no combination of paternal Rh type endangers the baby. An Rh-negative mother with an Rh-positive partner needs evaluation of the fetus's Rh status and prophylactic anti-D.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 36: Blood Types; Transfusion; Tissue and Organ Transplantation

  8. A patient develops fever and chills during transfusion but no hemolysis, and the reaction resolves after stopping the unit and giving paracetamol. This is most consistent with:

    • A. Acute hemolytic reaction
    • B. Febrile nonhemolytic transfusion reaction from recipient antibodies to donor white cells or cytokines
    • C. TRALI
    • D. Anaphylaxis
    • E. Bacterial sepsis
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Febrile nonhemolytic transfusion reaction from recipient antibodies to donor white cells or cytokines

    Febrile nonhemolytic reactions — fever and chills without red cell destruction — are caused by recipient antibodies against donor leukocytes or by cytokines accumulated in stored blood. They are the most common transfusion reaction and are managed by stopping the transfusion and giving antipyretics; leukoreduced blood reduces their frequency.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 36: Blood Types; Transfusion; Tissue and Organ Transplantation

  9. Transfusion-related acute lung injury (TRALI) is characterized by:

    • A. Delayed jaundice
    • B. Chronic iron overload
    • C. Hemolysis of red cells
    • D. Isolated thrombocytopenia
    • E. Acute noncardiogenic pulmonary edema during or shortly after transfusion, from donor antibodies activating recipient neutrophils in the lungs
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: E. Acute noncardiogenic pulmonary edema during or shortly after transfusion, from donor antibodies activating recipient neutrophils in the lungs

    TRALI presents as sudden respiratory distress with bilateral pulmonary infiltrates during or within hours of transfusion, without circulatory overload. Donor anti-leukocyte antibodies activate the recipient's neutrophils in pulmonary capillaries, causing capillary leak. It is a leading cause of transfusion-related death and is managed with oxygen and ventilatory support.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 36: Blood Types; Transfusion; Tissue and Organ Transplantation

  10. Hyperacute rejection of a transplanted kidney, occurring within minutes, is caused by:

    • A. Preformed recipient antibodies against donor ABO or HLA antigens causing immediate vascular thrombosis
    • B. Immunosuppressant toxicity
    • C. Surgical technique
    • D. T-cell mediated cellular rejection
    • E. Chronic fibrosis
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Preformed recipient antibodies against donor ABO or HLA antigens causing immediate vascular thrombosis

    Hyperacute rejection strikes within minutes when the recipient already has antibodies (from prior transfusion, pregnancy, or transplant) against donor antigens. Antibody binding activates complement and coagulation in the graft vessels, infarcting the organ instantly. It is prevented by ABO compatibility and a negative crossmatch before transplantation.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 36: Blood Types; Transfusion; Tissue and Organ Transplantation

  11. Acute cellular rejection of an organ transplant, occurring days to weeks after surgery, is mediated mainly by:

    • A. Recipient cytotoxic T lymphocytes recognizing foreign HLA on the graft
    • B. Complement alone
    • C. Preformed antibodies
    • D. Eosinophils
    • E. Natural killer cells only
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Recipient cytotoxic T lymphocytes recognizing foreign HLA on the graft

    Acute rejection is a T-cell–mediated attack: recipient CD8+ cytotoxic T cells recognize donor HLA (MHC) molecules on graft cells and destroy them, aided by CD4+ helper cells. It typically appears in the first weeks to months and usually responds to intensified immunosuppression — unlike hyperacute rejection, which is irreversible.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 36: Blood Types; Transfusion; Tissue and Organ Transplantation

  12. Matching donor and recipient for which antigens is most important in preventing transplant rejection?

    • A. Platelet antigens
    • B. Human leukocyte antigens (HLA), especially HLA-A, -B, and -DR
    • C. ABO blood group antigens only
    • D. Rhesus antigens
    • E. Red cell minor antigens
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Human leukocyte antigens (HLA), especially HLA-A, -B, and -DR

    HLA molecules (the human MHC) are the principal transplantation antigens; the closer the HLA-A, -B, and -DR match between donor and recipient, the lower the rejection risk and the better the graft survival. ABO compatibility is also required, but HLA matching determines long-term outcome.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 36: Blood Types; Transfusion; Tissue and Organ Transplantation

  13. Cyclosporine prevents transplant rejection mainly by:

    • A. Increasing antibody production
    • B. Blocking histamine receptors
    • C. Killing B lymphocytes directly
    • D. Inhibiting calcineurin in T cells, blocking interleukin-2 production and T-cell activation
    • E. Depleting complement
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Inhibiting calcineurin in T cells, blocking interleukin-2 production and T-cell activation

    Cyclosporine binds cyclophilin and inhibits calcineurin, the phosphatase needed to activate the IL-2 gene in helper T cells. Without IL-2, T-cell proliferation stalls and cellular rejection is suppressed. Its main toxicities are nephrotoxicity and hypertension, so drug levels and renal function are monitored.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 36: Blood Types; Transfusion; Tissue and Organ Transplantation

  14. Graft-versus-host disease occurs after bone marrow transplantation when:

    • A. Immunocompetent donor T cells in the graft attack the immunodeficient recipient's tissues
    • B. The donor was Rh-negative
    • C. Platelets are transfused
    • D. The graft is ABO-incompatible
    • E. The recipient rejects the graft
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Immunocompetent donor T cells in the graft attack the immunodeficient recipient's tissues

    In graft-versus-host disease the immunological direction reverses: donor T lymphocytes transplanted with the marrow recognize the recipient's tissues as foreign and attack skin, gut, and liver. It occurs when the recipient cannot reject the graft (e.g., severe immunodeficiency) and is prevented by T-cell depletion or immunosuppression.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 36: Blood Types; Transfusion; Tissue and Organ Transplantation

  15. A group O mother delivers a group A baby who develops mild jaundice on day 2 with a positive direct Coombs test. This ABO hemolytic disease of the newborn is typically:

    • A. Caused by IgM crossing the placenta
    • B. More severe than Rh disease
    • C. Milder than Rh disease, because fetal A/B antigens are weakly expressed and maternal IgG anti-A/B is limited
    • D. Fatal without exchange transfusion
    • E. Preventable with RhoGAM
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Milder than Rh disease, because fetal A/B antigens are weakly expressed and maternal IgG anti-A/B is limited

    ABO hemolytic disease (usually group O mother, group A or B baby) is generally mild: fetal ABO antigens are sparsely expressed and much maternal antibody is IgM, which cannot cross the placenta. Only the IgG fraction causes hemolysis, producing jaundice that usually responds to phototherapy — unlike Rh disease, which can cause hydrops.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 36: Blood Types; Transfusion; Tissue and Organ Transplantation

  16. Group O individuals are called universal red cell donors because their red cells:

    • A. Carry neither A nor B antigens, so no recipient ABO antibody can attack them
    • B. Contain no hemoglobin
    • C. Lack Rh antigen
    • D. Carry both A and B antigens
    • E. Are resistant to hemolysis
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Carry neither A nor B antigens, so no recipient ABO antibody can attack them

    Group O red cells display neither A nor B antigens on their surface, so neither anti-A nor anti-B in any recipient's plasma can bind and agglutinate them. (Their plasma, however, contains both antibodies — which is why group O whole blood is not a universal plasma donor; AB plasma is.)

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 36: Blood Types; Transfusion; Tissue and Organ Transplantation

  17. Autologous blood transfusion (donating one's own blood before elective surgery) has the major advantage of:

    • A. Eliminating the risks of alloimmunization and transfusion-transmitted infection
    • B. Being usable for anyone
    • C. Stimulating erythropoiesis permanently
    • D. Increasing platelet count
    • E. Requiring no crossmatch ever
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Eliminating the risks of alloimmunization and transfusion-transmitted infection

    Because the donor and recipient are the same person, autologous transfusion cannot cause ABO/Rh incompatibility reactions, alloimmunization, or transmission of donor-derived infections. It is ideal for scheduled surgeries with expected blood loss, though storage time and the patient's fitness to donate limit its use.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 36: Blood Types; Transfusion; Tissue and Organ Transplantation

  18. A delayed hemolytic transfusion reaction, occurring 5–10 days after transfusion with falling hemoglobin and jaundice, is caused by:

    • A. ABO mismatch
    • B. Anamnestic production of antibodies against minor red cell antigens (e.g., Kell, Duffy) from prior sensitization
    • C. Iron deficiency
    • D. Bacterial contamination
    • E. Volume overload
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Anamnestic production of antibodies against minor red cell antigens (e.g., Kell, Duffy) from prior sensitization

    Prior transfusion or pregnancy may sensitize a patient to minor red cell antigens at levels too low to detect. Re-exposure triggers a memory (anamnestic) antibody response days later, destroying the transfused cells extravascularly — with jaundice, falling hemoglobin, and a positive direct Coombs test. Careful antibody screening prevents recurrence.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 36: Blood Types; Transfusion; Tissue and Organ Transplantation

  19. AB plasma (rather than O plasma) is the universal plasma donor because:

    • A. It contains both anti-A and anti-B
    • B. It lacks Rh antigen
    • C. It is the most common group
    • D. It has more clotting factors
    • E. It contains neither anti-A nor anti-B antibodies
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: E. It contains neither anti-A nor anti-B antibodies

    Group AB individuals form neither anti-A nor anti-B, so their plasma can be given to recipients of any ABO group without agglutinating the recipient's red cells. This is the mirror image of red cell transfusion, where group O (no antigens) is the universal donor.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 36: Blood Types; Transfusion; Tissue and Organ Transplantation

  20. The direct (Coombs) antiglobulin test is positive in hemolytic disease of the newborn because:

    • A. Maternal IgG antibodies are already bound to the infant's red cells
    • B. Maternal IgM coats fetal cells
    • C. Complement is absent
    • D. Bilirubin coats the cells
    • E. The infant makes autoantibodies
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Maternal IgG antibodies are already bound to the infant's red cells

    The direct Coombs test uses anti-human globulin to detect antibodies already coating red cells in vivo. In hemolytic disease of the newborn, maternal IgG anti-D (or anti-A/B) has crossed the placenta and bound fetal red cells — so adding Coombs reagent agglutinates them, confirming immune-mediated hemolysis.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 36: Blood Types; Transfusion; Tissue and Organ Transplantation

  21. A patient with blood group O Rh-negative urgently needs red cells, but no O Rh-negative units are available. Which statement about ABO transfusion reactions is correct?

    • A. Group O red cells can be given to most recipients because they lack A and B antigens on their surface
    • B. Group AB plasma is dangerous to all recipients because it contains anti-A and anti-B
    • C. The major reaction in mismatched transfusion is agglutination of donor plasma antibodies with recipient plasma
    • D. Group A red cells are safe for a group O recipient because O plasma has no antibodies
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Group O red cells can be given to most recipients because they lack A and B antigens on their surface

    Group O red cells carry no A or B antigens, so recipient antibodies cannot agglutinate them — the basis of the universal red-cell donor concept. The dangerous reaction is recipient antibodies attacking donor red cells; group O plasma does contain anti-A and anti-B, which is why group A cells would haemolyse in an O recipient.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Blood Types; Transfusion; Tissue and Organ Transplantation

  22. An Rh-negative mother delivers her first Rh-positive baby without complications. To prevent haemolytic disease in a future Rh-positive pregnancy, she should receive:

    • A. A transfusion of Rh-positive blood immediately after delivery
    • B. High-dose vitamin K for one month
    • C. Anti-D immunoglobulin within about 72 hours after delivery
    • D. No treatment, because sensitisation can never occur after the first delivery
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Anti-D immunoglobulin within about 72 hours after delivery

    Anti-D immunoglobulin given soon after delivery clears fetal Rh-positive cells from the mother's circulation before she makes her own anti-D antibodies, preventing sensitisation. Without it, antibodies formed after the first exposure can cross the placenta in later pregnancies and haemolyse fetal red cells.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Blood Types; Transfusion; Tissue and Organ Transplantation
