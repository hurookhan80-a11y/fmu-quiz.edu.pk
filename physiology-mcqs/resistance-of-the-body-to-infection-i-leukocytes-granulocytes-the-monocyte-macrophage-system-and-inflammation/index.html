White blood cells defend the body against invading organisms. Neutrophils and other granulocytes are usually the first cells to reach an infection, while monocytes become macrophages that engulf microbes and debris. Inflammation is the local response that brings these cells, fluid, and mediators to the injured area, producing redness, swelling, heat, and pain.

  1. Neutrophils are the most abundant circulating leukocytes and the first cells to arrive at sites of bacterial invasion. Their dominant killing mechanism inside the phagosome is:

    • A. Phagocytosis of viruses only
    • B. Complement synthesis
    • C. Antibody production
    • D. The respiratory burst generating reactive oxygen species via NADPH oxidase
    • E. Histamine release
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. The respiratory burst generating reactive oxygen species via NADPH oxidase

    After engulfing bacteria, neutrophils activate NADPH oxidase on the phagosome membrane, producing superoxide and other reactive oxygen species (the 'respiratory burst') plus proteolytic enzymes that digest the microbe. This potent system explains rapid bacterial killing — and why defects (e.g., chronic granulomatous disease) cause recurrent infections.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 34: Resistance of the Body to Infection: I. Leukocytes, Granulocytes, the Monocyte-Macrophage System, and Inflammation

  2. The correct sequence of leukocyte recruitment from blood to inflamed tissue is:

    • A. Chemotaxis → margination → diapedesis
    • B. Margination → rolling/adhesion → diapedesis (emigration) → chemotaxis
    • C. Diapedesis → margination → chemotaxis
    • D. Adhesion → chemotaxis → margination
    • E. Phagocytosis → diapedesis → margination
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Margination → rolling/adhesion → diapedesis (emigration) → chemotaxis

    Leukocytes first marginate (move to the vessel wall) as flow slows, then roll and adhere firmly via selectins and integrins, squeeze between endothelial cells (diapedesis/emigration), and finally migrate up the chemotactic gradient toward the microbes. Each step is molecularly distinct and a potential therapeutic target in inflammation.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 34: Resistance of the Body to Infection: I. Leukocytes, Granulocytes, the Monocyte-Macrophage System, and Inflammation

  3. Chemotaxis — the directed migration of neutrophils toward bacteria — is triggered by all of the following EXCEPT:

    • A. Bacterial peptides such as formyl-methionyl peptides
    • B. Leukotrienes
    • C. Increased arterial oxygen tension
    • D. Complement fragments C3a and C5a
    • E. Products of injured tissues
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Increased arterial oxygen tension

    Neutrophils sense chemical gradients of bacterial products, complement fragments (C3a, C5a), leukotrienes, and substances released by damaged tissues, migrating toward the highest concentration. Arterial oxygen tension is not a chemotactic signal; hypoxia at inflamed sites instead promotes cell activation and mediator release.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 34: Resistance of the Body to Infection: I. Leukocytes, Granulocytes, the Monocyte-Macrophage System, and Inflammation

  4. Opsonization enhances phagocytosis because:

    • A. It increases red cell production
    • B. Coating particles with IgG antibodies or C3b lets phagocytes grip them via Fc and complement receptors
    • C. It blocks chemotaxis
    • D. It neutralizes bacterial toxins directly
    • E. It dilates blood vessels
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Coating particles with IgG antibodies or C3b lets phagocytes grip them via Fc and complement receptors

    Opsonins — mainly IgG antibodies and the complement fragment C3b — coat microbial surfaces, and phagocytes possess Fc receptors (for IgG) and complement receptors (for C3b) that latch onto them. This molecular 'handle' greatly accelerates engulfment, which is why antibody and complement deficiencies predispose to bacterial infections.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 34: Resistance of the Body to Infection: I. Leukocytes, Granulocytes, the Monocyte-Macrophage System, and Inflammation

  5. Monocytes that enter tissues differentiate into macrophages, which differ from neutrophils in that macrophages:

    • A. Release histamine
    • B. Die after a single phagocytic event
    • C. Cannot leave the bloodstream
    • D. Survive for months, digest larger particles, and present antigens to lymphocytes
    • E. Produce antibodies
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Survive for months, digest larger particles, and present antigens to lymphocytes

    Tissue macrophages are long-lived professional phagocytes that engulf larger debris, dead cells, and pathogens, then process and present antigens to lymphocytes — bridging innate and adaptive immunity. Neutrophils, by contrast, are short-lived kamikaze cells that die after killing (forming pus), while macrophages persist for months.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 34: Resistance of the Body to Infection: I. Leukocytes, Granulocytes, the Monocyte-Macrophage System, and Inflammation

  6. Kupffer cells are tissue macrophages located in the:

    • A. Liver sinusoids
    • B. Skin epidermis
    • C. Alveoli of the lung
    • D. Bone
    • E. Brain
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Liver sinusoids

    Kupffer cells line the liver sinusoids and filter blood arriving from the gut via the portal vein, clearing bacteria, endotoxin, and worn-out red cells. They are part of the mononuclear phagocyte (reticuloendothelial) system, whose other members include microglia in the brain, alveolar macrophages in the lung, and Langerhans cells in the skin.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 34: Resistance of the Body to Infection: I. Leukocytes, Granulocytes, the Monocyte-Macrophage System, and Inflammation

  7. A child with recurrent parasitic worm infections would be expected to show an increase in:

    • A. Monocytes
    • B. Neutrophils
    • C. Basophils
    • D. Lymphocytes
    • E. Eosinophils
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: E. Eosinophils

    Eosinophils specialize in defense against multicellular parasites (helminths), attacking them with toxic granule proteins such as major basic protein. Eosinophilia also occurs in allergic diseases, where eosinophils contribute to tissue damage in asthma and allergic inflammation.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 34: Resistance of the Body to Infection: I. Leukocytes, Granulocytes, the Monocyte-Macrophage System, and Inflammation

  8. Basophils and their tissue counterparts, mast cells, contribute to inflammation mainly by releasing:

    • A. Antibodies
    • B. Lysozyme
    • C. Interferon-gamma
    • D. Histamine and heparin
    • E. Complement
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Histamine and heparin

    Basophils in blood and mast cells in tissues store histamine (which dilates vessels and increases permeability) and heparin (an anticoagulant) in their granules. Their degranulation — classically triggered by IgE in allergy — initiates the vascular changes of acute inflammation and anaphylaxis.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 34: Resistance of the Body to Infection: I. Leukocytes, Granulocytes, the Monocyte-Macrophage System, and Inflammation

  9. The four cardinal signs of acute inflammation — rubor, tumor, calor, and dolor — are caused principally by:

    • A. Platelet aggregation alone
    • B. Lymphocyte proliferation
    • C. Fibrosis
    • D. Vasoconstriction and reduced permeability
    • E. Vasodilation, increased vascular permeability, and mediator stimulation of nerve endings
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: E. Vasodilation, increased vascular permeability, and mediator stimulation of nerve endings

    Histamine, prostaglandins, and kinins dilate arterioles (redness, heat) and make venules leaky so plasma proteins and leukocytes escape (swelling); prostaglandins and bradykinin sensitize pain fibers (dolor). A fifth sign, functio laesa (loss of function), was added later. These vascular events deliver defenses to the injured site.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 34: Resistance of the Body to Infection: I. Leukocytes, Granulocytes, the Monocyte-Macrophage System, and Inflammation

  10. Bradykinin, generated during inflammation, is especially important for:

    • A. Activating platelets only
    • B. Causing pain and further vasodilation
    • C. Producing antibodies
    • D. Constricting bronchioles
    • E. Stimulating erythropoiesis
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Causing pain and further vasodilation

    Bradykinin, a kinin released from plasma precursors at inflamed sites, is one of the most potent pain-producing substances known and also dilates vessels and increases permeability. It explains much of the aching pain of inflammation, while prostaglandins amplify its effects — which is why NSAIDs relieve inflammatory pain.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 34: Resistance of the Body to Infection: I. Leukocytes, Granulocytes, the Monocyte-Macrophage System, and Inflammation

  11. Complement fragments C3a and C5a (anaphylatoxins) promote inflammation by:

    • A. Producing antibodies
    • B. Directly killing bacteria through membrane attack
    • C. Constricting all vessels
    • D. Triggering mast cell degranulation and acting as chemoattractants for neutrophils
    • E. Inhibiting phagocytosis
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Triggering mast cell degranulation and acting as chemoattractants for neutrophils

    C3a and C5a, released during complement activation, degranulate mast cells (releasing histamine) and are powerful chemoattractants that summon neutrophils and monocytes. The membrane attack complex (C5b-9) separately punches holes in bacterial membranes — so complement both recruits cells and directly lyses microbes.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 34: Resistance of the Body to Infection: I. Leukocytes, Granulocytes, the Monocyte-Macrophage System, and Inflammation

  12. Pus at the site of a bacterial infection consists mainly of:

    • A. Dead neutrophils, tissue debris, and dead bacteria
    • B. Living lymphocytes only
    • C. Fibrin clots only
    • D. Plasma proteins alone
    • E. Red blood cells
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Dead neutrophils, tissue debris, and dead bacteria

    Pus is the graveyard of the acute inflammatory battle: neutrophils that died after phagocytosing bacteria, liquefied tissue debris, and dead microbes. Its formation reflects the short lifespan of neutrophils, in contrast to the longer-lived macrophages that dominate chronic inflammation and granulomas.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 34: Resistance of the Body to Infection: I. Leukocytes, Granulocytes, the Monocyte-Macrophage System, and Inflammation

  13. Microglia are:

    • A. Bone-resorbing cells
    • B. Skin dendritic cells
    • C. Resident macrophages of the central nervous system
    • D. Alveolar phagocytes
    • E. Liver macrophages
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Resident macrophages of the central nervous system

    Microglia are the mononuclear phagocyte system's representatives in the brain and spinal cord, where they scavenge debris, prune synapses, and respond to injury or infection. Like other tissue macrophages (Kupffer cells in liver, alveolar macrophages in lung, osteoclasts in bone), they derive from monocyte lineage precursors.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 34: Resistance of the Body to Infection: I. Leukocytes, Granulocytes, the Monocyte-Macrophage System, and Inflammation

  14. Osteoclasts, which resorb bone, belong to which cell system?

    • A. The lymphoid lineage
    • B. The osteoblast lineage
    • C. The monocyte-macrophage (mononuclear phagocyte) system
    • D. The fibroblast family
    • E. The megakaryocyte lineage
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. The monocyte-macrophage (mononuclear phagocyte) system

    Osteoclasts are specialized multinucleated members of the monocyte-macrophage system, formed by fusion of monocyte precursors. They dissolve bone mineral and matrix during remodeling — illustrating how widely the macrophage lineage is deployed, from brain microglia to bone.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 34: Resistance of the Body to Infection: I. Leukocytes, Granulocytes, the Monocyte-Macrophage System, and Inflammation

  15. Langerhans cells of the epidermis function mainly as:

    • A. Pain receptors
    • B. Antigen-presenting dendritic cells that carry skin antigens to lymph nodes
    • C. Sweat gland regulators
    • D. Melanin synthesizers
    • E. Keratin producers
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Antigen-presenting dendritic cells that carry skin antigens to lymph nodes

    Langerhans cells are dendritic antigen-presenting cells stationed in the skin. They capture invading antigens, migrate to regional lymph nodes, and present them to T lymphocytes — initiating adaptive immune responses and explaining their central role in contact hypersensitivity (e.g., nickel allergy).

  16. A patient with bacterial pneumonia has a total leukocyte count of 22,000/µL with 85% neutrophils. This neutrophilic leukocytosis reflects:

    • A. Mobilization of marginated and marrow-reserve neutrophils plus increased production in response to infection
    • B. Bone marrow failure
    • C. Viral suppression of marrow
    • D. Autoimmune neutropenia
    • E. Aplastic anemia
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Mobilization of marginated and marrow-reserve neutrophils plus increased production in response to infection

    Acute bacterial infection triggers demargination of neutrophils clinging to vessel walls, release of the marrow's storage pool, and accelerated granulopoiesis — producing neutrophilic leukocytosis, often with a 'left shift' (immature band forms). It is a hallmark of the innate response to pyogenic bacteria.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 34: Resistance of the Body to Infection: I. Leukocytes, Granulocytes, the Monocyte-Macrophage System, and Inflammation

  17. A patient on carbimazole develops fever, sore throat, and a neutrophil count near zero. This agranulocytosis is dangerous mainly because:

    • A. Platelets fall simultaneously
    • B. The bone marrow fibroses
    • C. Red cells cannot carry oxygen
    • D. Antibodies disappear
    • E. Loss of neutrophils removes the first line of defense against bacteria, risking overwhelming sepsis
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: E. Loss of neutrophils removes the first line of defense against bacteria, risking overwhelming sepsis

    Agranulocytosis — near-total loss of neutrophils, often drug-induced — leaves the body without its rapid-response phagocytes. Even normal mouth and gut flora can then cause fulminant infection, so fever with sore throat in such patients demands urgent blood counts and broad-spectrum antibiotics.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 34: Resistance of the Body to Infection: I. Leukocytes, Granulocytes, the Monocyte-Macrophage System, and Inflammation

  18. Alveolar macrophages protect the lungs by:

    • A. Producing surfactant
    • B. Filtering lymph
    • C. Secreting mucus
    • D. Phagocytosing inhaled particles, microbes, and debris that reach the alveoli
    • E. Constricting bronchioles
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Phagocytosing inhaled particles, microbes, and debris that reach the alveoli

    Alveolar macrophages patrol the airspaces, engulfing dust, bacteria, and cellular debris that evade the mucociliary escalator. In smokers they become laden with tar ('dust cells'). When overwhelmed — as in heavy pneumococcal infection — recruited neutrophils take over, sometimes at the cost of alveolar damage.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 34: Resistance of the Body to Infection: I. Leukocytes, Granulocytes, the Monocyte-Macrophage System, and Inflammation

  19. Prostaglandins contribute to inflammation by causing:

    • A. Platelet inhibition only
    • B. Immunosuppression
    • C. Bronchodilation
    • D. Vasoconstriction and reduced permeability
    • E. Vasodilation, increased permeability, pain sensitization, and fever
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: E. Vasodilation, increased permeability, pain sensitization, and fever

    Prostaglandins (especially PGE2), generated from arachidonic acid by cyclooxygenase, dilate vessels, increase permeability, sensitize pain receptors, and act on the hypothalamus to produce fever. This is why NSAIDs — which block cyclooxygenase — reduce pain, swelling, and fever together.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 34: Resistance of the Body to Infection: I. Leukocytes, Granulocytes, the Monocyte-Macrophage System, and Inflammation

  20. Selectins and integrins are important in inflammation because they mediate:

    • A. Rolling and firm adhesion of leukocytes to activated endothelium before emigration
    • B. Antibody production
    • C. Fibrin formation
    • D. Histamine synthesis
    • E. Complement activation
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Rolling and firm adhesion of leukocytes to activated endothelium before emigration

    Inflamed endothelium expresses selectins that tether rolling leukocytes, while activated integrins on the leukocyte bind endothelial ICAMs for firm adhesion — the prerequisite for diapedesis. Blocking these adhesion molecules is a therapeutic strategy in diseases driven by excessive leukocyte recruitment.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 34: Resistance of the Body to Infection: I. Leukocytes, Granulocytes, the Monocyte-Macrophage System, and Inflammation

  21. During phagocytosis, a neutrophil kills ingested bacteria most effectively by:

    • A. Releasing histamine from its specific granules only
    • B. Secreting erythropoietin into the phagosome
    • C. Generating reactive oxygen species through the respiratory (oxidative) burst
    • D. Converting fibrinogen to fibrin inside the cell
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Generating reactive oxygen species through the respiratory (oxidative) burst

    Activated neutrophils and macrophages assemble NADPH oxidase to produce superoxide, hydrogen peroxide and other reactive oxygen species that kill microbes inside the phagolysosome. Histamine is chiefly a basophil/mast cell mediator, and neither erythropoietin release nor intracellular fibrin formation is a killing mechanism.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Resistance of the Body to Infection: I. Leukocytes, Granulocytes, the Monocyte-Macrophage System, and Inflammation
