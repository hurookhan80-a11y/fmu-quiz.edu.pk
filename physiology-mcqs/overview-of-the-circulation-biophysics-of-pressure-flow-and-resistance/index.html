Overview of the Circulation; Biophysics of Pressure, Flow, and Resistance MCQs for FMU 1st Year MBBS Physiology

Home › Physiology MCQs › Overview of the Circulation; Biophysics of Pressure, Flow, and Resistance

Blood flows through the circulation because the heart creates a pressure difference between the arteries and the veins. Flow through any vessel depends on that pressure difference and on the resistance of the vessel, which rises sharply as the vessel narrows. The circulation is arranged so that the same blood passes through the lungs and the body in sequence.

  1. Turbulent blood flow is most likely to occur when which factor increases?

    • A. Vessel length alone decreases
    • B. Blood viscosity falls to zero
    • C. Vessel diameter becomes very small
    • D. Velocity of blood flow (high Reynolds number)
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Velocity of blood flow (high Reynolds number)

    Turbulence develops when Reynolds number rises with high velocity, large diameter, low viscosity or irregular vessel walls. In normal physiology, increased velocity is the exam factor tested here.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 14: Overview of the Circulation; Biophysics of Pressure, Flow, and Resistance

  2. If vessel diameter (radius) is halved, resistance to blood flow increases by how many times (Poiseuille's law)?

    • A. 2 times
    • B. 16 times
    • C. 4 times
    • D. 8 times
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. 16 times

    Poiseuille's law states resistance is inversely proportional to radius to the fourth power (1/r^4). Halving the radius gives (1/(1/2)^4) = 16 times the resistance.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 14: Overview of the Circulation; Biophysics of Pressure, Flow, and Resistance

  3. Which substance is a powerful local/systemic vasoconstrictor in this recall?

    • A. Nitric oxide
    • B. Prostacyclin
    • C. Angiotensin II
    • D. Atrial natriuretic peptide
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Angiotensin II

    Angiotensin II is one of the most potent vasoconstrictors and also stimulates aldosterone release to retain sodium and water. Nitric oxide and prostacyclin are vasodilators.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 14: Overview of the Circulation; Biophysics of Pressure, Flow, and Resistance

  4. According to Poiseuille's law, if the radius of a vessel is halved, resistance to flow increases by a factor of:

    • A. 2
    • B. 8
    • C. 32
    • D. 16
    • E. 4
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. 16

    Poiseuille's law gives flow F = ΔP·π·r⁴/8ηl, so resistance R = 8ηl/πr⁴ — inversely proportional to the FOURTH power of radius. Halving r multiplies resistance 16-fold (2⁴). This is why tiny arteriolar constriction so powerfully raises blood pressure.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 14: Overview of the Circulation; Biophysics of Pressure, Flow, and Resistance

  5. The greatest drop in blood pressure in the systemic circulation occurs across the:

    • A. Capillaries
    • B. Large arteries
    • C. Aorta
    • D. Vena cava
    • E. Arterioles
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: E. Arterioles

    Although individually tiny, arterioles have thick smooth-muscle walls and high tone, and the pressure drop from ~90 mmHg to ~30 mmHg occurs across them — the steepest gradient in the circulation. They are therefore the principal site of vascular resistance and the main effectors of blood pressure control.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 14: Overview of the Circulation; Biophysics of Pressure, Flow, and Resistance

  6. Turbulent blood flow is most likely when:

    • A. Vessels are very long
    • B. The Reynolds number exceeds ~2000, as in anemia (low viscosity) or stenosis (high velocity)
    • C. Pressure is very low
    • D. Blood is cold
    • E. Velocity is very low
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. The Reynolds number exceeds ~2000, as in anemia (low viscosity) or stenosis (high velocity)

    Reynolds number Re = (velocity × diameter × density)/viscosity; above ~2000, laminar sheets break into turbulence. Anemia lowers viscosity and stenosis raises velocity — both push Re up, which is why anemic patients and stenotic valves develop murmurs without any structural valve disease.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 14: Overview of the Circulation; Biophysics of Pressure, Flow, and Resistance

  7. A systolic murmur in aortic stenosis is caused by:

    • A. Smooth laminar flow
    • B. Valve regurgitation
    • C. Turbulent flow through the narrowed valve orifice
    • D. A venous hum
    • E. Pericardial friction
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Turbulent flow through the narrowed valve orifice

    Blood jetting through the narrowed aortic orifice at high velocity exceeds the critical Reynolds number, breaking into chaotic turbulent eddies whose vibrations are heard as a systolic murmur. Murmurs are thus acoustic signatures of turbulence, not of laminar flow.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 14: Overview of the Circulation; Biophysics of Pressure, Flow, and Resistance

  8. About 64% of the total blood volume is contained in the:

    • A. Heart chambers
    • B. Systemic veins and venules
    • C. Aorta and large arteries
    • D. Pulmonary circulation
    • E. Capillaries
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Systemic veins and venules

    The systemic venous system — venules and veins — is highly compliant and holds about 64% of blood volume, acting as the body's adjustable reservoir. Only ~7% is in the heart and ~9% in the pulmonary circuit. Sympathetic venoconstriction can mobilize this reservoir to support cardiac output in shock.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 14: Overview of the Circulation; Biophysics of Pressure, Flow, and Resistance

  9. Mean pulmonary arterial pressure is approximately:

    • A. 5 mmHg
    • B. 16 mmHg
    • C. 60 mmHg
    • D. 120 mmHg
    • E. 100 mmHg
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. 16 mmHg

    The pulmonary circuit is a low-pressure, low-resistance system: systolic ~25, diastolic ~8, mean ~16 mmHg — about one-sixth of systemic pressures. This protects the thin alveolar-capillary membrane from fluid leakage while still perfusing the entire lung.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 14: Overview of the Circulation; Biophysics of Pressure, Flow, and Resistance

  10. In left ventricular failure, pulmonary edema develops because:

    • A. Elevated left atrial pressure is transmitted back, raising pulmonary capillary hydrostatic pressure above oncotic pressure
    • B. Plasma proteins increase
    • C. Pulmonary lymphatics dilate
    • D. Pulmonary arterial pressure falls
    • E. Right ventricular output falls
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Elevated left atrial pressure is transmitted back, raising pulmonary capillary hydrostatic pressure above oncotic pressure

    When the failing left ventricle cannot eject normally, left atrial pressure rises and backs up into the pulmonary veins and capillaries. Once capillary hydrostatic pressure exceeds plasma oncotic pressure (~28 mmHg), fluid floods the alveoli — pulmonary edema, the hallmark of left heart failure.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 14: Overview of the Circulation; Biophysics of Pressure, Flow, and Resistance

  11. By the Fick principle, cardiac output equals:

    • A. Body surface area × 3
    • B. Mean arterial pressure divided by total peripheral resistance
    • C. End-diastolic volume minus end-systolic volume
    • D. Heart rate × stroke volume
    • E. Oxygen consumption divided by the arteriovenous oxygen difference
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: E. Oxygen consumption divided by the arteriovenous oxygen difference

    The Fick principle conserves mass: the oxygen the lungs add to blood per minute must equal what the tissues remove. So cardiac output = O2 uptake ÷ (arterial − venous O2 content). It is the gold-standard reference method, using pulmonary artery catheter samples.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 14: Overview of the Circulation; Biophysics of Pressure, Flow, and Resistance

  12. A patient's oxygen consumption is 250 mL/min, arterial O2 content 190 mL/L, and mixed venous O2 content 140 mL/L. Cardiac output is:

    • A. 2.5 L/min
    • B. 3.0 L/min
    • C. 5.0 L/min
    • D. 7.5 L/min
    • E. 10 L/min
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. 5.0 L/min

    CO = 250 mL/min ÷ (190 − 140) mL/L = 250/50 = 5.0 L/min — a normal resting value. Note the units: the AV difference must be in mL per LITER to yield L/min. This calculation is a send-up staple.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 14: Overview of the Circulation; Biophysics of Pressure, Flow, and Resistance

  13. The normal cardiac index is approximately:

    • A. 1.0 L/min/m²
    • B. 0.5 L/min/m²
    • C. 7.0 L/min/m²
    • D. 3.0 L/min/m²
    • E. 5.0 L/min/m²
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. 3.0 L/min/m²

    Cardiac index normalizes output to body size: ~3.0 L/min/m² (range ~2.5-4.0). It lets you compare a small woman with a large man fairly — a raw output of 4 L/min might be heart failure in one and normal in the other.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 14: Overview of the Circulation; Biophysics of Pressure, Flow, and Resistance

  14. Mean systemic filling pressure (about 7 mmHg) is determined by:

    • A. Blood volume and the tone (compliance) of the systemic vessels, especially the veins
    • B. Heart rate
    • C. Respiratory rate
    • D. Arterial blood pressure
    • E. Cardiac output
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Blood volume and the tone (compliance) of the systemic vessels, especially the veins

    If the heart stopped, pressure everywhere in the systemic circulation would settle at ~7 mmHg — the mean systemic filling pressure, set by how much blood is squeezed into the compliant vessels (mainly veins). Raising blood volume or venomotor tone raises MSFP and thus the driving force for venous return.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 14: Overview of the Circulation; Biophysics of Pressure, Flow, and Resistance

  15. Acute hemorrhage decreases cardiac output mainly by:

    • A. Constricting the arterioles
    • B. Increasing myocardial contractility
    • C. Lowering mean systemic filling pressure and therefore venous return
    • D. Increasing blood viscosity
    • E. Increasing the afterload
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Lowering mean systemic filling pressure and therefore venous return

    Bleeding empties the vascular reservoir, so mean systemic filling pressure falls and the pressure gradient driving blood back to the heart shrinks. Venous return — and hence cardiac output, which must equal it in the steady state — falls in parallel. This is why fluids restore output in hemorrhagic shock.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 14: Overview of the Circulation; Biophysics of Pressure, Flow, and Resistance

  16. Sympathetic stimulation increases venous return by:

    • A. Venoconstriction, which raises mean systemic filling pressure
    • B. Dilating the veins
    • C. Decreasing total blood volume
    • D. Slowing the heart
    • E. Increasing capillary permeability
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Venoconstriction, which raises mean systemic filling pressure

    Sympathetic venoconstriction stiffens the venous reservoir, raising mean systemic filling pressure and steepening the gradient for venous return. This translocates blood centrally — a key compensation in shock and exercise, independent of any change in total blood volume.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 14: Overview of the Circulation; Biophysics of Pressure, Flow, and Resistance

  17. The pressure in the vena cava and right atrium is normally about:

    • A. 25 mmHg
    • B. 0-2 mmHg
    • C. 80 mmHg
    • D. -10 mmHg
    • E. 100 mmHg
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. 0-2 mmHg

    By the time blood traverses the systemic circuit, nearly all pressure energy is dissipated — the great veins and right atrium sit at 0-2 mmHg. This near-zero central venous pressure is why venous return depends on small gradients (MSFP − RAP) and on the muscle and respiratory pumps.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 14: Overview of the Circulation; Biophysics of Pressure, Flow, and Resistance

  18. In polycythemia, increased blood viscosity:

    • A. Causes reflex vasodilation
    • B. Decreases vascular resistance
    • C. Decreases the hematocrit
    • D. Increases resistance to flow, raising blood pressure and cardiac workload
    • E. Has no hemodynamic effect
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Increases resistance to flow, raising blood pressure and cardiac workload

    Viscosity is dominated by hematocrit (red cell concentration). In polycythemia the thick blood raises resistance (R ∝ η in Poiseuille's law), forcing higher pressures to maintain flow and burdening the heart — one reason phlebotomy helps polycythemia vera.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 14: Overview of the Circulation; Biophysics of Pressure, Flow, and Resistance

  19. The indicator (dye) dilution method measures cardiac output from:

    • A. Oxygen consumption
    • B. The time-concentration curve of an injected indicator (Stewart-Hamilton principle)
    • C. Brachial blood pressure
    • D. Heart sounds
    • E. The surface ECG
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. The time-concentration curve of an injected indicator (Stewart-Hamilton principle)

    A known amount of indicator (dye or cold saline) is injected upstream and its concentration sampled downstream over time; cardiac output = amount injected ÷ area under the concentration-time curve (Stewart-Hamilton equation). Thermodilution with a Swan-Ganz catheter is the bedside version.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 14: Overview of the Circulation; Biophysics of Pressure, Flow, and Resistance

  20. In vessels arranged in parallel (as in the systemic organs), the total resistance:

    • A. Cannot be calculated
    • B. Equals the sum of the individual resistances
    • C. Equals the largest single resistance
    • D. Is infinite
    • E. Is less than any single vessel's resistance (1/R = sum of 1/Ri)
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: E. Is less than any single vessel's resistance (1/R = sum of 1/Ri)

    Parallel resistors add reciprocally: 1/R_total = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + ... — so total resistance is SMALLER than any branch. Removing (or constricting) parallel beds raises total peripheral resistance; this is why widespread arteriolar constriction so effectively raises blood pressure.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 14: Overview of the Circulation; Biophysics of Pressure, Flow, and Resistance

  21. The mean functional capillary hydrostatic pressure is about:

    • A. 100 mmHg
    • B. 5 mmHg
    • C. 0 mmHg
    • D. 17 mmHg
    • E. 60 mmHg
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. 17 mmHg

    Capillary pressure falls along the bed from ~30 mmHg at the arterial end to ~10 at the venous end, averaging ~17 mmHg — the 'functional' mean weighted by filtration. This low pressure permits exchange without rupturing the single-cell endothelium, balanced against oncotic pressure in Starling forces.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 14: Overview of the Circulation; Biophysics of Pressure, Flow, and Resistance

  22. Laminar blood flow has a velocity profile that is:

    • A. Pulsatile only, never steady
    • B. Flat across the vessel
    • C. Completely random
    • D. Parabolic, fastest in the center and slowest near the wall
    • E. Fastest at the wall
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Parabolic, fastest in the center and slowest near the wall

    In laminar flow, fluid moves in concentric layers with no mixing: the central lamina is fastest, velocity falling parabolically to near zero at the wall (no-slip condition). This profile minimizes energy loss; turbulence destroys it and dissipates energy as sound and heat.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 14: Overview of the Circulation; Biophysics of Pressure, Flow, and Resistance

  23. A 40-year-old man with chronic severe anemia has a systolic murmur but no valve disease. The murmur is best explained by:

    • A. Pericarditis
    • B. Aortic dissection
    • C. Infective endocarditis
    • D. Low blood viscosity lowering the threshold for turbulent flow
    • E. Essential hypertension
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Low blood viscosity lowering the threshold for turbulent flow

    Severe anemia halves viscosity, which lowers the Reynolds number threshold for turbulence — fast flow through normal valves then becomes turbulent and audible. Such 'flow murmurs' vanish when the anemia is corrected, distinguishing them from structural valve disease.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 14: Overview of the Circulation; Biophysics of Pressure, Flow, and Resistance
