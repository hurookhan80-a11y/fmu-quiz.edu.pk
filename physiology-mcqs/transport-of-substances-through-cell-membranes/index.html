Transport of Substances Through Cell Membranes MCQs for FMU 1st Year MBBS Physiology

Home › Physiology MCQs › Transport of Substances Through Cell Membranes

Substances cross cell membranes by several routes. Small lipid-soluble molecules diffuse directly through the membrane, while water and small solutes use channels, and larger or charged molecules need carrier proteins. Active transport uses energy to move substances against their gradient, and bulk transport moves large particles in vesicles.

  1. According to Fick's law of diffusion, which change would DECREASE the net rate of simple diffusion across a membrane?

    • A. Raising the temperature
    • B. Reducing the concentration gradient
    • C. Increasing the lipid solubility of the solute
    • D. Increasing the membrane surface area
    • E. Using a thinner membrane
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Reducing the concentration gradient

    Fick's law states that net flux is proportional to the concentration gradient and membrane area, and inversely proportional to membrane thickness. The other options increase diffusion: higher temperature speeds molecular motion, lipid solubility raises permeability, and larger gradients drive faster net movement. Only a shallower gradient slows it.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 4: Transport of Substances Through Cell Membranes

  2. Facilitated diffusion differs from simple diffusion in that facilitated diffusion:

    • A. Requires metabolic energy
    • B. Moves solute against its gradient
    • C. Shows saturation, specificity, and competition
    • D. Occurs only for ions
    • E. Is faster than simple diffusion at all concentrations
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Shows saturation, specificity, and competition

    Facilitated diffusion uses a carrier protein, so it is selective, can be saturated when all carriers are occupied, and can be competitively inhibited — yet it still moves only down the gradient and needs no ATP. GLUT transporters for glucose are the classic example. Simple diffusion through the lipid bilayer shows none of these carrier features.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 4: Transport of Substances Through Cell Membranes

  3. Glucose enters most body cells by:

    • A. Simple diffusion through the lipid bilayer
    • B. Facilitated diffusion via GLUT transporters
    • C. Primary active transport via Na+/K+ ATPase
    • D. Osmosis through aquaporins
    • E. Pinocytosis
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Facilitated diffusion via GLUT transporters

    Most cells admit glucose through GLUT carrier proteins by facilitated diffusion — down its gradient, no ATP. Only intestinal and renal tubular cells move glucose uphill, using secondary active Na+-glucose cotransport (SGLT). Insulin increases GLUT4 insertion in muscle and adipose, which is why those tissues clear glucose after meals.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 4: Transport of Substances Through Cell Membranes

  4. The Na+/K+ ATPase (sodium-potassium pump) transports, per ATP hydrolyzed:

    • A. 2 Na+ out and 3 K+ in
    • B. 3 Na+ out and 2 K+ in
    • C. 1 Na+ out and 1 K+ in
    • D. 3 K+ out and 2 Na+ in
    • E. Equal Na+ and K+ exchange
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. 3 Na+ out and 2 K+ in

    Each cycle exports 3 Na+ and imports 2 K+ at the cost of one ATP, making the pump electrogenic (net outward positive current) and the major determinant of the resting membrane potential. It maintains the Na+ and K+ gradients that drive secondary active transport and excitable cell function. Digitalis and ouabain inhibit it.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 4: Transport of Substances Through Cell Membranes

  5. Ouabain (and therapeutic digitalis) inhibits the Na+/K+ ATPase. The immediate cellular consequences include:

    • A. Intracellular Na+ falls and K+ rises
    • B. Intracellular Na+ rises, extracellular K+ rises, and cardiac contractility increases
    • C. Intracellular Ca2+ falls
    • D. The resting membrane becomes more negative
    • E. Osmotic pressure falls
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Intracellular Na+ rises, extracellular K+ rises, and cardiac contractility increases

    Pump blockade raises intracellular Na+, which slows the Na+/Ca2+ exchanger, raising cytosolic Ca2+ and strengthening cardiac contraction — the therapeutic basis of digoxin in heart failure. Excess blockade causes dangerous hyperkalemia and arrhythmias, since extracellular K+ rises as the pump fails.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 4: Transport of Substances Through Cell Membranes

  6. In the small intestine, glucose is absorbed against its concentration gradient. The energy for this ultimately comes from:

    • A. Direct ATP hydrolysis by the glucose carrier
    • B. The inward Na+ electrochemical gradient via SGLT cotransport
    • C. The outward K+ gradient
    • D. A Ca2+ counter-exchange
    • E. Facilitated diffusion alone
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. The inward Na+ electrochemical gradient via SGLT cotransport

    SGLT1 couples one glucose to two Na+ moving down their steep electrochemical gradient, dragging glucose uphill into the enterocyte. The Na+ gradient itself is built by the basolateral Na+/K+ ATPase — hence 'secondary' active transport. Renal proximal tubules use SGLT2 similarly, the target of gliflozin drugs.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 4: Transport of Substances Through Cell Membranes

  7. Which of the following is an example of counter-transport (antiport)?

    • A. Na+-glucose symport
    • B. Na+-amino acid symport
    • C. Na+/Ca2+ exchange
    • D. K+ leak channel
    • E. Glucose uniport
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Na+/Ca2+ exchange

    Counter-transport (antiport) moves two solutes in opposite directions — Na+ in drives Ca2+ or H+ out. Cotransport (symport) moves them the same way, as in Na+-glucose or Na+-amino acid uptake. Both are secondary active processes powered by the Na+ gradient.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 4: Transport of Substances Through Cell Membranes

  8. The osmotic pressure of a solution is determined principally by:

    • A. The size of the solute molecules
    • B. The number of solute particles per unit volume
    • C. The electrical charge of the solute
    • D. The lipid solubility of the solute
    • E. The temperature only
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. The number of solute particles per unit volume

    Osmotic pressure depends on the number of dissolved particles per unit volume (van't Hoff's law), not their size or charge. One mole of NaCl dissociates into two osmoles and exerts roughly twice the osmotic pressure of one mole of glucose. This is why osmolarity, not molarity, predicts water movement.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 4: Transport of Substances Through Cell Membranes

  9. Red blood cells placed in a hypotonic solution will:

    • A. Shrink (crenate)
    • B. Swell and may burst (hemolyze)
    • C. Remain unchanged
    • D. Lose potassium only
    • E. Become permeable to proteins
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Swell and may burst (hemolyze)

    In hypotonic surroundings water enters the cell down its gradient; red cells, lacking a wall, swell until the membrane ruptures (hemolysis). In hypertonic solution water leaves and the cell crenates. Isotonic (0.9%) saline neither swells nor shrinks red cells, which is why it is the standard IV fluid.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 4: Transport of Substances Through Cell Membranes

  10. Water crosses most cell membranes most rapidly through:

    • A. Simple diffusion through the lipid bilayer
    • B. Aquaporin water channels
    • C. The Na+/K+ ATPase
    • D. Voltage-gated Ca2+ channels
    • E. Pinocytotic vesicles
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Aquaporin water channels

    Aquaporins are dedicated protein water channels that let water cross at high speed while excluding solutes — far faster than seepage through the lipid bilayer. ADH (vasopressin) inserts aquaporin-2 into collecting-duct cells to concentrate urine. Simple diffusion of water through lipid is slow by comparison.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 4: Transport of Substances Through Cell Membranes

  11. Which is a defining characteristic of primary active transport?

    • A. It never saturates
    • B. It directly uses metabolic energy (ATP) to move solute against its gradient
    • C. It moves solute only down its gradient
    • D. It requires no carrier protein
    • E. It is unaffected by metabolic poisons
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. It directly uses metabolic energy (ATP) to move solute against its gradient

    Primary active transport hydrolyzes ATP directly on the transporter to move solute uphill. It shows the carrier hallmarks: specificity, saturation, and competitive inhibition. The Na+/K+ ATPase, H+/K+ ATPase of the stomach, and Ca2+ pumps of the SR are the major examples.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 4: Transport of Substances Through Cell Membranes

  12. In secondary active transport, the immediate energy source is:

    • A. Direct hydrolysis of ATP by the carrier
    • B. The electrochemical gradient of another ion, usually Na+
    • C. The membrane potential alone
    • D. Osmotic pressure
    • E. Thermal motion of water
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. The electrochemical gradient of another ion, usually Na+

    Secondary active transport spends no ATP itself; it hitches a ride on an ion (usually Na+) flowing down the gradient created by primary active transport. The Na+/K+ ATPase continuously rebuilds that gradient. Examples include intestinal glucose/amino acid uptake and the Na+/Ca2+ exchanger.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 4: Transport of Substances Through Cell Membranes

  13. The Nernst (equilibrium) potential for an ion is the membrane voltage at which:

    • A. Maximum ion flux occurs
    • B. There is no net movement of that ion across the membrane
    • C. The membrane becomes impermeable to the ion
    • D. Active transport stops
    • E. The ion precipitates
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. There is no net movement of that ion across the membrane

    At the Nernst potential, the electrical force exactly balances the concentration force, so there is no net ion movement. For K+ it is about -94 mV and for Na+ about +61 mV in mammalian cells. The resting potential (-90 mV) sits near the K+ Nernst potential because K+ permeability dominates at rest.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 4: Transport of Substances Through Cell Membranes

  14. Voltage-gated ion channels open in response to:

    • A. Binding of a hormone
    • B. A change in membrane potential
    • C. An increase in temperature
    • D. Mechanical stretch always
    • E. Light exposure
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. An increase in temperature

    Voltage-gated channels contain charged voltage sensors that flip the gate open when the membrane depolarizes (or hyperpolarizes). Ligand-gated channels instead open when a chemical messenger binds. Voltage-gated Na+, K+, and Ca2+ channels generate action potentials; ligand-gated channels mediate synaptic transmission.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 4: Transport of Substances Through Cell Membranes

  15. Saturation of the transport rate at high solute concentrations is evidence of:

    • A. Simple diffusion
    • B. Carrier-mediated transport
    • C. Osmosis
    • D. Bulk flow
    • E. Electrical drift
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Carrier-mediated transport

    A finite number of carrier proteins means transport velocity plateaus (Vmax) at high substrate concentrations — Michaelis-Menten-like saturation kinetics. Simple diffusion never saturates because the lipid area is effectively unlimited. Saturation therefore proves a carrier is involved.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 4: Transport of Substances Through Cell Membranes

  16. Galactose competitively inhibits intestinal glucose absorption. This observation indicates:

    • A. Simple diffusion
    • B. A shared carrier protein
    • C. Osmosis
    • D. Gap junctions
    • E. Active extrusion of both
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. A shared carrier protein

    Competition means two substrates vie for the same binding site on one carrier — proof of a shared, specific transport protein. Galactose competes with glucose for intestinal SGLT1. Simple diffusion shows no competition because solutes cross the lipid independently.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 4: Transport of Substances Through Cell Membranes

  17. A poison that completely blocks the Na+/K+ ATPase would first cause:

    • A. Hyperpolarization and increased excitability
    • B. Intracellular Na+ accumulation, K+ loss, and membrane depolarization
    • C. Intracellular dehydration
    • D. Increased Ca2+ extrusion
    • E. Blockade of all diffusion
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Intracellular Na+ accumulation, K+ loss, and membrane depolarization

    Pump inhibition lets Na+ accumulate inside and K+ leak out, so the cell depolarizes toward zero and eventually cannot fire. In the heart this is exploited therapeutically (digoxin raises contractility via secondary Ca2+ gain), but overdose causes hyperkalemia and lethal arrhythmias — a favorite exam trap.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 4: Transport of Substances Through Cell Membranes

  18. Release of neurotransmitter by exocytosis at the nerve terminal requires:

    • A. Na+ influx into the terminal
    • B. Ca2+ influx into the presynaptic terminal
    • C. K+ efflux from the terminal
    • D. Cl- influx
    • E. ATP depletion
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Ca2+ influx into the presynaptic terminal

    Synaptic vesicle fusion is triggered when an arriving action potential opens presynaptic voltage-gated Ca2+ channels; the Ca2+ microdomain drives SNARE-mediated exocytosis. Botulinum toxin blocks this step (flaccid paralysis), while Lambert-Eaton antibodies reduce the Ca2+ channels. No Ca2+ influx means no transmitter release.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 4: Transport of Substances Through Cell Membranes

  19. The net electrochemical driving force on Na+ across a typical resting cell membrane is directed:

    • A. Outward, because the membrane is negative inside
    • B. Inward, driven by both the concentration and electrical gradients
    • C. Zero net, because the gradients cancel exactly
    • D. Outward, down the K+ gradient
    • E. Only during the refractory period
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Inward, driven by both the concentration and electrical gradients

    Extracellular Na+ is ~142 mEq/L versus ~14 inside (a 10-fold gradient inward), and the negative interior (-90 mV) also pulls the positive ion in — both forces drive Na+ inward. For K+ the forces oppose (gradient outward, voltage inward), nearly balancing at rest. This dual inward drive makes Na+ entry explosive once channels open.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 4: Transport of Substances Through Cell Membranes

  20. Which transporter moves glucose AGAINST its concentration gradient by coupling it to Na+ entry?

    • A. GLUT uniporters
    • B. SGLT Na+-glucose cotransporters
    • C. Aquaporins
    • D. Voltage-gated Na+ channels
    • E. Na+/K+ ATPase acting on glucose
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. SGLT Na+-glucose cotransporters

    SGLT cotransporters harness the downhill Na+ gradient to drag glucose uphill — secondary active transport. GLUT carriers only permit downhill facilitated diffusion. Renal SGLT2 is blocked by gliflozins to spill glucose into urine in diabetes treatment, a direct clinical application of this concept.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 4: Transport of Substances Through Cell Membranes

  21. A laboratory adds red blood cells to a solution that has the same total osmolarity as plasma but contains only urea, which freely crosses the red cell membrane. What will happen to the cells?

    • A. They will shrink and crenate
    • B. They will remain unchanged in volume
    • C. They will swell and may haemolyse
    • D. They will first shrink, then return to normal
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. They will swell and may haemolyse

    Tonicity depends on non-penetrating solutes, not total osmolarity. Urea penetrates the membrane, so the solution is effectively hypotonic and water enters the cells, causing swelling and possible haemolysis. A truly isotonic non-penetrating solution such as 0.9% NaCl would leave volume unchanged, while a hypertonic non-penetrating solution would crenate the cells.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 4: Transport of Substances Through Cell Membranes

  22. Which of the following changes would most increase the rate of simple diffusion of a lipid-soluble substance across a cell membrane?

    • A. Decreasing the membrane surface area
    • B. Increasing the thickness of the membrane
    • C. Increasing its lipid solubility and the membrane surface area
    • D. Decreasing the concentration gradient across the membrane
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Increasing its lipid solubility and the membrane surface area

    By Fick's law, diffusion rate rises with surface area, concentration gradient and lipid solubility, and falls with membrane thickness and molecular size. Thickening the membrane or reducing area or gradient would all slow diffusion, whereas greater lipid solubility lets the substance dissolve in and cross the bilayer faster.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 4: Transport of Substances Through Cell Membranes

  23. Cardiac glycosides such as ouabain inhibit the Na+-K+ ATPase pump. What is the most likely immediate consequence for a cell exposed to ouabain?

    • A. The cell gains Na+, loses K+ and tends to swell
    • B. The cell loses Na+ and gains K+
    • C. Intracellular Na+ falls sharply within seconds
    • D. The membrane becomes markedly hyperpolarised
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. The cell gains Na+, loses K+ and tends to swell

    The pump normally extrudes 3 Na+ and imports 2 K+ per ATP, keeping intracellular Na+ low and controlling cell volume. Blocking it lets Na+ accumulate inside, K+ leak out, and water follow Na+ osmotically, so the cell swells; the small electrogenic hyperpolarising contribution of the pump is also lost, not increased.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 4: Transport of Substances Through Cell Membranes

  24. Glucose uptake into skeletal muscle and adipose tissue is greatly increased by insulin. Which transport mechanism is responsible?

    • A. Simple diffusion through the lipid bilayer
    • B. Primary active transport by a glucose ATPase
    • C. Facilitated diffusion through GLUT4 transporters recruited to the membrane
    • D. Na+-glucose co-transport powered directly by ATP hydrolysis at the transporter
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Facilitated diffusion through GLUT4 transporters recruited to the membrane

    Insulin causes GLUT4 carriers to move to the cell membrane, increasing facilitated diffusion of glucose down its gradient without direct ATP use at the carrier. Simple diffusion is negligible for polar glucose, there is no glucose ATPase, and Na+-glucose co-transport is the mechanism in intestine and proximal tubule, not the insulin-sensitive tissues.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 4: Transport of Substances Through Cell Membranes

  25. A child with a defect in receptor-mediated endocytosis has very high plasma LDL cholesterol. Failure of which step best explains this finding?

    • A. LDL particles cannot be recognised because coated pits fail to internalise the LDL-receptor complex
    • B. The Golgi apparatus cannot synthesise cholesterol
    • C. Hepatocytes cannot excrete bile salts into the canaliculus
    • D. Intestinal cells cannot form chylomicrons from dietary fat
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. LDL particles cannot be recognised because coated pits fail to internalise the LDL-receptor complex

    Cells take up LDL when LDL binds its receptor in coated pits and the complex is internalised by receptor-mediated endocytosis for lysosomal breakdown. If internalisation fails, LDL stays in plasma and cholesterol remains high. The defect is in cellular uptake, not in cholesterol synthesis, bile excretion or intestinal chylomicron formation.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Transport of Substances Through Cell Membranes
