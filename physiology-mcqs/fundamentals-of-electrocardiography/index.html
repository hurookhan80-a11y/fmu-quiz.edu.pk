Fundamentals of Electrocardiography MCQs for FMU 1st Year MBBS Physiology

Home › Physiology MCQs › Fundamentals of Electrocardiography

Electrocardiography records the electrical activity of the heart from electrodes placed on the body surface. As the cardiac impulse spreads through the heart, it creates voltages that change over time, and the electrocardiograph draws these as waves. The main waves are named P, QRS, and T, and each reflects a stage of atrial or ventricular activity.

  1. On a normal ECG, the P wave represents:

    • A. Ventricular depolarization
    • B. AV nodal delay
    • C. Atrial depolarization
    • D. Atrial repolarization
    • E. Ventricular repolarization
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Atrial depolarization

    The P wave is the summed surface voltage of atrial depolarization spreading from the SA node across both atria. It precedes atrial contraction. Ventricular depolarization gives the QRS, ventricular repolarization the T wave; atrial repolarization is hidden inside the QRS.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 11: Fundamentals of Electrocardiography

  2. The normal PR interval (0.12-0.20 s) mainly reflects:

    • A. Atrial depolarization plus the AV nodal delay
    • B. SA node recovery time
    • C. Ventricular repolarization
    • D. Purkinje conduction time only
    • E. Ventricular muscle conduction time
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Atrial depolarization plus the AV nodal delay

    The PR interval spans atrial depolarization plus the time the impulse spends creeping through the AV node and His bundle. Its normal 0.12-0.20 s duration therefore mainly reflects the AV nodal delay; prolongation beyond 0.20 s defines first-degree AV block.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 11: Fundamentals of Electrocardiography

  3. The T wave of the ECG is normally upright (positive) in most leads because:

    • A. The SA node fires a second time
    • B. Depolarization spreads from epicardium to endocardium
    • C. Atrial repolarization adds to it
    • D. Purkinje fibers repolarize first
    • E. The epicardium repolarizes before the endocardium, reversing the repolarization vector
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: E. The epicardium repolarizes before the endocardium, reversing the repolarization vector

    Epicardial muscle has a shorter action potential than endocardial muscle, so despite depolarizing last, the epicardium repolarizes FIRST. The repolarization wave thus travels from epicardium to endocardium — away from a chest electrode — yet records upright because repolarization moving away from the positive electrode gives a positive deflection. This reversal is why T is normally upright.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 11: Fundamentals of Electrocardiography

  4. Einthoven's law states that:

    • A. aVR + aVL + aVF = 0
    • B. Lead II = Lead I + Lead III
    • C. V1 + V6 = 0
    • D. PR interval = QRS duration
    • E. Lead I + Lead II = Lead III
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Lead II = Lead I + Lead III

    Einthoven's law follows from the triangle geometry: at any instant, the voltage in lead II equals the sum of leads I and III. It is a useful check — if the three limb-lead voltages don't satisfy this relation, an electrode is misplaced or the tracing is mislabeled.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 11: Fundamentals of Electrocardiography

  5. In lead II, the positive electrode is placed on the:

    • A. Chest wall
    • B. Right leg (ground)
    • C. Right arm
    • D. Left leg
    • E. Left arm
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Left leg

    Lead II is the classic rhythm strip lead: negative electrode on the right arm, positive on the left leg, so its axis runs parallel to the heart's normal electrical axis (down and left). P waves and QRS complexes are therefore usually upright and prominent in lead II.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 11: Fundamentals of Electrocardiography

  6. Atrial repolarization (Ta wave) is usually invisible on the ECG because:

    • A. The T wave cancels it out
    • B. Atria do not repolarize
    • C. It occurs during the QRS complex and is buried within it
    • D. Repolarization is too slow to record
    • E. Limb leads cannot detect atrial activity
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. It occurs during the QRS complex and is buried within it

    Atrial repolarization does occur and would inscribe a Ta wave, but it falls during the much larger QRS complex generated by ventricular depolarization at the same time. The tiny atrial signal is completely buried — a reminder that the ECG shows only net surface voltages, not every event.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 11: Fundamentals of Electrocardiography

  7. A normal QRS duration is:

    • A. 0.20-0.30 s
    • B. Greater than 0.20 s
    • C. 0.12-0.20 s
    • D. Equal to the PR interval
    • E. Less than 0.12 s (typically 0.06-0.10 s)
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: E. Less than 0.12 s (typically 0.06-0.10 s)

    Rapid Purkinje distribution depolarizes both ventricles in 0.06-0.10 s, so the QRS is narrow. A QRS of 0.12 s or more means the impulse is spreading slowly muscle-to-muscle (as in bundle branch block or a ventricular ectopic focus) instead of via the fast conduction system.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 11: Fundamentals of Electrocardiography

  8. The QT interval represents:

    • A. The vulnerable period only
    • B. Atrial systole
    • C. AV nodal conduction time
    • D. Atrial repolarization
    • E. Ventricular depolarization and repolarization (electrical systole of the ventricles)
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: E. Ventricular depolarization and repolarization (electrical systole of the ventricles)

    The QT interval spans the QRS (depolarization) plus the ST segment and T wave (repolarization) — the total time the ventricles are electrically active, i.e., electrical systole. Prolongation (drugs, hypokalemia, congenital) predisposes to torsades de pointes.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 11: Fundamentals of Electrocardiography

  9. The ST segment corresponds to:

    • A. Rapid ventricular filling
    • B. SA node firing
    • C. The plateau phase, when the entire ventricular muscle is depolarized (normally isoelectric)
    • D. Atrial depolarization
    • E. Purkinje fiber repolarization
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. The plateau phase, when the entire ventricular muscle is depolarized (normally isoelectric)

    During the ST segment the entire ventricular mass is depolarized (the plateau phase of the action potentials), so no voltage differences exist between regions and the trace is flat at baseline. ST elevation or depression therefore signals abnormal currents — injury or ischemia — during this normally silent interval.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 11: Fundamentals of Electrocardiography

  10. With standard ECG calibration (25 mm/s, 1 mV = 10 mm), one small (1 mm) square represents:

    • A. 0.12 s
    • B. 0.04 s
    • C. 0.01 s
    • D. 0.20 s
    • E. 1.0 mV
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. 0.04 s

    Standard calibration runs paper at 25 mm/s, so 1 mm (one small square) = 0.04 s, and 1 mV deflects 10 mm (two large squares). These constants let you measure intervals (e.g., PR 3-5 small squares) and voltages directly from the grid.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 11: Fundamentals of Electrocardiography

  11. The normal mean electrical axis of the QRS lies between:

    • A. +100° to +180°
    • B. 0° to -90°
    • C. -90° to -30°
    • D. -30° to +100°
    • E. +90° to +270°
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. -30° to +100°

    The average QRS vector points down and left at about +59°, with a normal range of −30° to +100°. Beyond −30° is left axis deviation (e.g., LVH); beyond +100° is right axis deviation (e.g., RVH). Axis is read from the net QRS deflections in leads I and aVF.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 11: Fundamentals of Electrocardiography

  12. A depolarization wave traveling toward the positive electrode of a lead records:

    • A. An upward (positive) deflection
    • B. A downward deflection
    • C. Always a biphasic complex
    • D. No deflection
    • E. An inverted T wave
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. An upward (positive) deflection

    This is the fundamental rule of electrocardiography: a wave of depolarization approaching the positive electrode inscribes an upward deflection; moving away gives a downward one. Repolarization follows the opposite convention, which is why the T wave can be upright even though repolarization spreads away from chest leads.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 11: Fundamentals of Electrocardiography

  13. Chest lead V1 is positioned at the:

    • A. Left lower leg
    • B. 4th intercostal space, left sternal border
    • C. 2nd intercostal space, right side
    • D. Mid-axillary line
    • E. 4th intercostal space, right sternal border
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: E. 4th intercostal space, right sternal border

    V1 sits in the 4th intercostal space at the right sternal edge, overlying the right ventricle and septum — hence its normal rS pattern and why right-sided events (RBBB, RVH, anterior MI) show best in V1-V2. V2 mirrors it on the left sternal border.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 11: Fundamentals of Electrocardiography

  14. Lead aVR normally shows:

    • A. The tallest R wave of any lead
    • B. No waveforms at all
    • C. Predominantly negative deflections because it views the heart from the right shoulder
    • D. Upright P, QRS and T waves
    • E. ST elevation in acute MI
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Predominantly negative deflections because it views the heart from the right shoulder

    aVR's positive electrode is on the right shoulder, so it views the heart from above-right — looking into the ventricular cavities rather than along the depolarization path. Normal P, QRS, and T are therefore negative in aVR; a positive QRS in aVR suggests dextrocardia or lead reversal.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 11: Fundamentals of Electrocardiography

  15. The J point on the ECG marks:

    • A. The end of the T wave
    • B. The start of atrial systole
    • C. The start of the P wave
    • D. The peak of the T wave
    • E. The junction where the QRS ends and the ST segment begins
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: E. The junction where the QRS ends and the ST segment begins

    The J point is where the QRS complex ends and the ST segment begins — the exact junction of depolarization and repolarization. ST elevation or depression is measured relative to the baseline at (or just after) this point, making it the landmark for diagnosing injury currents.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 11: Fundamentals of Electrocardiography

  16. If lead I shows a predominantly positive QRS and lead aVF a predominantly negative QRS, the mean QRS axis is directed:

    • A. Leftward (left axis deviation)
    • B. Rightward
    • C. Posteriorly
    • D. Anteriorly
    • E. Inferiorly
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Leftward (left axis deviation)

    Lead I points left (0°) and aVF points down (+90°). A positive lead I with negative aVF places the vector in the upper-left quadrant (0° to −90°) — left axis deviation, seen in left ventricular hypertrophy, left anterior fascicular block, or inferior MI.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 11: Fundamentals of Electrocardiography

  17. The PR segment (between the end of the P wave and the start of the QRS) is normally:

    • A. Absent in normal hearts
    • B. Depressed by 2 mm
    • C. Elevated above baseline
    • D. Identical to the ST segment
    • E. Isoelectric, reflecting slow AV nodal conduction with no net surface voltage change
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: E. Isoelectric, reflecting slow AV nodal conduction with no net surface voltage change

    Between the P wave and QRS, the impulse is traversing the AV node — a slow, small-scale event generating no detectable surface voltage, so the trace stays flat at baseline. (In pericarditis this segment can depress, a useful diagnostic clue.)

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 11: Fundamentals of Electrocardiography

  18. Which statement about bipolar limb leads is correct?

    • A. A chest electrode against the right leg
    • B. The voltage difference between two exploring electrodes on the body
    • C. The same signal as unipolar leads
    • D. An exploring electrode against an absolute zero
    • E. Nothing — they are obsolete
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. The voltage difference between two exploring electrodes on the body

    Bipolar leads (I, II, III) record the potential difference between two exploring limb electrodes, with neither being a true zero. Unipolar leads (aVR, aVL, aVF, V1-V6) compare one exploring electrode against a constructed zero reference (Wilson central terminal).

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 11: Fundamentals of Electrocardiography

  19. A 25-year-old man has no P waves before wide, bizarre QRS complexes occurring regularly at 150/min. This rhythm most likely originates in the:

    • A. Pulmonary veins
    • B. Ventricles
    • C. SA node
    • D. AV node with normal conduction
    • E. Atria
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Ventricles

    No P waves with a rapid, regular, wide bizarre QRS points to a ventricular origin — the impulse starts in ventricular muscle and spreads slowly cell-to-cell. A supraventricular rhythm with aberrant conduction would usually still show preceding atrial activity; ventricular tachycardia must be assumed until proven otherwise.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 11: Fundamentals of Electrocardiography

  20. The small q wave sometimes seen in left-sided leads (I, V5-V6) represents:

    • A. A ventricular aneurysm
    • B. Atrial activity
    • C. Pathologic infarction
    • D. An electrode artifact
    • E. Normal septal depolarization from left to right
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: E. Normal septal depolarization from left to right

    The septum depolarizes first, from the left bundle side toward the right — i.e., left-to-right, away from left-sided leads. This inscribes a small normal septal q in I, aVL, V5-V6. Such q waves are narrow (<0.04 s) and small, unlike the broad deep Q of infarction.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 11: Fundamentals of Electrocardiography
