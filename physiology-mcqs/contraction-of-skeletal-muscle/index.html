Contraction of Skeletal Muscle MCQs for FMU 1st Year MBBS Physiology

Home › Physiology MCQs › Contraction of Skeletal Muscle

Skeletal muscle contracts when a nerve impulse reaches the muscle fibre and triggers the release of calcium inside the cell. Calcium allows the thick myosin filaments to bind the thin actin filaments and pull them, shortening each sarcomere. Because sarcomeres are arranged in series along the fibre, the whole muscle shortens and produces force.

  1. The basic contractile unit of skeletal muscle is the:

    • A. Muscle fascicle
    • B. Sarcomere (Z disc to Z disc)
    • C. Myofibril
    • D. Motor unit
    • E. Muscle fiber
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Sarcomere (Z disc to Z disc)

    The sarcomere — the segment between two successive Z discs — is the smallest contractile unit; shortening of all sarcomeres in series shortens the whole fiber. Within it, thick myosin and thin actin filaments slide past each other. A bands stay constant in length while I bands shorten during contraction.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 6: Contraction of Skeletal Muscle

  2. The A band of the sarcomere contains:

    • A. Thin filaments only
    • B. Thick myosin filaments along their whole length
    • C. Z discs
    • D. T-tubules
    • E. Sarcoplasmic reticulum
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Thick myosin filaments along their whole length

    The dark A band spans the full length of the thick (myosin) filaments, including the central overlap zone where thin filaments interdigitate. Because thick filament length never changes, the A band stays constant during contraction — a classic exam fact. I bands (actin only) and H zones shrink as filaments slide.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 6: Contraction of Skeletal Muscle

  3. The light I band of the sarcomere contains:

    • A. Thick and thin filaments
    • B. Thin actin filaments only
    • C. Thick filaments only
    • D. Titin only
    • E. Myosin heads
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Thin actin filaments only

    I bands flank the Z discs and hold only thin actin filaments, so they appear light. During contraction the thin filaments slide inward, narrowing the I bands (and the H zone), while the A band is unchanged. Measuring band changes under the microscope demonstrates the sliding-filament theory.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 6: Contraction of Skeletal Muscle

  4. During excitation-contraction coupling, released Ca2+ binds to which thin-filament protein to initiate contraction?

    • A. Tropomyosin
    • B. Troponin C
    • C. Troponin T
    • D. Actin
    • E. Myosin light chain
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Troponin C

    Troponin C is the Ca2+ sensor: when cytosolic Ca2+ rises, it binds troponin C, which shifts tropomyosin away from actin's myosin-binding sites so cross-bridges can form. Troponin I inhibits actin-myosin interaction at rest, and troponin T anchors the complex to tropomyosin. This is the molecular switch of contraction.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 6: Contraction of Skeletal Muscle

  5. In resting skeletal muscle, cross-bridge formation is prevented because:

    • A. Myosin heads are detached by ATP
    • B. Tropomyosin covers the myosin-binding sites on actin
    • C. The SR sequesters all ATP
    • D. Z discs dissolve
    • E. Titin stiffens
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Tropomyosin covers the myosin-binding sites on actin

    At rest, tropomyosin strands lie across the myosin-binding sites on actin, physically blocking attachment. Ca2+-troponin C interaction drags tropomyosin aside, exposing the sites. This steric blocking model explains why resting muscle is relaxed despite myosin heads being 'cocked' and ready.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 6: Contraction of Skeletal Muscle

  6. The power stroke of the cross-bridge cycle is triggered by:

    • A. Release of Ca2+ from the SR
    • B. Release of ADP and Pi from the myosin head, pivoting it against actin
    • C. Binding of ATP to actin
    • D. Phosphorylation of troponin
    • E. Opening of T-tubule Ca2+ channels
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Release of ADP and Pi from the myosin head, pivoting it against actin

    After the myosin head binds actin, release of ADP and inorganic phosphate drives the head's pivoting pull — the power stroke — dragging the thin filament ~10 nm toward the sarcomere center. ATP then binds the head to detach it, and ATP hydrolysis re-cocks the head for the next cycle. Each cycle shortens the sarcomere slightly; thousands sum to visible contraction.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 6: Contraction of Skeletal Muscle

  7. Which statement about ATP in the cross-bridge cycle is correct?

    • A. It powers only the power stroke
    • B. Its binding detaches the cross-bridge and its hydrolysis re-cocks the myosin head
    • C. It binds troponin C
    • D. It opens the ryanodine receptor
    • E. It contracts titin
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Its binding detaches the cross-bridge and its hydrolysis re-cocks the myosin head

    ATP has two jobs per cycle: its binding detaches the myosin head from actin after the power stroke, and its hydrolysis re-energizes (cocks) the head. Without ATP, heads lock onto actin in rigor. This is why muscle needs ATP even to relax — a paradox students must master.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 6: Contraction of Skeletal Muscle

  8. Rigor mortis (postmortem muscle stiffening) occurs because:

    • A. Sustained Ca2+ release
    • B. Excessive troponin phosphorylation
    • C. Depletion of ATP, preventing cross-bridge detachment
    • D. T-tubule rupture
    • E. Myosin denaturation
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Depletion of ATP, preventing cross-bridge detachment

    After death, ATP synthesis stops; existing ATP is consumed, so myosin heads cannot detach from actin and the muscles lock stiff. Rigor develops in hours and later resolves as proteases degrade the filaments. It is the in-vivo proof that ATP is required for cross-bridge detachment.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 6: Contraction of Skeletal Muscle

  9. In skeletal muscle, the signal passes from the T-tubule to the sarcoplasmic reticulum by:

    • A. Direct Ca2+ entry through T-tubules
    • B. DHP voltage sensors opening ryanodine-receptor Ca2+ channels of the SR
    • C. IP3 diffusing from the surface
    • D. Na+ entering the SR
    • E. K+ leaving the myofibrils
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. DHP voltage sensors opening ryanodine-receptor Ca2+ channels of the SR

    The T-tubule action potential is sensed by DHP receptors (voltage sensors) that mechanically open ryanodine receptors in the adjacent SR terminal cisternae, flooding the cytosol with Ca2+. This is excitation-contraction coupling — converting the electrical signal into the Ca2+ signal that moves tropomyosin.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 6: Contraction of Skeletal Muscle

  10. The 'triad' of skeletal muscle consists of:

    • A. Two T-tubules and one cisterna
    • B. One T-tubule flanked by two terminal cisternae of the SR
    • C. Three parallel myofibrils
    • D. Tropomyosin, troponin, and actin
    • E. Three Z discs
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. One T-tubule flanked by two terminal cisternae of the SR

    The triad — one central T-tubule flanked by two terminal cisternae of the SR — places the voltage sensor within nanometers of the Ca2+ release channels at every sarcomere boundary. This architecture gives skeletal muscle its famously rapid, synchronous activation. Cardiac muscle has diads instead.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 6: Contraction of Skeletal Muscle

  11. During an isometric contraction:

    • A. The muscle shortens with constant tension
    • B. Muscle length stays constant while tension rises
    • C. No ATP is consumed
    • D. Cross-bridges do not cycle
    • E. The load exceeds maximal tension
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Muscle length stays constant while tension rises

    In isometric contraction the muscle develops tension without shortening (e.g., pushing against a wall), so all cross-bridge energy appears as force. In isotonic contraction the load is constant and the muscle shortens. Most real movements mix both phases.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 6: Contraction of Skeletal Muscle

  12. Maximal active tension in skeletal muscle develops at a sarcomere length of about:

    • A. 1.0 um
    • B. 2.0-2.2 um
    • C. 3.5 um
    • D. 4.0 um
    • E. Length does not affect tension
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. 2.0-2.2 um

    At 2.0-2.2 um the thick and thin filaments overlap optimally, maximizing cross-bridge formation. Overstretching reduces overlap; excessive shortening lets thin filaments collide and crumple. The length-tension curve is the macroscopic result of this microscopic geometry and explains the Frank-Starling mechanism in the heart.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 6: Contraction of Skeletal Muscle

  13. A motor unit is defined as:

    • A. One muscle fiber and its sarcomeres
    • B. One motor neuron and all the muscle fibers it innervates
    • C. One sarcomere and its filaments
    • D. The NMJ plus the T-tubule
    • E. All fibers of one fascicle
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. One motor neuron and all the muscle fibers it innervates

    One alpha motor neuron plus every fiber it supplies (from a handful in eye muscles to thousands in the gastrocnemius) is a motor unit — the smallest functional unit of neural control. Recruitment of more units gives multiple-fiber summation; faster firing gives frequency summation. Fine muscles have tiny motor units for precision.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 6: Contraction of Skeletal Muscle

  14. Tetanization of skeletal muscle results from:

    • A. Summation of different motor units only
    • B. Fusion of successive twitches at high stimulation frequency into sustained contraction
    • C. Staircase increase in resting tension
    • D. Fatigue from ATP loss
    • E. Rigor from Ca2+ leak
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Fusion of successive twitches at high stimulation frequency into sustained contraction

    When stimuli arrive faster than the twitch can relax, twitches summate; at high frequency they fuse into smooth, maximal tetanus. This frequency summation lets the nervous system grade force finely. Unfused (incomplete) tetanus shows ripples; fused tetanus is smooth.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 6: Contraction of Skeletal Muscle

  15. The most immediate (first few seconds) source of ATP for contracting muscle is:

    • A. Oxidative phosphorylation
    • B. Phosphocreatine breakdown
    • C. Anaerobic glycolysis
    • D. Fatty acid oxidation
    • E. Protein catabolism
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Phosphocreatine breakdown

    Phosphocreatine donates its phosphate to ADP in one enzymatic step, restoring ATP within milliseconds — but the store lasts only ~10 seconds of maximal effort. Glycolysis then bridges minutes, and oxidative phosphorylation sustains prolonged activity. Creatine kinase is the enzyme, and its MB isoform marks cardiac injury.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 6: Contraction of Skeletal Muscle

  16. Slow-twitch (type I) skeletal muscle fibers are best described as:

    • A. Pale, powerful, fatigue-resistant
    • B. Red, mitochondria-rich, fatigue-resistant, suited for posture
    • C. Fast, glycolytic, easily fatigued
    • D. Large motor units only
    • E. Anaerobic only
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Red, mitochondria-rich, fatigue-resistant, suited for posture

    Type I (slow oxidative) fibers are red with myoglobin, rich in mitochondria, and built for endurance posture — they resist fatigue. Type II (fast glycolytic) fibers are pale, powerful, and fatigue quickly. Sprinters favor type II; marathoners type I. Training can shift fiber properties modestly.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 6: Contraction of Skeletal Muscle

  17. The SERCA pump of the sarcoplasmic reticulum:

    • A. Releases Ca2+ into the cytosol
    • B. Pumps Ca2+ back into the SR, causing relaxation
    • C. Opens the ryanodine receptor
    • D. Binds troponin C
    • E. Generates the end plate potential
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Pumps Ca2+ back into the SR, causing relaxation

    SERCA actively pumps cytosolic Ca2+ back into the SR, dropping Ca2+ below the troponin threshold so tropomyosin re-covers actin's binding sites — relaxation. The pump burns ATP continuously during and after contraction. Its failure would leave muscle locked in contracture.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 6: Contraction of Skeletal Muscle

  18. A young patient under general anesthesia develops rapidly rising temperature, muscle rigidity, tachycardia, and severe metabolic acidosis. The most likely diagnosis and its mechanism is:

    • A. Myasthenia gravis
    • B. Botulism
    • C. Malignant hyperthermia from ryanodine-receptor Ca2+ leak
    • D. Lambert-Eaton syndrome
    • E. Periodic paralysis
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Malignant hyperthermia from ryanodine-receptor Ca2+ leak

    Malignant hyperthermia comes from mutant ryanodine receptors that dump excessive Ca2+ when triggered by volatile anesthetics or succinylcholine — runaway cross-bridge cycling generates heat, CO2, acidosis, and rigidity. Dantrolene, which blocks SR Ca2+ release, is lifesaving. Family screening prevents anesthesia catastrophes.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 6: Contraction of Skeletal Muscle

  19. The correct sequence of excitation-contraction coupling in skeletal muscle is:

    • A. Ca2+ release, then the action potential
    • B. Action potential, T-tubule spread, SR Ca2+ release, cross-bridge cycling
    • C. Cross-bridge cycling, then Ca2+ release
    • D. Tropomyosin shift before Ca2+ release
    • E. ATP binding before the action potential
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Action potential, T-tubule spread, SR Ca2+ release, cross-bridge cycling

    The steps run: motor neuron AP -> ACh release -> end plate potential -> muscle AP -> T-tubule spread -> DHP/ryanodine Ca2+ release -> Ca2+-troponin C binding -> tropomyosin shift -> cross-bridge cycling -> shortening. Any exam answer missing Ca2+ or the T-tubule is incomplete.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 6: Contraction of Skeletal Muscle

  20. The staircase (treppe) phenomenon — successive identical stimuli producing progressively stronger twitches at first — is due to:

    • A. Recruitment of more motor units
    • B. Improved Ca2+ handling over the first few twitches, increasing twitch strength
    • C. Tetanus
    • D. Fatigue
    • E. Length-tension changes
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Improved Ca2+ handling over the first few twitches, increasing twitch strength

    Treppe is the warm-up phenomenon: Ca2+ delivery and myosin phosphorylation improve over the first few twitches, so each contraction is slightly stronger. It reflects better EC coupling, not more motor units. Athletes warm up partly to exploit it.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 6: Contraction of Skeletal Muscle

  21. During recruitment of motor units for a gradually increasing voluntary contraction, which motor units are normally activated first?

    • A. Small, slow, fatigue-resistant motor units
    • B. Large, fast, fatigable motor units
    • C. All motor units simultaneously, regardless of size
    • D. Only motor units supplying white (type IIb) fibres
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Small, slow, fatigue-resistant motor units

    This is the size principle: small motor neurons have lower thresholds and are recruited first, giving finely graded, fatigue-resistant force; larger fast units join only for stronger contractions. Recruiting large fatigable units first or all units at once would waste energy and lose fine control.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 6: Contraction of Skeletal Muscle

  22. A muscle spindle is stretched passively. Which structure primarily detects the change in muscle length and initiates the stretch reflex?

    • A. Intrafusal fibres whose sensory endings discharge with stretch
    • B. Golgi tendon organ in series with extrafusal fibres
    • C. Free nerve endings responding to muscle metabolites
    • D. Pacinian corpuscles in the joint capsule
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Intrafusal fibres whose sensory endings discharge with stretch

    Muscle spindles lie in parallel with extrafusal fibres; stretch of their intrafusal fibres excites Ia and II afferents that monosynaptically excite alpha motor neurons. Golgi tendon organs are in series and sense tension, not length, while joint and free endings do not drive the classic stretch reflex.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 6: Contraction of Skeletal Muscle

  23. A skeletal muscle is stimulated repeatedly at a frequency so high that no relaxation occurs between stimuli and tension reaches a smooth maximum plateau. This state is called:

    • A. Treppe (staircase phenomenon)
    • B. Wave summation without fusion
    • C. Complete tetanus
    • D. A single twitch with prolonged latent period
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Complete tetanus

    At high stimulation frequencies, successive twitches fuse because the muscle cannot relax, producing sustained maximal tension — complete tetanus. Treppe is the stepwise increase in tension of early twitches at low frequency, and unfused summation still shows partial relaxation between stimuli.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 6: Contraction of Skeletal Muscle

  24. Which fibre type is most characteristic of postural muscles that must contract for long periods without fatigue?

    • A. Slow oxidative (type I) fibres rich in myoglobin and mitochondria
    • B. Fast glycolytic fibres with few mitochondria
    • C. Fast oxidative fibres found only in extraocular muscles
    • D. Fibres that depend entirely on stored ATP for energy
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Slow oxidative (type I) fibres rich in myoglobin and mitochondria

    Type I slow oxidative fibres have abundant mitochondria, myoglobin and capillaries and resist fatigue, suiting them to posture. Fast glycolytic fibres fatigue quickly despite powerful brief contractions, and no fibre can work for long on stored ATP alone, which lasts only a few seconds.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 6: Contraction of Skeletal Muscle
