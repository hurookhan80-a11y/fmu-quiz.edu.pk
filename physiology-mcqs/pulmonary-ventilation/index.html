Pulmonary Ventilation MCQs for FMU 1st Year MBBS Physiology

Home › Physiology MCQs › Pulmonary Ventilation

Pulmonary ventilation is the process of moving air into and out of the lungs. Inspiration is active: the diaphragm contracts and moves down while the rib cage expands, lowering pressure inside the chest so air flows in. Expiration at rest is mainly passive, as the lungs and chest wall recoil and push air out.

  1. At the end of inspiration, when there is momentarily no airflow, intra-alveolar (intrapulmonary) pressure is:

    • A. 0 mmHg (equal to atmospheric pressure)
    • B. -10 mmHg
    • C. +10 mmHg
    • D. -30 mmHg
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. 0 mmHg (equal to atmospheric pressure)

    During inspiration alveolar pressure falls to about -1 mmHg to draw air in, then returns to 0 (atmospheric) at end-inspiration when airflow stops. Large negative values describe intrapleural pressure changes in forced maneuvers, not quiet end-inspiration.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Pulmonary Ventilation

  2. Intrapulmonary/intrapleural pressure becomes more negative during:

    • A. Expiration than inspiration
    • B. Both equally at all times
    • C. Only during coughing
    • D. Inspiration than expiration
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Inspiration than expiration

    As the chest expands in inspiration, intrapleural pressure falls (becomes more negative, e.g. -5 to -7.5 cmH2O), drawing air in. During expiration it returns toward -5 cmH2O.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Pulmonary Ventilation

  3. A student breathes with a tidal volume of 500 ml at 12 breaths/min. If his anatomical dead space is 150 ml, what is his alveolar ventilation?

    • A. 6000 ml/min
    • B. 7500 ml/min
    • C. 1800 ml/min
    • D. 4200 ml/min
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. 4200 ml/min

    Alveolar ventilation equals (tidal volume minus dead space) multiplied by respiratory rate: (500-150) x 12 = 4200 ml/min. Multiplying tidal volume alone by rate gives total minute ventilation (6000 ml/min), which overstates the air that actually reaches the alveoli for gas exchange.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 38: Pulmonary Ventilation

  4. Which lung volume cannot be measured directly by simple spirometry?

    • A. Tidal volume
    • B. Inspiratory reserve volume
    • C. Expiratory reserve volume
    • D. Residual volume
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Residual volume

    Residual volume is the air remaining after maximal expiration and never leaves the lungs, so a spirometer that measures only moving air cannot record it; it is derived from functional residual capacity measured by helium dilution or plethysmography. Tidal and reserve volumes are all recorded directly from spirometer tracings.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 38: Pulmonary Ventilation

  5. According to the law of Laplace, a small alveolus lined with surfactant tends to remain open because surfactant:

    • A. Increases surface tension more in small alveoli than in large ones
    • B. Prevents any gas from leaving the alveolus
    • C. Has no effect on surface tension but stiffens the alveolar wall
    • D. Lowers surface tension, and lowers it more as the alveolus gets smaller and surfactant concentrates
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Lowers surface tension, and lowers it more as the alveolus gets smaller and surfactant concentrates

    Surfactant reduces surface tension, and as an alveolus shrinks its surfactant film concentrates, reducing tension further and preventing the high collapsing pressure that Laplace's law (pressure proportional to tension divided by radius) would otherwise predict. Without this effect, small alveoli would empty into larger ones and collapse.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 38: Pulmonary Ventilation

  6. Quiet expiration at rest is largely a passive process. This is mainly because:

    • A. Expiratory muscles contract powerfully during every resting breath
    • B. The diaphragm contracts further to push air out
    • C. Surfactant actively pumps air out of the alveoli
    • D. Elastic recoil of the lungs and chest wall returns the system to its resting volume when inspiratory muscles relax
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Elastic recoil of the lungs and chest wall returns the system to its resting volume when inspiratory muscles relax

    Inspiration stretches elastic tissues; when the diaphragm and inspiratory muscles relax, stored elastic recoil deflates the lungs without muscular effort. Forceful expiratory muscle contraction is needed only for active expiration, as in exercise or coughing, and the diaphragm relaxes rather than contracts during expiration.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 38: Pulmonary Ventilation

  7. A patient with asthma has severe airway narrowing. Airway resistance is increased most dramatically because resistance varies with:

    • A. The first power of airway length only
    • B. Alveolar surfactant concentration
    • C. Blood haemoglobin concentration
    • D. The inverse fourth power of airway radius
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. The inverse fourth power of airway radius

    As in Poiseuille's law, airway resistance is inversely proportional to the fourth power of radius, so modest bronchoconstriction causes a large rise in resistance and work of breathing. Bronchodilators that relax airway smooth muscle help precisely because small increases in radius greatly reduce resistance.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 38: Pulmonary Ventilation

  8. Intrapleural pressure at the end of a quiet expiration is normally about -5 cm H2O (subatmospheric). The main significance of this negative pressure is that it:

    • A. Pushes the lungs tightly against the chest wall so they follow chest expansion and do not collapse
    • B. Forces air into the alveoli during expiration
    • C. Is produced by contraction of the pleural muscles
    • D. Prevents blood flow through the pulmonary capillaries
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Pushes the lungs tightly against the chest wall so they follow chest expansion and do not collapse

    Opposing elastic recoil of lung (inward) and chest wall (outward) makes pleural pressure negative, coupling the lungs to the chest wall so ventilation can occur and transpulmonary pressure keeps alveoli open. Loss of this pressure in pneumothorax lets the lung collapse; there are no pleural muscles generating it.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 38: Pulmonary Ventilation
