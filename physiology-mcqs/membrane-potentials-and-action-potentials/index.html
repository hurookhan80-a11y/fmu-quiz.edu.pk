Membrane Potentials and Action Potentials MCQs for FMU 1st Year MBBS Physiology

Home › Physiology MCQs › Membrane Potentials and Action Potentials

All living cells maintain a voltage difference across their membranes, called the membrane potential, because ions are distributed unequally inside and outside the cell. In nerve and muscle cells, a stimulus can trigger a rapid, brief reversal of this voltage called an action potential. Action potentials travel along nerve fibres and muscle membranes and carry signals through the body.

  1. Aqua porins permit rapid passage of water through cell membrane but the pores are too narrow to permit passage of;

    • A. Dehydrated potassium
    • B. Lipid soluble substances
    • C. Hydrated ions
    • D. Crystalloid subtances
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Hydrated ions

    Aquaporins are narrow water channels that let water pass in single file but exclude hydrated ions, because ions carry a shell of water molecules too large to enter the pore and are stripped only at high energy cost. Lipid-soluble substances cross mainly through the lipid bilayer itself, not aquaporins.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 5: Membrane Potentials and Action Potentials

  2. A local anesthetic is applied to a nerve fiber, reducing Na+ channel opening probability. Conduction fails when:

    • A. Membrane potential becomes positive
    • B. Na+ influx exceeds K+ efflux
    • C. Safety factor for propagation falls below 1
    • D. K+ channels remain closed
    • E. Na+/K+ pump is inhibited
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Safety factor for propagation falls below 1

    Conduction continues only if each new action potential depolarises the next segment above threshold — the safety factor must exceed 1. Local anaesthetics reduce Na+ channel opening, so the depolarising current falls, the safety factor drops below 1, and propagation fails even though the membrane can still be depolarised locally.

  3. A myelinated axon can conduct impulses significantly faster than an unmyelinated axon of the same diameter due to:

    • A. Continuous activation of voltage-gated Na+ channels along the entire axon membrane
    • B. Decreased membrane time constant allowing rapid charge-discharge at internodes
    • C. Increased axial resistance facilitating faster local current spread
    • D. Reduced amplitude of action potential due to insulation by myelin
    • E. Increased leakage of ions at nodes of Ranvier to speed depolarization
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Decreased membrane time constant allowing rapid charge-discharge at internodes

    Myelin insulation lowers membrane capacitance and the time constant of the internodal membrane, so local currents charge the next node of Ranvier very rapidly and the action potential jumps node-to-node (saltatory conduction). Channels are concentrated at nodes, not continuously along the axon, and axial resistance is not increased to speed conduction.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 5: Membrane Potentials and Action Potentials

  4. A nerve fiber depolarized to a level close to threshold but no action potential is generated, the most likely explanation is:

    • A. Increased fast voltage-gated Na+ channel availability
    • B. Failure of impulse propagation due to Na+ channel inactivation
    • C. Temporal summation of subthreshold stimuli
    • D. Lowered firing threshold due to increased excitability
    • E. Enhanced local circuit currents causing facilitation
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Failure of impulse propagation due to Na+ channel inactivation

    If depolarisation is slow or held just near threshold, voltage-gated Na+ channels inactivate before a regenerative upstroke can occur (accommodation), so no action potential fires. A rapid rise to threshold would open Na+ channels faster than they inactivate and trigger firing.

  5. A neuron receives repeated subthreshold depolarizations reaching -70 mV but never -65 mV. Absence of action potential in this condition is due to:

    • B. Positive feedback cycle is not triggered
    • C. K+ channels are permanently open
    • D. Na+ gradient is insufficient
    • E. Membrane resistance is zero

    Answer: B. Positive feedback cycle is not triggered

    An action potential needs membrane potential to reach threshold (about -65 mV in this item) so that Na+ influx exceeds K+ efflux and the positive-feedback cycle starts. Depolarisations that stop at -70 mV remain subthreshold graded potentials, so no regenerative action potential is produced regardless of repetition.

  6. A patient develops severe hypocalcemia due to renal failure. Option best explaining the altered neuromuscular excitability is:

    • A. Increased Na+ channel activation threshold → decreased excitability
    • B. Decreased Na+ channel activation threshold → spontaneous firing
    • C. Increased K+ permeability → hyperpolarization
    • D. Decreased Ca2+ influx → membrane stabilization
    • E. Increased Na+ pump activity → depolarization
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Decreased Na+ channel activation threshold → spontaneous firing

    Extracellular Ca2+ normally stabilises the membrane by screening surface charge and keeping the Na+-channel activation threshold relatively high. In hypocalcaemia this stabilisation is lost, the threshold falls toward resting potential, and nerves fire spontaneously, causing paraesthesia, cramps and tetany.

  7. A peripheral nerve can conduct impulses repetitively for long periods without loss of response; underlying physiology is:

    • A. Action potentials do not significantly deplete cellular energy stores
    • B. Refractory periods allow complete metabolic recovery between impulses
    • C. High intracellular glycogen content sustains membrane excitability
    • D. Myelin sheath continuously regenerates ATP during conduction
    • E. Synaptic facilitation enhances repeated impulse transmission efficiency
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Action potentials do not significantly deplete cellular energy stores

    Each action potential involves only a minute exchange of Na+ and K+ across the membrane, so intracellular/extracellular ion concentrations and cellular energy stores are barely changed and the Na+-K+ pump easily restores them. A nerve can therefore conduct thousands of impulses without fatigue. Glycogen stores and myelin do not regenerate ATP for conduction.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 5: Membrane Potentials and Action Potentials

  8. A second action potential cannot occur immediately after the first as long as:

    • A. Na+ gradient disappears instantly
    • B. Na+ channels remain inactivated until repolarization
    • C. K+ channels permanently close
    • D. Pump stops working
    • E. Membrane becomes impermeable
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Na+ channels remain inactivated until repolarization

    During the absolute refractory period most voltage-gated Na+ channels are in the inactivated state and cannot reopen until the membrane repolarises, so no second action potential can be generated however strong the stimulus. The Na+ gradient and pump have not disappeared; it is channel inactivation that enforces the refractory period.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 5: Membrane Potentials and Action Potentials

  9. An action potential initiated at the midpoint of a nerve fiber propagates in both directions. The primary mechanism responsible is:

    • A. Active transport of Na+ along the axon
    • B. Local circuit currents depolarizing adjacent membrane
    • C. Direct opening of all Na+ channels simultaneously
    • D. Na+/K+ pump activation
    • E. Diffusion of K+ into adjacent regions
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Local circuit currents depolarizing adjacent membrane

    An action potential propagates because positive charge from the active region flows through the axoplasm as local circuit currents and depolarises the adjacent membrane to threshold on both sides of the initial site. In an unmyelinated fibre stimulated in the middle, the impulse therefore travels in both directions; the Na+-K+ pump maintains gradients but does not carry the impulse.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 5: Membrane Potentials and Action Potentials

  10. At the threshold potential (~-65 mV), which condition is critical for triggering an action potential?

    • A. Na+ efflux exceeds K+ influx
    • B. Na+ influx exceeds K+ efflux
    • C. K+ influx exceeds Na+ efflux
    • D. Equal Na+ and K+ movement
    • E. Only Na+ channels are closed
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Na+ influx exceeds K+ efflux

    Threshold is the membrane potential at which inward Na+ current just exceeds outward K+ current, so net inward current becomes self-reinforcing (positive feedback) and the upstroke of the action potential is triggered. Below this point K+ efflux and leak currents repolarise the membrane and only a local potential results.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 5: Membrane Potentials and Action Potentials

  11. Donnan's effect justifies lack of intracellular negatively charged proteins would effect RMP as:

    • A. RMP becomes more negative due to increased K+ efflux
    • B. RMP becomes less negative due to reduction in intracellular fixed anions
    • C. RMP remains unchanged because Na+/K+ ATPase is the primary determinant
    • D. RMP becomes more positive due to increased Na+ permeability
    • E. RMP becomes unstable due to spontaneous depolarizations
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. RMP becomes less negative due to reduction in intracellular fixed anions

    The resting membrane potential is made negative mainly by intracellular nondiffusible anions such as proteins and organic phosphates, which attract K+ but cannot leave the cell. If these fixed negative charges are lost, the interior becomes less negative and the RMP moves toward zero. The Na+/K+ ATPase contributes only a few millivolts directly, so loss of the Donnan anions depolarises rather than hyperpolarises the cell.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 5: Membrane Potentials and Action Potentials

  12. During an action potential upstroke, Na+ permeability increases rapidly, but Of Cl- is minimal. The negligible contribution of Cl- to this phase is due to:

    • A. Cl- ions are present in very low intracellular and extracellular concentrations
    • B. Cl- channels undergo rapid voltage-dependent activation similar to Na+ channels
    • C. Cl- permeability remains low and does not change significantly during the upstroke
    • D. Cl- ions are actively pumped out of the neuron during depolarization
    • E. Cl- equilibrium potential is identical to resting membrane potential, preventing flux
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Cl- permeability remains low and does not change significantly during the upstroke

    During the nerve action potential, the upstroke is almost entirely due to a rapid, transient increase in Na+ conductance followed by increased K+ conductance; Cl- conductance changes very little. Chloride therefore carries negligible current in this phase even though its equilibrium potential is near rest. It is not actively pumped during the spike, and low concentration alone does not explain the minimal role.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 5: Membrane Potentials and Action Potentials

  13. During high-frequency nerve stimulation, the progressive increase in heat production by the nerve fiber is due to:

    • A. Increased passive diffusion of Na+ during depolarization
    • B. Enhanced leakage of K+ through resting channels
    • C. Increased activity of the Na+/K+-ATPase secondary to intracellular Na+ accumulation
    • D. Increased Ca2+ influx through voltage-gated channels
    • E. Denaturation of membrane proteins due to repeated depolarization
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Increased activity of the Na+/K+-ATPase secondary to intracellular Na+ accumulation

    Each action potential allows a small Na+ influx and K+ efflux; with high-frequency firing these ions accumulate and the Na+/K+-ATPase must hydrolyse more ATP to restore the gradients. The ATP breakdown liberates heat, called recovery heat, which rises progressively with activity. Passive leak alone does not explain the activity-dependent increase.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 5: Membrane Potentials and Action Potentials

  14. Electrotonic conduction in excitable tissues fails to trigger a propagated spike at the axon hillock because of:

    • A. Regenerative propagation of action potentials through voltage-gated Na+ channel activation
    • B. Passive spread of local potential changes with progressive decrease in amplitude over distance
    • C. Rapid jumping of impulses between nodes of Ranvier in myelinated fibers
    • D. Energy-dependent transmission mediated by Na+/K+-ATPase activity
    • E. Active ion exchange across the membrane producing non-decremental signals
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Passive spread of local potential changes with progressive decrease in amplitude over distance

    Electrotonic (graded) potentials spread passively through cytoplasm and membrane but decay exponentially with distance because current leaks out across the membrane. By the time the signal reaches the axon hillock its amplitude may have fallen below threshold, so no regenerative spike is triggered. This decremental, passive spread is fundamentally different from non-decremental saltatory or continuous action potential propagation.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 5: Membrane Potentials and Action Potentials

  15. How RMP would be effected if there is reduced conductance of tandem pore domain K+ leak channels in a neuron without affecting Na+ permeability or Na+/K+ pump activity?

    • A. Hyperpolarization due to reduced K+ efflux
    • B. Depolarization due to decreased dominance of K+ permeability
    • C. No change because Na+ gradient remains constant
    • D. Initial depolarization followed by hyperpolarization due to pump compensation
    • E. Membrane potential equals K+ equilibrium potential
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Depolarization due to decreased dominance of K+ permeability

    Tandem-pore K+ leak channels provide the dominant resting K+ conductance that holds the membrane near the K+ equilibrium potential (about -90 mV). If their conductance falls, K+ influences the potential less and the relatively greater Na+ leak pulls the membrane toward the Na+ equilibrium potential, i.e. depolarisation. The cell does not hyperpolarise, because reduced K+ efflux retains positive charge inside.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 5: Membrane Potentials and Action Potentials

  16. If membrane permeability to Na+ increases such that P_K : P_Na becomes 10 : 1, what is the most likely effect on membrane potential?

    • A. Hyperpolarization toward K+ equilibrium potential
    • B. Depolarization toward Na+ equilibrium potential
    • C. No change due to dominant K+ gradient
    • D. Oscillatory membrane potential due to ion competition
    • E. Membrane potential becomes exactly zero
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Depolarization toward Na+ equilibrium potential

    The membrane potential follows the Goldman-Hodgkin-Katz equation as a weighted average of ionic equilibrium potentials. At rest PK:PNa is about 100:1 so the potential sits near EK; if the ratio falls to 10:1, Na+ permeability becomes relatively much more influential and the potential depolarises toward ENa (about +60 mV). It cannot become exactly zero unless permeabilities and concentrations make it so, and it moves away from, not toward, EK.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 5: Membrane Potentials and Action Potentials

  17. If the rheobase of a nerve increases due to an ionic change, Its excitability would change as:

    • A. Excitability increases because less current is needed to reach threshold
    • B. Excitability decreases because a stronger stimulus is required to reach threshold
    • C. Excitability remains unchanged because rheobase does not affect threshold
    • D. Excitability becomes infinite due to complete loss of accommodation
    • E. Excitability oscillates due to alternating channel activation
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Excitability decreases because a stronger stimulus is required to reach threshold

    Rheobase is the smallest current of long duration that can just excite a tissue. If rheobase rises, a stronger stimulus is required to reach threshold, which by definition means excitability has decreased. Excitability would increase only if rheobase fell and less current were needed.

  18. In hypocalcaemia, reduced extracellular calcium causes which physiological change?

    • A. Increased excitability of nerves and muscles due to easier activation of Na+ channels
    • B. Increased acetylcholine breakdown
    • C. Reduced receptor activation
    • D. Inhibition of acetylcholine channels
    • E. Decreased membrane excitability
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Increased excitability of nerves and muscles due to easier activation of Na+ channels

    Extracellular Ca2+ normally screens negative surface charges on the membrane and keeps voltage-gated Na+ channels relatively hard to open. When Ca2+ falls in hypocalcaemia, this screening is reduced, Na+ channels open at more negative potentials, and nerves and muscles become hyperexcitable. Clinically this produces paraesthesia, tetany, and positive Trousseau and Chvostek signs, not decreased excitability.

  19. Most negative Resting membrane potential is produced by this combination.

    • A. ↑P_K, ↓[K+]o
    • B. ↓P_K, ↑[Na+]o
    • C. ↑P_Na, ↓[Na+]i
    • D. ↓P_K, ↑[K+]i
    • E. ↑P_Na, ↑[Na+]o
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. ↑P_K, ↓[K+]o

    The most negative resting potential occurs when the membrane is highly permeable to K+ and the K+ equilibrium potential itself is very negative. Lowering extracellular K+ makes EK more negative by the Nernst equation, and high PK in the Goldman equation pulls the membrane potential close to that value. Increasing Na+ permeability or raising extracellular K+ would depolarise the cell.

  20. Myelin increases conduction velocity primarily by:

    • A. Increasing ion leakage
    • B. Increasing membrane resistance and decreasing capacitance
    • C. Activating voltage-gated calcium channels
    • D. Decreasing axon diameter
    • E. Blocking sodium influx
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Increasing membrane resistance and decreasing capacitance

    Myelin acts as an electrical insulator that increases transverse membrane resistance and decreases membrane capacitance. Charge therefore spreads further and faster down the internode, and the action potential is regenerated only at nodes of Ranvier (saltatory conduction), greatly increasing velocity. It does not work by increasing leak, reducing axon diameter, or blocking Na+ influx.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 5: Membrane Potentials and Action Potentials

  21. One of following condition can make Goldman equation reduce effectively to Nernst equation for K+.

    • A. P (Permeability)_Na = P_K
    • B. P_K >> P_Na
    • C. Na+ gradient absent
    • D. Pump inactive
    • E. Equal ion concentrations
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. P_K >> P_Na

    The Goldman equation simplifies to the Nernst equation for K+ when K+ permeability overwhelmingly dominates all other ion permeabilities (PK >> PNa, PCl). The potential then depends essentially only on the K+ concentration ratio. Equal Na+ and K+ permeabilities, an inactive pump, or equal concentrations would not produce this reduction.

  22. The All-or-None Law : nature of the action potential is correctly justified as:

    • A. Linear increase in Na+ permeability with voltage
    • B. Gradual activation of K+ channels
    • C. Regenerative Na+ channel opening once threshold is reached
    • D. Constant Na+/K+ pump activity
    • E. Equal permeability to all ions
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Regenerative Na+ channel opening once threshold is reached

    The all-or-none law exists because once threshold is reached, voltage-gated Na+ channel opening becomes a regenerative positive-feedback process that always drives the membrane to the same peak. A subthreshold stimulus produces no spike, while any suprathreshold stimulus produces a full-sized spike of fixed amplitude. The response is therefore not graded or linear with stimulus strength.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 5: Membrane Potentials and Action Potentials

  23. The fundamental property that distinguishes graded potentials from action potentials in excitable tissues is:

    • A. They obey the all-or-none law and are non-decremental
    • B. They propagate without loss of amplitude over long distances
    • C. Their amplitude varies in proportion to the strength of the stimulus
    • D. They are always associated with a refractory period
    • E. They are generated exclusively by voltage-gated Na+ channels
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Their amplitude varies in proportion to the strength of the stimulus

    Graded (local) potentials are distinguished by their variable amplitude: the stronger the stimulus, the larger the depolarisation or hyperpolarisation, and the signal decays with distance. Action potentials, by contrast, are all-or-none, non-decremental, and have a refractory period. Graded potentials can also summate temporally and spatially.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 5: Membrane Potentials and Action Potentials

  24. The ion channel events during an action potential is given in correct sequence:

    • A. K+ opens → Na+ opens → Na+ inactivates
    • B. Na+ opens → Na+ inactivates → K+ opens (delayed)
    • C. Na+ inactivates → K+ opens → Na+ opens
    • D. K+ opens → K+ closes → Na+ opens
    • E. Na+ opens → K+ closes → Na+ inactivates
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Na+ opens → Na+ inactivates → K+ opens (delayed)

    The correct sequence is: voltage-gated Na+ channels open rapidly causing the upstroke, then Na+ channels inactivate, and delayed voltage-gated K+ channels open to repolarise the membrane. K+ channels do not open before Na+ channels, and K+ efflux is responsible for repolarisation and the after-hyperpolarisation. This Hodgkin cycle underlies threshold, overshoot, and the refractory periods.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 5: Membrane Potentials and Action Potentials

  25. The ionic and electrochemical basis of Over Shoot phase in a rapidly depolarizing neuron is:

    • A. The membrane potential approaches the equilibrium potential of K+ due to increased K+ conductance
    • B. The membrane potential transiently approaches the equilibrium potential of Na+ due to a marked increase in Na+ permeability
    • C. Activation of the Na+/K+-ATPase leads to net positive charge influx into the cell
    • D. Increased Cl- influx shifts the membrane potential toward positive values
    • E. Decreased membrane resistance limits outward K+ current, causing depolarization
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. The membrane potential transiently approaches the equilibrium potential of Na+ due to a marked increase in Na+ permeability

    The overshoot occurs because Na+ permeability increases several-thousand-fold during the upstroke, so by the Goldman equation the membrane potential is pulled transiently toward the Na+ equilibrium potential (about +60 mV) and becomes positive inside. It does not approach EK, and the Na+/K+ pump and Cl- influx play no significant role in this rapid phase.

  26. The safety factor for Nerve conduction is best defined as:

    • A. Ratio of Na+ influx to K+ efflux
    • B. Ratio of membrane potential to equilibrium potential
    • C. Ratio of generated depolarization to threshold depolarization
    • D. Ratio of intracellular to extracellular Na+
    • E. Ratio of pump activity to ion leakage
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Ratio of generated depolarization to threshold depolarization

    The safety factor for conduction is the ratio of the depolarisation actually produced by an action potential in the next segment to the depolarisation required to reach threshold there. A value greater than 1 (normally 5-10) ensures reliable propagation. It is not a ratio of ion fluxes, equilibrium potentials, or pump activity.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 5: Membrane Potentials and Action Potentials

  27. Which ion channel state is responsible for the absolute refractory period of a neuron?

    • A. Ligand-gated Cl- channels
    • B. Slow calcium channels
    • C. Voltage-gated K+ channels
    • D. Inactivated voltage-gated Na+ channels
    • E. Na+/K+ ATPase
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Inactivated voltage-gated Na+ channels

  28. Which sequence correctly describes conductance changes during an action potential?

    • A. ↑K+ → ↑Na+ → ↓Na+
    • B. ↑Na+ → ↓Na+ + ↑K+ → ↓K+
    • C. ↓Na+ → ↑K+ → ↑Na+
    • D. ↑K+ → ↓K+ → ↑Na+
    • E. No change in conductance
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. ↑Na+ → ↓Na+ + ↑K+ → ↓K+

    During the action potential, Na+ conductance first rises steeply then falls as Na+ channels inactivate, while K+ conductance rises in a delayed fashion and then falls as K+ channels close during repolarisation. The sequence is therefore increased Na+, then decreased Na+ with increased K+, then decreased K+. K+ conductance does not rise before Na+ conductance.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 5: Membrane Potentials and Action Potentials

  29. Which statement correctly describes a graded (local) potential?

    • A. It follows the all-or-none principle
    • B. Its amplitude is independent of stimulus strength
    • C. Its amplitude varies with the strength of the stimulus
    • D. It propagates without decrement
    • E. It has a refractory period
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Its amplitude varies with the strength of the stimulus

    A graded (local) potential is graded: its amplitude varies directly with stimulus strength, it decays with distance, and it can summate. It does not follow the all-or-none law, does not propagate without decrement, and has no refractory period. These properties allow it to act as a variable signal at synapses and sensory receptors.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 5: Membrane Potentials and Action Potentials

  30. Which statement correctly describes an action potential?

    • A. It is a local, graded change in membrane potential
    • B. It follows the all-or-none principle and propagates without decrement
    • C. It decreases in amplitude as it travels
    • D. It occurs only in dendrites
    • E. Its size varies with stimulus strength
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. It follows the all-or-none principle and propagates without decrement

    An action potential is an all-or-none, self-regenerating wave of depolarisation that propagates along the membrane without decrement once threshold is reached. Its amplitude does not vary with stimulus strength (suprathreshold stimuli do not make it larger), and it is not confined to dendrites. Graded, decaying, size-variable changes describe local potentials instead.

  39. Which of the following normally behaves as leaky channels at rest?

    Answer: B. K+ leak channels

    K+ leak channels are open at rest and largely set the resting membrane potential because the membrane is most permeable to K+. Voltage-gated Na+ channels are closed at rest and open only on depolarization.

  40. The activation gate of the voltage-gated potassium channel is located on which side of the membrane?

    • A. Inside (cytoplasmic side) of the membrane
    • B. Extracellular side
    • C. It has no gate
    • D. Within the lipid core only
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Inside (cytoplasmic side) of the membrane

    In Guyton's description, the K+ channel gate is on the intracellular side and opens slowly on depolarization, allowing K+ efflux that repolarizes the membrane. Na+ channels, in contrast, have both activation and inactivation gates.

  41. Which physiological event is a classic example of positive feedback?

    • A. Baroreceptor control of blood pressure
    • B. Insulin control of blood glucose
    • C. Thermoregulation by sweating
    • D. Action potential upstroke (nerve impulse)
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Action potential upstroke (nerve impulse)

    During the action potential upstroke, Na+ entry depolarizes the membrane, which opens more Na+ channels, causing further depolarization — a positive feedback loop. Blood pressure, temperature, and glucose control are negative feedback systems.

  42. The upstroke of the action potential in working ventricular cardiac muscle is caused primarily by:

    • A. Na+ influx
    • B. K+ efflux
    • C. Cl- efflux
    • D. Ca++ influx
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Na+ influx

    Fast voltage-gated Na+ channels produce the rapid upstroke in atrial, ventricular and Purkinje fibers. In nodal tissue the upstroke is slower and mainly Ca++ dependent.

  43. The resting membrane potential of a large nerve fiber is approximately:

    • A. -70 mV in all cells
    • B. -90 mV in large nerve and muscle fibers
    • C. +61 mV
    • D. 0 mV
    • E. -94 mV exactly
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. -90 mV in large nerve and muscle fibers

    Large nerve and muscle fibers rest near -90 mV, dominated by K+ diffusion down its gradient through leak channels, with a small electrogenic contribution from the Na+/K+ pump. Smaller neurons rest nearer -70 mV. The negative interior reflects K+ loss exceeding Na+ gain at rest.

  44. The resting membrane potential is closest to the equilibrium (Nernst) potential of which ion?

    • A. Sodium
    • B. Potassium
    • C. Calcium
    • D. Chloride
    • E. Magnesium
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Potassium

    At rest the membrane is ~100 times more permeable to K+ than Na+, so the Goldman equation yields a potential near the K+ Nernst potential (-94 mV). The Na+/K+ ATPase maintains the gradients, and its electrogenic 3:2 pumping adds a few millivolts of negativity.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 5: Membrane Potentials and Action Potentials

  45. The Nernst equilibrium potential for Na+ in a typical mammalian neuron is approximately:

    • A. -94 mV
    • B. -70 mV
    • C. 0 mV
    • D. +61 mV
    • E. +90 mV
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. -70 mV

    With ~142 mEq/L outside and ~14 inside, the Nernst equation gives about +61 mV for Na+ — far from the -90 mV resting potential, so Na+ is strongly driven inward. This stored gradient energy powers the action potential upstroke when Na+ channels open.

  46. The threshold potential that must be reached to trigger an action potential in a typical nerve fiber is about:

    • A. -90 mV
    • B. -65 mV
    • C. -94 mV
    • D. 0 mV
    • E. +35 mV
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. -65 mV

    Threshold (about -65 mV in large fibers) is the voltage at which Na+ influx exceeds K+ efflux, igniting the Hodgkin positive-feedback cycle. Weaker depolarizations decay as graded potentials. Threshold is not fixed — it rises with accommodation and falls in hypocalcemia.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 5: Membrane Potentials and Action Potentials

  47. During the rapid upstroke (depolarization phase) of the action potential, membrane permeability increases most dramatically to:

    • A. Potassium
    • B. Sodium
    • C. Calcium
    • D. Chloride
    • E. Magnesium
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Sodium

    Depolarization opens voltage-gated Na+ channels, and Na+ permeability briefly rises several-hundred-fold, driving the membrane toward the Na+ equilibrium potential (+61 mV). The peak overshoot reaches about +35 mV. K+ permeability rises later and more slowly, causing repolarization.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 5: Membrane Potentials and Action Potentials

  48. Repolarization of the neuronal action potential is caused mainly by:

    • A. Continued Na+ influx
    • B. K+ efflux combined with Na+ channel inactivation
    • C. Cl- influx
    • D. Ca2+ influx
    • E. Closure of all K+ channels
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. K+ efflux combined with Na+ channel inactivation

    Repolarization has two arms: Na+ channels inactivate (h gates close) ending Na+ influx, while slow voltage-gated K+ channels open, letting K+ leave and restoring negativity. K+ efflux briefly overshoots, producing the after-hyperpolarization.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 5: Membrane Potentials and Action Potentials

  49. Voltage-gated Na+ channels possess:

    • A. One slow gate
    • B. A fast activation gate and a slower inactivation gate
    • C. Two identical slow gates
    • D. A ligand-binding gate only
    • E. No gates — always open
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. A fast activation gate and a slower inactivation gate

    The activation (m) gate opens quickly with depolarization while the slower inactivation (h) gate closes shortly after, terminating Na+ entry and creating the absolute refractory period. K+ channels have only a single slow gate. This dual-gate design shapes the brief, self-terminating spike.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 5: Membrane Potentials and Action Potentials

  50. The absolute refractory period of the action potential is due to:

    • A. Open K+ channels
    • B. Inactivated (closed) Na+ channels
    • C. Closed Ca2+ channels
    • D. Depleted ATP
    • E. Exhausted neurotransmitter
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Inactivated (closed) Na+ channels

    During the absolute refractory period the h gates are closed and no stimulus, however strong, can reopen Na+ channels — guaranteeing one-way propagation and limiting maximal firing rate. It lasts through most of the spike. The relative refractory period follows, when only strong stimuli can fire the still-recovering fiber.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 5: Membrane Potentials and Action Potentials

  51. During the relative refractory period:

    • A. Complete inexcitability
    • B. Firing possible only with a stronger-than-normal stimulus
    • C. Spontaneous firing
    • D. Reversed polarity spikes
    • E. Permanent inactivation
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Firing possible only with a stronger-than-normal stimulus

    After the spike, K+ conductance remains elevated, hyperpolarizing the membrane; a larger-than-normal depolarization is then needed to reach threshold. This period allows the fiber to recover while still permitting very strong stimuli to fire — unlike the absolute period, where firing is impossible.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 5: Membrane Potentials and Action Potentials

  52. The all-or-none principle of the action potential means:

    • A. Larger stimuli produce taller spikes
    • B. Any suprathreshold stimulus produces a full-sized, identical spike
    • C. Spike amplitude grades with stimulus duration
    • D. Subthreshold stimuli produce small spikes
    • E. Spikes summate like graded potentials
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Any suprathreshold stimulus produces a full-sized, identical spike

    Once threshold is crossed, the Hodgkin cycle drives the spike to the same amplitude regardless of stimulus strength — the impulse is stereotyped. Stimulus intensity is encoded by firing frequency, not spike height. This digital signaling gives nerves their reliability.

  53. Saltatory conduction in myelinated fibers refers to:

    • A. Continuous regeneration along the whole axon
    • B. Jumping of the impulse between nodes of Ranvier
    • C. Slower conduction than unmyelinated fibers
    • D. Passive electrotonic spread only
    • E. Reverse-direction propagation
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Jumping of the impulse between nodes of Ranvier

    Myelin insulates internodes so current jumps from node to node, regenerating the spike only where Na+ channels cluster — up to 50x faster and far more energy-efficient than continuous conduction. Demyelination (multiple sclerosis, Guillain-Barre) slows or blocks this jumping, causing weakness and sensory loss.

  54. Hyperkalemia (raised extracellular K+) causes:

    • A. Hyperpolarization and increased conduction
    • B. No change in membrane potential
    • C. Depolarization of the resting potential, with eventual conduction block if severe
    • D. Blockade of Ca2+ channels
    • E. Increased threshold to infinity
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Depolarization of the resting potential, with eventual conduction block if severe

    Raising extracellular K+ reduces the K+ gradient, so less K+ leaves and the resting potential becomes less negative (e.g., -90 to -70 mV). Mild depolarization first heightens excitability, but severe hyperkalemia inactivates Na+ channels and blocks conduction — causing the peaked T waves, widened QRS, and arrest seen in renal failure.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 5: Membrane Potentials and Action Potentials

  55. Hypocalcemia (low extracellular Ca2+) produces:

    • A. Decreased neuronal excitability
    • B. Increased neuronal excitability, causing tetany
    • C. Blockade of K+ channels
    • D. Hyperpolarization
    • E. No effect on excitability
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Increased neuronal excitability, causing tetany

    Extracellular Ca2+ normally 'screens' fixed negative charges on the membrane, stabilizing Na+ channels. When Ca2+ falls, less depolarization opens Na+ channels, so nerves fire spontaneously — perioral tingling, carpopedal spasm, Chvostek and Trousseau signs of hypocalcemic tetany. This is why calcium gluconate is given IV in tetany.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 5: Membrane Potentials and Action Potentials

  56. Local anesthetics such as lidocaine relieve pain by blocking:

    • A. Voltage-gated K+ channels
    • B. Voltage-gated Na+ channels
    • C. Nicotinic receptors
    • D. GABA receptors
    • E. Ca2+ pumps
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Voltage-gated Na+ channels

    Lidocaine, bupivacaine, and procaine enter open/inactivated Na+ channels from inside and plug them, preventing the upstroke — so no action potential, no pain signal. They preferentially block small pain fibers and are use-dependent (bind better to rapidly firing channels). Toxicity causes seizures then cardiac depression.

  57. Accommodation of a nerve fiber — failure to fire during very slow depolarization — is due to:

    • A. Increased threshold from K+ buildup
    • B. Na+ channel inactivation during slow depolarization, preventing firing
    • C. Depletion of ATP
    • D. Exhaustion of Ca2+ stores
    • E. Myelin thickening
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Na+ channel inactivation during slow depolarization, preventing firing

    With slow depolarization, Na+ channels open but their inactivation gates close before threshold is reached, so the fiber never fires — it has 'accommodated.' Rapid depolarization outruns inactivation and triggers the spike. Accommodation explains why slowly growing compressions may be painless while sudden ones hurt.

  58. The after-hyperpolarization (positive after-potential) following the spike is caused by:

    • A. Continued Na+ entry
    • B. Prolonged K+ efflux through slow voltage-gated K+ channels
    • C. Cl- exit
    • D. Electrogenic pump reversal
    • E. Ca2+ channel opening
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Prolonged K+ efflux through slow voltage-gated K+ channels

    Slow voltage-gated K+ channels stay open briefly after the spike, driving the membrane below resting level (after-hyperpolarization). This contributes to the relative refractory period. In pacemaker cells, the subsequent decay of K+ conductance plus funny-current Na+ entry drives the next spontaneous depolarization.

  59. Nerve conduction velocity is increased by:

    • A. Smaller fiber diameter
    • B. Myelination and larger diameter
    • C. Higher extracellular K+
    • D. Accommodation
    • E. Shorter refractory period
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Myelination and larger diameter

  60. A patient with chronic renal failure develops peaked T waves, widened QRS complexes, and muscle weakness. The most likely electrolyte abnormality is:

    • A. Hypocalcemia
    • B. Hyperkalemia
    • C. Hyponatremia
    • D. Hypomagnesemia
    • E. Metabolic alkalosis
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Hyperkalemia

    In renal failure, retained K+ depolarizes cardiac membranes: first the T wave peaks (faster repolarization), then the QRS widens (slowed conduction), and finally asystole.   61. In the Goldman-Hodgkin-Katz equation for the resting membrane potential, the permeability term that dominates is that of:

    • A. Sodium
    • B. Potassium
    • C. Calcium
    • D. Chloride
    • E. Hydrogen
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Potassium

    At rest the Goldman-Hodgkin-Katz equation is dominated by the K+ term because resting K+ permeability dwarfs Na+ and Cl- permeabilities. During the spike, the Na+ term dominates. This permeability weighting is why the resting potential sits near -90 mV but the peak approaches +35 mV.

  62. A sustained depolarizing stimulus well above threshold applied to a nerve fiber produces:

    • A. A single spike only
    • B. A train of repetitive action potentials for as long as the stimulus lasts
    • C. Progressively taller spikes
    • D. Immediate accommodation
    • E. Reverse propagation
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. A train of repetitive action potentials for as long as the stimulus lasts

    A maintained suprathreshold current repeatedly re-depolarizes the membrane after each refractory period, producing a train of identical spikes — the basis of frequency coding. Stronger stimuli shorten the interspike interval (up to the refractory limit). When the stimulus ends, firing stops.

    • B. Type B preganglionic autonomic fibres

    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Type A-alpha fibres supplying skeletal muscle proprioception and motor function

    Large myelinated A-alpha fibres (about 70–120 m/sec) conduct fastest because velocity rises with fibre diameter and myelination. C fibres are unmyelinated and slowest (about 0.5–2 m/sec), B fibres are intermediate, and A-delta fibres are thinner and slower than A-alpha.
