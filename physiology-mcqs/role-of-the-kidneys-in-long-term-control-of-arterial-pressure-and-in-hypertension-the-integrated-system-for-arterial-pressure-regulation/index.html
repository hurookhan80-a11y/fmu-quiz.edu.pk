Over days and weeks, arterial pressure is controlled mainly by the kidneys through their control of body fluid volume. When pressure rises, the kidneys excrete more salt and water, reducing blood volume and bringing pressure down; when pressure falls, they retain salt and water. If this system is disturbed, pressure stays raised, which is hypertension.

  1. When arterial pressure rises above normal, the kidneys excrete more sodium and water; when pressure falls, they retain sodium and water. This phenomenon is called:

    • A. Tubuloglomerular feedback
    • B. Osmotic diuresis
    • C. Pressure natriuresis and pressure diuresis
    • D. Aldosterone escape
    • E. The Bainbridge reflex
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Pressure natriuresis and pressure diuresis

    Pressure natriuresis/diuresis is the kidney's intrinsic response to arterial pressure: higher pressure increases renal interstitial pressure and medullary blood flow, reducing tubular reabsorption and increasing excretion. This infinite-gain feedback is the dominant long-term controller of arterial pressure — it continues until pressure returns exactly to the equilibrium point.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 19: Role of the Kidneys in Long-Term Control of Arterial Pressure and in Hypertension: The Integrated System for Arterial Pressure Regulation

  2. The renal-body fluid feedback system is said to have 'infinite gain' because:

    • A. It keeps correcting until arterial pressure returns precisely to its set point, no matter how large the disturbance
    • B. It acts within seconds
    • C. It involves infinite hormones
    • D. It works without the kidneys
    • E. It raises pressure without limit
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. It keeps correcting until arterial pressure returns precisely to its set point, no matter how large the disturbance

    Infinite gain means the feedback never fatigues: as long as pressure deviates from the kidney's equilibrium point, sodium and water balance keeps shifting in the corrective direction until pressure is restored exactly. Baroreflexes, by contrast, adapt within days. This is why long-term pressure is ultimately set by renal function, not by nerves.

  3. The correct sequence of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone cascade is:

    • A. Aldosterone → renin → angiotensin I → angiotensin II
    • B. Angiotensin II → renin → angiotensin I → aldosterone
    • C. Renin → aldosterone → angiotensin I → angiotensin II
    • D. Renin → angiotensin I → angiotensin II (via ACE) → aldosterone
    • E. Angiotensin I → renin → aldosterone → angiotensin II
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Renin → angiotensin I → angiotensin II (via ACE) → aldosterone

    Juxtaglomerular cells release renin, which cleaves angiotensinogen to angiotensin I; pulmonary ACE converts it to angiotensin II, which constricts vessels and stimulates adrenal aldosterone release. Aldosterone then drives distal tubular sodium reabsorption. ACE inhibitors interrupt this chain at the angiotensin II step.

  4. Angiotensin II raises arterial pressure through all of the following EXCEPT:

    • A. Direct arteriolar vasoconstriction
    • B. Stimulation of aldosterone secretion
    • C. ADH release and thirst stimulation
    • D. Increased renal tubular sodium reabsorption
    • E. Vasodilation of skeletal muscle beds
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: E. Vasodilation of skeletal muscle beds

    Angiotensin II is a comprehensive pressure-raising hormone: potent vasoconstriction, aldosterone-mediated sodium retention, ADH release, thirst, sympathetic enhancement, and direct renal sodium reabsorption. Vasodilation is the opposite of its action — that would lower pressure, which is why vasodilation of muscle beds is the EXCEPT answer.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 19: Role of the Kidneys in Long-Term Control of Arterial Pressure and in Hypertension: The Integrated System for Arterial Pressure Regulation

  5. Aldosterone increases renal sodium reabsorption in the principal cells of the collecting duct mainly by:

    • A. Blocking ADH receptors
    • B. Inhibiting the Na+/K+ ATPase
    • C. Increasing apical ENaC sodium channels and basolateral Na+/K+ ATPase activity
    • D. Constricting the afferent arteriole
    • E. Increasing GFR
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Increasing apical ENaC sodium channels and basolateral Na+/K+ ATPase activity

    Aldosterone acts on mineralocorticoid receptors to synthesize more ENaC channels (apical sodium entry) and Na+/K+ ATPase pumps (basolateral extrusion). Sodium reabsorption creates an electrical gradient driving potassium and hydrogen secretion — explaining why hyperaldosteronism causes hypertension with hypokalemia and metabolic alkalosis.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 19: Role of the Kidneys in Long-Term Control of Arterial Pressure and in Hypertension: The Integrated System for Arterial Pressure Regulation

  6. Renin release from juxtaglomerular cells is stimulated by all of the following EXCEPT:

    • A. Increased renal perfusion pressure
    • B. Decreased NaCl delivery to the macula densa
    • C. Sympathetic beta-1 stimulation
    • D. Decreased afferent arteriolar stretch
    • E. Hemorrhage
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Increased renal perfusion pressure

    Renin is released when the kidney senses underperfusion: low afferent arteriolar stretch (the renal baroreceptor), low NaCl at the macula densa, and sympathetic beta-1 activation. INCREASED perfusion pressure suppresses renin — it signals the kidney that pressure is adequate, the opposite of the release stimuli.

  7. In the two-kidney, one-clip Goldblatt model of hypertension, the clipped kidney releases excess renin. If the OTHER (unclipped) kidney is then removed (one-kidney, one-clip), the hypertension becomes primarily:

    • A. Renin-dependent forever
    • B. Cured immediately
    • C. Neurogenic
    • D. Volume-dependent with suppressed renin
    • E. Due to pheochromocytoma
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Volume-dependent with suppressed renin

    In 2K1C, the stenotic kidney secretes renin while the normal kidney undergoes pressure natriuresis — renin-dependent hypertension. Removing the normal kidney eliminates the escape route: sodium and water are retained, volume expands, and renin is suppressed — converting it to volume-dependent (1K1C) hypertension, which responds to diuretics rather than ACE inhibitors.

  8. In most patients with established essential hypertension, the hemodynamic pattern is:

    • A. Low cardiac output with low peripheral resistance
    • B. Normal cardiac output with elevated total peripheral resistance
    • C. High cardiac output with low resistance
    • D. Low blood volume
    • E. Purely systolic elevation
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Normal cardiac output with elevated total peripheral resistance

    Essential hypertension often begins with increased cardiac output (from excess sympathetic drive or salt retention), but the established phase is characterized by normal output with raised total peripheral resistance from structural vascular remodeling. Treatment therefore targets resistance vessels, volume, or both.

  9. A hypertensive patient started on an ACE inhibitor develops a persistent dry cough. The cough is attributed to:

    • A. Allergic reaction to the drug
    • B. Bronchospasm from angiotensin II
    • C. Accumulation of bradykinin, normally degraded by ACE
    • D. Excessive aldosterone
    • E. Pulmonary edema
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Accumulation of bradykinin, normally degraded by ACE

    ACE (kininase II) normally degrades bradykinin; inhibiting ACE raises bradykinin in airways, sensitizing cough receptors and causing the classic dry cough in 5-20% of patients. Switching to an angiotensin receptor blocker (ARB), which does not affect bradykinin, usually resolves it.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 19: Role of the Kidneys in Long-Term Control of Arterial Pressure and in Hypertension: The Integrated System for Arterial Pressure Regulation

  10. Atrial natriuretic peptide, released from stretched atria, lowers arterial pressure by:

    • A. Promoting renal sodium and water excretion, vasodilation, and suppressing RAAS and ADH
    • B. Stimulating aldosterone
    • C. Constricting afferent arterioles
    • D. Increasing sympathetic outflow
    • E. Retaining water
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Promoting renal sodium and water excretion, vasodilation, and suppressing RAAS and ADH

    ANP is the heart's own pressure-relief hormone: it dilates vessels, increases GFR, inhibits tubular sodium reabsorption (natriuresis/diuresis), and suppresses renin, aldosterone, and ADH. BNP, its ventricular cousin released in heart failure, works similarly and is used diagnostically.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 19: Role of the Kidneys in Long-Term Control of Arterial Pressure and in Hypertension: The Integrated System for Arterial Pressure Regulation

  11. Salt-sensitive individuals develop hypertension on high salt intake mainly because their kidneys:

    • A. Excrete salt too rapidly
    • B. Cannot produce renin
    • C. Require higher arterial pressure to excrete the salt load (blunted pressure natriuresis)
    • D. Lack aldosterone receptors
    • E. Produce excess ANP
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Require higher arterial pressure to excrete the salt load (blunted pressure natriuresis)

    In salt sensitivity, the pressure-natriuresis curve is shifted right: the kidney needs a higher perfusion pressure to excrete a given sodium load. Salt intake therefore raises pressure until natriuresis matches intake. This rightward shift underlies much of essential hypertension in salt-sensitive populations.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 19: Role of the Kidneys in Long-Term Control of Arterial Pressure and in Hypertension: The Integrated System for Arterial Pressure Regulation

  12. In chronic hypertension, the renal function (pressure-natriuresis) curve is typically:

    • A. Shifted left, so less pressure is needed
    • B. Shifted right, so higher pressure is needed to excrete normal sodium
    • C. Unchanged
    • D. Abolished
    • E. Reversed
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Shifted right, so higher pressure is needed to excrete normal sodium

    Hypertension represents a new equilibrium: the kidney's pressure-natriuresis curve shifts rightward, so sodium balance is achieved only at higher arterial pressure. Antihypertensive therapy works by shifting the curve back left (diuretics, RAAS blockade) or by reducing the pressure needed for balance.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 19: Role of the Kidneys in Long-Term Control of Arterial Pressure and in Hypertension: The Integrated System for Arterial Pressure Regulation

  13. ADH (vasopressin) promotes water reabsorption in the collecting duct by:

    • A. Blocking ENaC channels
    • B. Constricting the efferent arteriole
    • C. Inserting aquaporin-2 water channels into the apical membrane of principal cells
    • D. Increasing GFR
    • E. Inhibiting aldosterone
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Inserting aquaporin-2 water channels into the apical membrane of principal cells

    ADH binds V2 receptors, raising cAMP and inserting aquaporin-2 channels into the luminal membrane, making the collecting duct water-permeable so water follows the medullary osmotic gradient out of the tubule. Absence of ADH (central diabetes insipidus) leaves the duct impermeable, causing massive dilute urine output.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 19: Role of the Kidneys in Long-Term Control of Arterial Pressure and in Hypertension: The Integrated System for Arterial Pressure Regulation

  14. Increased renal sympathetic nerve activity raises long-term arterial pressure primarily by:

    • A. Stimulating renin release and increasing tubular sodium reabsorption
    • B. Dilating renal vessels
    • C. Suppressing ADH
    • D. Increasing GFR directly
    • E. Blocking aldosterone
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Stimulating renin release and increasing tubular sodium reabsorption

    Renal sympathetic activation does three pro-hypertensive things: constricts renal vessels (reducing GFR), directly stimulates tubular sodium reabsorption, and triggers renin release via beta-1 receptors. Chronic sympathetic overactivity thus resets long-term pressure upward — a rationale for renal denervation therapy in resistant hypertension.

  15. A 58-year-old smoker with resistant hypertension has an abdominal bruit and worsening renal function after starting an ACE inhibitor. Serum potassium is low. The most likely diagnosis is:

    • A. Essential hypertension
    • B. Pheochromocytoma
    • C. Coarctation of the aorta
    • D. Primary hyperaldosteronism
    • E. Atherosclerotic renal artery stenosis with secondary hyperaldosteronism
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: E. Atherosclerotic renal artery stenosis with secondary hyperaldosteronism

    Unilateral renal artery stenosis causes renal hypoperfusion, driving renin and secondary aldosterone excess (hypertension with hypokalemia). ACE inhibitors can precipitate renal failure in bilateral stenosis or stenosis of a solitary kidney by removing angiotensin II-dependent efferent arteriolar tone. The bruit and asymmetric kidneys clinch the renovascular diagnosis.

  16. The juxtaglomerular apparatus, which regulates renin release and tubuloglomerular feedback, consists of:

    • A. Podocytes, mesangium, and Bowman's capsule
    • B. Juxtaglomerular (granular) cells, macula densa, and lacis (extraglomerular mesangial) cells
    • C. Proximal tubule and loop of Henle
    • D. Afferent and efferent arterioles only
    • E. Collecting duct and vasa recta
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Juxtaglomerular (granular) cells, macula densa, and lacis (extraglomerular mesangial) cells

    At the vascular pole where the thick ascending limb passes between the arterioles, three specialized structures meet: renin-secreting granular JG cells in the afferent arteriole wall, NaCl-sensing macula densa cells of the tubule, and lacis cells bridging them. This is the kidney's combined sensor and effector for pressure and sodium balance.

  17. When NaCl delivery to the macula densa decreases (as in reduced GFR), the macula densa signals:

    • A. Afferent arteriolar constriction only
    • B. Suppression of renin
    • C. Afferent arteriolar dilation and renin release
    • D. Collecting duct water impermeability
    • E. Efferent constriction only
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Afferent arteriolar dilation and renin release

    Low distal NaCl delivery tells the macula densa that filtration is inadequate. It triggers tubuloglomerular feedback (dilation of the afferent arteriole to raise GFR) and stimulates renin release from JG cells. This couples single-nephron filtration to systemic pressure control.

  18. Why can't the arterial baroreceptor reflex correct chronic hypertension?

    • A. Baroreceptors are destroyed by high pressure
    • B. The heart becomes unresponsive
    • C. Aldosterone blocks vagal tone
    • D. Baroreceptors reset to the higher pressure within days, accepting it as normal
    • E. The kidneys override the reflex acutely
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Baroreceptors reset to the higher pressure within days, accepting it as normal

    Baroreceptors are rapidly adapting stretch receptors: after 1-2 days of sustained hypertension they reset their threshold upward and resume firing normally at the higher pressure. The reflex then defends the hypertensive level instead of correcting it. Lasting pressure control requires the non-adapting renal-body fluid system.

  19. Excess mineralocorticoid (as in primary hyperaldosteronism or heavy licorice ingestion) causes hypertension mainly through:

    • A. Renal sodium and water retention expanding extracellular fluid volume
    • B. Direct cardiac stimulation
    • C. Increased sympathetic outflow
    • D. Renal artery constriction
    • E. Increased vascular permeability
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Renal sodium and water retention expanding extracellular fluid volume

    Mineralocorticoid excess drives relentless distal sodium reabsorption with water following, expanding ECF volume and raising pressure via the renal-body fluid mechanism. The volume expansion suppresses renin (low-renin hypertension), and the coupled K+/H+ secretion causes the characteristic hypokalemia and metabolic alkalosis.

  20. Thiazide diuretics lower arterial pressure in hypertension primarily by:

    • A. Direct arteriolar dilation
    • B. Reducing extracellular fluid volume through renal sodium and water loss, shifting the pressure-natriuresis equilibrium leftward
    • C. Blocking sympathetic nerves
    • D. Increasing cardiac contractility
    • E. Stimulating renin
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Reducing extracellular fluid volume through renal sodium and water loss, shifting the pressure-natriuresis equilibrium leftward

    Thiazides block the NaCl cotransporter in the distal tubule, causing natriuresis and volume contraction. The reduced ECF volume lowers the pressure required for sodium balance — shifting the renal function curve leftward. A mild chronic vasodilatory effect also contributes, but volume reduction is the primary long-term mechanism.
