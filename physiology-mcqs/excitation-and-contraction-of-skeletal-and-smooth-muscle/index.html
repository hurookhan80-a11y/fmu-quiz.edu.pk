Excitation and Contraction of Skeletal and Smooth Muscle MCQs for FMU 1st Year MBBS Physiology

Home › Physiology MCQs › Excitation and Contraction of Skeletal and Smooth Muscle

Both skeletal and smooth muscle contract when calcium rises inside the cell and allows the contractile proteins to interact. Skeletal muscle is under voluntary control, contracts quickly, and is triggered by nerve impulses at the neuromuscular junction. Smooth muscle contracts slowly and involuntarily, and it is found in the walls of hollow organs and blood vessels.

  1. During rehabilitation a patient pushes against an immovable wall with full force. Which will change in the exercising muscle?

    • A. Muscle length
    • B. Muscle tension
    • C. Sarcomere number
    • D. Muscle mass immediately
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Muscle tension

    Pushing against an immovable wall is an isometric contraction: cross-bridges cycle and tension rises, but the muscle cannot shorten because the load cannot be moved, so muscle length stays constant. Sarcomere number and muscle mass do not change acutely during a single effort. Length would change only in isotonic shortening.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Excitation and Contraction of Skeletal and Smooth Muscle

  2. In a smooth muscle cell compared to a typical neuron fast Na+ channels; replaced by voltage-gated Ca2+ channels. It would characterize the action potential as

    • A. Faster onset and shorter duration due to rapid Ca2+ kinetics
    • B. Slower onset and prolonged depolarization due to slow Ca2+ channel activation
    • C. No depolarization due to absence of Na+ channels
    • D. Immediate repolarization due to K+ dominance
    • E. Hyperpolarized resting membrane potential
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Slower onset and prolonged depolarization due to slow Ca2+ channel activation

    Smooth muscle relies mainly on slow voltage-gated Ca2+ channels rather than fast Na+ channels. Because these Ca2+ channels open and close slowly, the action potential has a slow upstroke and a prolonged plateau-like depolarisation, suiting tonic contraction. It does not show the rapid onset and brief duration of a neuronal Na+ spike.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Excitation and Contraction of Skeletal and Smooth Muscle

  3. In smooth muscle, calcium binds to which protein to initiate contraction?

    • A. Troponin C
    • B. Tropomyosin
    • C. Calmodulin
    • D. Actin
    • E. Dystrophin
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Calmodulin

    Smooth muscle has no troponin system; instead Ca2+ entering the cell binds calmodulin. The Ca2+-calmodulin complex activates myosin light-chain kinase, which phosphorylates myosin light chains and permits cross-bridge cycling. Troponin C is the Ca2+ sensor in skeletal and cardiac muscle, while tropomyosin and actin are filament proteins, not the Ca2+ receptor here.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Excitation and Contraction of Skeletal and Smooth Muscle

  4. Stored ATP in a contracting muscle fibre provides energy for only a few seconds mainly because:

    • A. Stored ATP reserves are very small
    • B. Excessive calcium release
    • C. Increased acetylcholine breakdown
    • D. Increased oxygen storage
    • E. Myosin ATPase is absent
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Stored ATP reserves are very small

    The amount of preformed ATP stored in muscle is very small and can sustain only a few seconds (a few contractions) of maximal activity. Continued contraction depends on rapid ATP regeneration from phosphocreatine, glycolysis, and oxidative phosphorylation. The limitation is store size, not excessive Ca2+ release, ACh breakdown, or absence of myosin ATPase.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Excitation and Contraction of Skeletal and Smooth Muscle

  5. The 'latch mechanism' in smooth muscle:

    • A. Requires continuous rapid ATP hydrolysis
    • B. Enables prolonged contraction with very low energy use
    • C. Causes immediate relaxation
    • D. Is dependent on troponin activation
    • E. Functions only in multi-unit muscle
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Enables prolonged contraction with very low energy use

    In the latch mechanism, phosphorylated cross-bridges in smooth muscle detach very slowly and can maintain tension while cycling at a very low rate. This allows prolonged tonic contraction, such as vascular tone, with minimal ATP consumption. It does not require rapid ATP hydrolysis, is not troponin-dependent, and does not cause immediate relaxation.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Excitation and Contraction of Skeletal and Smooth Muscle

  6. The efficiency of muscle contraction (conversion of chemical energy into mechanical work) is approximately:

    • A. 10%
    • B. 25%
    • C. 50%
    • D. 75%
    • E. 90%
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. 25%

    Skeletal muscle is only about 25% efficient: roughly one quarter of the chemical energy from ATP appears as external mechanical work and the rest is dissipated as heat (initial and recovery heat). Efficiency is far below 50-90%, which is why exercise markedly increases body heat production.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Excitation and Contraction of Skeletal and Smooth Muscle

  7. The enzyme responsible for smooth muscle relaxation by dephosphorylating myosin is:

    • A. Calmodulin
    • B. Myosin light-chain kinase
    • C. Myosin light-chain phosphatase
    • D. Myosin ATPase
    • E. Protein kinase C
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Myosin light-chain phosphatase

    Relaxation of smooth muscle occurs when myosin light-chain phosphatase dephosphorylates the regulatory light chains of myosin, so cross-bridges detach and tension falls. Myosin light-chain kinase has the opposite, contraction-producing action, while calmodulin is the Ca2+ sensor that activates the kinase. Myosin ATPase and protein kinase C do not directly dephosphorylate myosin.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Excitation and Contraction of Skeletal and Smooth Muscle

  8. The invagination of sarcolemma that increases surface area for action potential propagation in skeletal muscle is the:

    • A. Caveola
    • B. Myofibril
    • C. Sarcoplasmic reticulum
    • D. Triad junction
    • E. T-tubule (transverse tubule)
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: E. T-tubule (transverse tubule)

    The transverse (T) tubules are deep invaginations of the sarcolemma that carry the action potential into the interior of the skeletal muscle fibre. This ensures that Ca2+ release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum via ryanodine receptors occurs almost simultaneously throughout the fibre. The SR itself stores Ca2+, myofibrils are the contractile apparatus, and the triad is the T-tubule-SR junction, not the invagination itself.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Excitation and Contraction of Skeletal and Smooth Muscle

  9. The latent period in a muscle twitch is the time between:

    • A. Relaxation and re-stimulation
    • B. Cross-bridge formation and power stroke
    • C. Action potential and onset of contraction
    • D. ATP synthesis and release
    • E. Calcium reuptake and relaxation
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Action potential and onset of contraction

    The latent period is the brief interval (about 10 ms) between the muscle action potential and the onset of measurable tension. During it the impulse travels down T-tubules, Ca2+ is released from the SR, binds troponin, and cross-bridges begin to form (excitation-contraction coupling). It is not the time for relaxation, ATP synthesis, or Ca2+ reuptake.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Excitation and Contraction of Skeletal and Smooth Muscle

  10. The power stroke in muscle contraction occurs when:

    • A. ATP binds to myosin
    • B. Calcium binds to tropomyosin
    • C. Myosin head pivots after releasing ADP and Pi
    • D. Sarcolemma depolarises
    • E. Myosin detaches from actin
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Myosin head pivots after releasing ADP and Pi

    The power stroke occurs when the cocked myosin head, bound to actin, releases ADP and inorganic phosphate; this triggers pivoting of the head that drags the actin filament toward the centre of the sarcomere. ATP binding itself detaches myosin from actin, and ATP hydrolysis re-cocks the head, but neither is the stroke. Ca2+ binds troponin, not tropomyosin, to expose actin sites.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Excitation and Contraction of Skeletal and Smooth Muscle

  11. The treppe (staircase) effect in muscle is best described as:

    • A. Progressive decrease in contraction with repeated stimulation
    • B. Progressive increase in contraction strength with repeated stimulation
    • C. Complete relaxation after repeated stimulation
    • D. Sustained contraction due to absence of ATP
    • E. Failure of relaxation
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Progressive increase in contraction strength with repeated stimulation

    Treppe, or the staircase phenomenon, is the progressive increase in twitch tension when a resting muscle is stimulated repeatedly at a frequency too low to cause summation. It is attributed to gradual rise in cytosolic Ca2+ availability and warming of the muscle. It is the opposite of fatigue (decreasing force), and differs from tetanus in that relaxation still occurs between stimuli.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Excitation and Contraction of Skeletal and Smooth Muscle

  12. What arrangement of filaments in a sarcomere allows muscle contraction?

    • A. Actin filaments overlap myosin filaments in the A band
    • B. Actin filaments are confined to the Z line
    • C. Actin overlaps myosin only in the I band
    • D. Myosin filaments are confined to the M line
    • E. Myosin filaments are confined to the Z line
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Actin filaments overlap myosin filaments in the A band

    Contraction is possible because in the A band thin actin filaments interdigitate and overlap with thick myosin filaments, allowing myosin heads to form cross-bridges and slide the filaments. Actin is anchored at the Z discs and extends into the A band; it is not confined to the Z line or only to the I band. Myosin is centred on the M line but extends through the A band, not confined to the M line alone.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Excitation and Contraction of Skeletal and Smooth Muscle

  13. What is the first major event after ATP depletion in rigor mortis?

    • A. Myosin detaches from actin
    • B. Calcium is completely removed from cytoplasm
    • C. Myosin heads remain attached to actin (rigor complexes)
    • D. Acetylcholine is released continuously
    • E. Sarcomeres shorten indefinitely
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Myosin heads remain attached to actin (rigor complexes)

    After death, ATP is no longer regenerated, Ca2+ accumulates in the cytosol, and myosin heads bind actin but cannot detach because detachment requires fresh ATP. The persistent actin-myosin rigor complexes stiffen the muscle in rigor mortis until autolysis breaks the proteins down. Myosin therefore remains attached, rather than detaching, and Ca2+ is not removed from the cytoplasm.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Excitation and Contraction of Skeletal and Smooth Muscle

  14. What is the relationship between load and velocity of shortening in muscle contraction?

    • A. Directly proportional
    • B. Not related
    • C. Velocity increases with heavier loads
    • D. Velocity decreases as load increases
    • E. Velocity is constant regardless of load
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Velocity decreases as load increases

    The force-velocity relation shows an inverse relationship: as the load against which a muscle shortens increases, the velocity of shortening decreases, reaching zero at maximal isometric force. With no load, velocity is maximal. Velocity is therefore neither constant nor directly increased by heavier loads.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Excitation and Contraction of Skeletal and Smooth Muscle

  15. What is the role of ATP in the cross-bridge cycle?

    • A. Binds actin to initiate contraction
    • B. Triggers Ca2+ release from the SR
    • C. Breaks the actin-myosin bond for cross-bridge detachment
    • D. Stabilises troponin-tropomyosin
    • E. Forms the power stroke directly
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Breaks the actin-myosin bond for cross-bridge detachment

    ATP has two roles: its hydrolysis cocks the myosin head, but binding of a fresh ATP molecule to myosin is what breaks the actin-myosin link and detaches the cross-bridge. Without ATP the bridges stay locked, as in rigor mortis. ATP does not directly trigger SR Ca2+ release or form the power stroke itself.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Excitation and Contraction of Skeletal and Smooth Muscle

  16. What is the role of the ryanodine receptor (RyR) in skeletal muscle contraction?

    • A. Pumps calcium into the SR
    • B. Acts as a calcium-binding buffer only
    • C. Forms calcium channels in the sarcolemma
    • D. Releases calcium from the sarcoplasmic reticulum
    • E. Degrades excess calcium
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Releases calcium from the sarcoplasmic reticulum

    The ryanodine receptor (RyR1) is the Ca2+ release channel in the sarcoplasmic reticulum membrane of skeletal muscle. Depolarisation of the T-tubule opens RyR1 (via the DHP receptor linkage), flooding the cytosol with Ca2+ to initiate contraction. Ca2+ pumping back into the SR is done by SERCA, and RyR is not a sarcolemmal channel or Ca2+ buffer.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Excitation and Contraction of Skeletal and Smooth Muscle

  17. Which energy source provides rapid but short-term energy during the initial seconds of intense muscle activity?

    • A. Oxidative phosphorylation
    • B. Anaerobic glycolysis to lactate
    • C. Creatine phosphate (phosphocreatine)
    • D. Fatty acid oxidation
    • E. Protein breakdown
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Creatine phosphate (phosphocreatine)

    Phosphocreatine (creatine phosphate) provides the fastest ATP regeneration via the creatine kinase reaction and sustains maximal effort for the first several seconds. Glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation are slower to ramp up, fatty acid oxidation is slowest, and protein breakdown is not a rapid energy source. Once phosphocreatine is depleted, glycolysis becomes dominant.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Excitation and Contraction of Skeletal and Smooth Muscle

  18. Which statement about isometric contraction is TRUE?

    • A. Muscle shortens while lifting a load
    • B. Muscle length changes but tension stays constant
    • C. Muscle tension increases but length remains constant
    • D. It occurs only during fatigue
    • E. It is the primary contraction type in walking
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Muscle tension increases but length remains constant

    Isometric contraction is defined by development of tension without change in muscle length, as occurs when the load is too heavy to lift. Length does not shorten, tension is not constant, and it is common in posture and stabilisation, not only in fatigue. Shortening with constant tension describes isotonic contraction.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Excitation and Contraction of Skeletal and Smooth Muscle

  19. Which step directly triggers the power stroke during skeletal muscle contraction?

    • A. Hydrolysis of ATP on the myosin head
    • B. Release of ADP and Pi from the myosin head
    • C. Attachment of calcium to troponin C
    • D. Opening of calcium channels
    • E. Binding of ATP to myosin
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Release of ADP and Pi from the myosin head

    The power stroke is directly triggered when the myosin head, already cocked by prior ATP hydrolysis and bound to actin, releases ADP and Pi; the head then pivots and pulls the actin filament. ATP hydrolysis itself only re-cocks the head, Ca2+ binding to troponin exposes actin sites, and ATP binding causes detachment, not the stroke.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Excitation and Contraction of Skeletal and Smooth Muscle

  20. Which structure forms the boundary of a sarcomere?

    • A. H zone
    • B. M line
    • C. Z line (Z disc)
    • D. A band
    • E. I band
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Z line (Z disc)

    A sarcomere, the functional unit of striated muscle, is the region between two successive Z discs (Z lines). The A band, I band, H zone, and M line are internal subdivisions of the sarcomere, not its boundaries. During contraction the distance between Z discs shortens as actin slides over myosin.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Excitation and Contraction of Skeletal and Smooth Muscle

  21. Which type of smooth muscle exhibits coordinated contractions through gap-junction electrical coupling?

    • A. Multi-unit smooth muscle
    • B. Skeletal muscle
    • C. Cardiac muscle
    • D. Unitary (single-unit) smooth muscle
    • E. Striated muscle
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Unitary (single-unit) smooth muscle

    Unitary (single-unit) visceral smooth muscle cells are linked by gap junctions, so depolarisation spreads from cell to cell and the whole sheet contracts in a coordinated, syncytial fashion, as in gut and uterus. Multi-unit smooth muscle (iris, piloerectors) lacks extensive coupling and each unit is separately innervated. Skeletal muscle fibres are not coupled in this way, and cardiac muscle, although coupled, is not classified as smooth muscle.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Excitation and Contraction of Skeletal and Smooth Muscle
