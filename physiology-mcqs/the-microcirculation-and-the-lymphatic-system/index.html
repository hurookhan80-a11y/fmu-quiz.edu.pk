Home › Physiology MCQs › The Microcirculation and the Lymphatic System

The microcirculation consists of the smallest blood vessels, where oxygen and nutrients leave the blood and waste products enter it. The lymphatic system collects the fluid that remains in the tissues, filters it through lymph nodes, and returns it to the bloodstream. Together these systems maintain fluid balance and support immune defence.

  1. The alternate route of blood flow when the main channel is blocked is through:

    • A. Simple anastomosis
    • B. Complex anastomosis
    • C. Shunt
    • D. Thoroughfare channel
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Thoroughfare channel

    When precapillary sphincters close, blood can still pass from arteriole to venule through the thoroughfare channel, the central channel of the metarteriole that bypasses the true capillaries. This provides the alternate route when the capillary bed/main channel is not perfused. A shunt/arteriovenous anastomosis is a more direct larger connection.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 16: The Microcirculation and the Lymphatic System

  2. A 58 year old man with a history of long-standing hypertension presents with dyspnea on exertion. Echocardiography confirms reduced left ventricular ejection fraction. Which mechanism best explains the development of pulmonary edema in this patient?

    • A. Increased plasma oncotic pressure
    • B. Increased pulmonary capillary hydrostatic pressure
    • C. Increased lymphatic drainage
    • D. Decreased pulmonary venous resistance
    • E. Decreased alveolar surface tension
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Increased pulmonary capillary hydrostatic pressure

    Left ventricular systolic failure raises left atrial and pulmonary venous pressure, which is transmitted back to increase pulmonary capillary hydrostatic pressure. When this exceeds plasma oncotic pressure, fluid transudes into interstitium and alveoli (Starling forces), producing pulmonary oedema and dyspnoea. Increased oncotic pressure or lymphatic drainage would protect against oedema, and alveolar surface tension is not the primary mechanism.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 16: The Microcirculation and the Lymphatic System

  3. A 70 year old with right heart failure develops ankle edema. Which mechanism best explains this?

    • A. Increased systemic venous hydrostatic pressure
    • B. Decreased plasma oncotic pressure
    • C. Increased lymphatic drainage
    • D. Decreased systemic vascular resistance
    • E. Increased capillary permeability
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Increased systemic venous hydrostatic pressure

    Right heart failure raises systemic venous pressure, which increases capillary hydrostatic pressure throughout the systemic circulation. By Starling forces more fluid is filtered than reabsorbed, producing dependent pitting oedema, earliest at the ankles in an ambulant patient. Increased oncotic pressure or lymphatic drainage would reduce oedema, and this is a hydrostatic, not primarily permeability, mechanism.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 16: The Microcirculation and the Lymphatic System

  4. A deep vein thrombosis blocks venous return from the leg. Consequent change is:

    • A. Increased capillary hydrostatic pressure
    • B. Decreased filtration from capillaries
    • C. Increased plasma oncotic pressure
    • D. Decreased interstitial fluid volume
    • E. Decreased venous hydrostatic pressure
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Increased capillary hydrostatic pressure

    Blocking venous outflow by deep vein thrombosis raises pressure downstream in venules and capillaries. Increased capillary hydrostatic pressure shifts Starling forces toward filtration, producing local oedema. Plasma oncotic pressure does not fall from venous block alone, and lymphatic drainage is initially increased, not the cause of the pressure rise.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 16: The Microcirculation and the Lymphatic System

  5. Despite wide fluctuations in pulmonary capillary hydrostatic pressure, the lungs normally resist edema formation because of which safety factor?

    • A. Negative intrapleural pressure that prevents fluid transudation
    • B. High pulmonary capillary hydrostatic pressure compared with interstitial pressure
    • C. Efficient lymphatic drainage that removes filtered fluid from interstitium
    • D. Greater colloid osmotic pressure of interstitial fluid than plasma
    • E. Tight junctions in pulmonary capillary endothelium that allow no fluid movement
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Efficient lymphatic drainage that removes filtered fluid from interstitium

    Pulmonary lymphatics have a large reserve capacity and continuously remove filtered interstitial fluid, proteins, and particles, keeping alveoli dry despite ongoing Starling filtration. This safety factor, together with low capillary pressure and tight epithelium, resists oedema until pressures rise greatly or lymphatics are overwhelmed. Negative interstitial pressure alone does not remove fluid.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 16: The Microcirculation and the Lymphatic System

  6. In acute hemorrhage, compensatory arteriolar vasoconstriction helps to sustain systemic arterial pressure. What is the most likely consequent change at the capillary level?

    • A. Rise in capillary hydrostatic pressure with enhanced filtration
    • B. Fall in capillary hydrostatic pressure promoting absorption
    • C. Elevation of plasma oncotic pressure with greater absorption
    • D. Increased permeability of the capillary wall
    • E. Rise in lymphatic flow due to augmented interstitial pressure
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Fall in capillary hydrostatic pressure promoting absorption

    Compensatory arteriolar vasoconstriction in haemorrhage reduces capillary hydrostatic pressure downstream. With lower filtration pressure, Starling forces favour reabsorption of interstitial fluid into capillaries (autotransfusion), helping restore plasma volume while arterial pressure is defended by increased resistance. Capillary pressure falls, not rises, distal to constricted arterioles.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 16: The Microcirculation and the Lymphatic System

  7. A patient has severe generalized edema with recurrent infections due to low plasma albumin. Edema in hypoalbuminemia occurs mainly because of:

    • A. Lymphatic obstruction only
    • B. Increased capillary permeability only
    • C. Decreased plasma colloid osmotic (oncotic) pressure
    • D. Increased capillary hydrostatic pressure only
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Decreased plasma colloid osmotic (oncotic) pressure

    Albumin is the main determinant of plasma oncotic pressure; when it falls (nephrotic syndrome, malnutrition, liver failure), fluid leaves capillaries and edema forms. Infections follow loss of immunoglobulins in the same conditions.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 16: The Microcirculation and the Lymphatic System

  8. A person with severe albumin deficiency develops generalized edema mainly because of:

    • A. Increased capillary hydrostatic pressure
    • B. Lymphatic obstruction
    • C. Decreased plasma colloid osmotic (oncotic) pressure
    • D. Increased capillary permeability to plasma proteins
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Decreased plasma colloid osmotic (oncotic) pressure

    Albumin is the main plasma protein maintaining colloid osmotic pressure, which holds fluid inside capillaries and reabsorbs it at the venular end. When albumin falls, oncotic pressure drops, less fluid is reabsorbed, and fluid accumulates in the interstitium as edema. This is the Starling-force mechanism, separate from raised hydrostatic pressure or lymphatic blockage.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 16: The Microcirculation and the Lymphatic System

  9. Edema in heart failure develops mainly due to increased:

    • A. Plasma oncotic pressure
    • B. Lymphatic drainage above normal
    • C. Capillary hydrostatic pressure
    • D. Capillary permeability decrease
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Capillary hydrostatic pressure

    In heart failure, venous congestion raises capillary hydrostatic pressure, forcing excess fluid into interstitium (Starling forces). Plasma oncotic pressure would need to fall (as in nephrotic syndrome/kwashiorkor) for that mechanism.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 16: The Microcirculation and the Lymphatic System

  10. The main function of the lymphatic system in the tissues is to:

    • A. Generate action potentials in tissues
    • B. Produce red blood cells
    • C. Filter arterial blood entering capillaries
    • D. Clear excess interstitial fluid and protein and return them to the systemic blood
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Clear excess interstitial fluid and protein and return them to the systemic blood

    Lymphatics drain excess filtered fluid, plasma proteins and particulate matter from the interstitium and return them to the venous blood. Failure of this drainage causes lymphedema.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 16: The Microcirculation and the Lymphatic System

  11. Tissue edema most characteristically results from a combination of:

    • A. Increased capillary hydrostatic pressure and decreased plasma oncotic pressure
    • B. Increased lymphatic drainage and decreased capillary permeability
    • C. Decreased hydrostatic pressure and increased plasma oncotic pressure
    • D. Venoconstriction alone
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Increased capillary hydrostatic pressure and decreased plasma oncotic pressure

    Raised hydrostatic pressure pushes more fluid out of capillaries while low plasma albumin reduces the oncotic force holding fluid in, so fluid accumulates in tissues. Increased lymphatic drainage would oppose, not cause, edema.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 16: The Microcirculation and the Lymphatic System
