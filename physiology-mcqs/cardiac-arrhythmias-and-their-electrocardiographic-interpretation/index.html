Cardiac Arrhythmias and Their Electrocardiographic Interpretation MCQs for FMU 1st Year MBBS Physiology

Home › Physiology MCQs › Cardiac Arrhythmias and Their Electrocardiographic Interpretation

A cardiac arrhythmia is any rhythm of the heart that differs from the normal sinus rhythm. Arrhythmias arise when impulses form in the wrong place, travel by abnormal routes, or are blocked on their way through the conducting system. The electrocardiogram shows these disturbances as changes in rate, rhythm, and the shape of the waves.

  1. First-degree AV block is defined as:

    • A. Complete AV dissociation
    • B. Wenckebach pattern
    • C. Dropped QRS complexes
    • D. Bundle branch block
    • E. PR interval prolonged beyond 0.20 s with every P wave still conducted
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: E. PR interval prolonged beyond 0.20 s with every P wave still conducted

    First-degree block is simply a slow AV node: every atrial impulse still reaches the ventricles, but transit exceeds 0.20 s. It is usually benign (athletes, beta-blockers, vagal tone) and needs no treatment — but it warns that the node is diseased and higher blocks may follow.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 13: Cardiac Arrhythmias and Their Electrocardiographic Interpretation

  2. Mobitz type I (Wenckebach) second-degree AV block is characterized by:

    • A. Complete AV dissociation
    • B. Progressive lengthening of the PR interval until a QRS is dropped, usually at the AV node level
    • C. Wide bizarre QRS complexes
    • D. Short PR with delta wave
    • E. Constant PR with sudden dropped beats
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Progressive lengthening of the PR interval until a QRS is dropped, usually at the AV node level

    In Wenckebach, each successive impulse finds the AV node more refractory, so the PR stretches until one impulse fails entirely (dropped QRS), then the cycle resets. Because the block is within the AV node itself, it is usually benign and often vagally mediated — unlike the infranodal Mobitz II.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 13: Cardiac Arrhythmias and Their Electrocardiographic Interpretation

  3. Compared with Mobitz I, Mobitz type II AV block is more dangerous because:

    • A. It causes tachycardia rather than bradycardia
    • B. The block is below the AV node (infranodal) and often progresses to complete heart block, usually requiring a pacemaker
    • C. It cannot be seen on a surface ECG
    • D. It is always symptomatic
    • E. It responds reliably to atropine
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. The block is below the AV node (infranodal) and often progresses to complete heart block, usually requiring a pacemaker

    Mobitz II block sits below the AV node in the His-Purkinje system, where escape rhythms are slow and unreliable. It can abruptly degenerate into complete heart block with asystole, so unlike Mobitz I it is an indication for a permanent pacemaker even if the patient feels well.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 13: Cardiac Arrhythmias and Their Electrocardiographic Interpretation

  4. In complete (third-degree) AV block:

    • A. The QRS is always narrow
    • B. Atria and ventricles beat independently (AV dissociation), with the atrial rate faster than the ventricular escape rate
    • C. Every P wave is conducted to the ventricles
    • D. The patient is always asymptomatic
    • E. The PR interval is abnormally short
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Atria and ventricles beat independently (AV dissociation), with the atrial rate faster than the ventricular escape rate

    No atrial impulse crosses the blocked AV node, so atria (driven by the SA node, ~70-90/min) and ventricles (driven by a slow escape focus, 15-40/min) beat independently — P waves march through the tracing unrelated to QRS complexes. The slow ventricular rate causes fatigue, syncope, and Stokes-Adams attacks.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 13: Cardiac Arrhythmias and Their Electrocardiographic Interpretation

  5. A 72-year-old man has recurrent syncope. ECG shows P waves marching through QRS complexes at independent rates (atrial 90/min, ventricular 35/min). The diagnosis is:

    • A. Complete (third-degree) heart block
    • B. Sinus tachycardia
    • C. WPW syndrome
    • D. First-degree AV block
    • E. Atrial flutter
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Complete (third-degree) heart block

    P waves at 90/min marching straight through QRS complexes at 35/min with no fixed relationship is textbook AV dissociation of complete heart block. The ventricles are on a slow escape rhythm, explaining the syncope — this patient needs a permanent pacemaker urgently.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 13: Cardiac Arrhythmias and Their Electrocardiographic Interpretation

  6. Atrial flutter typically shows:

    • A. Delta waves
    • B. Absent atrial activity
    • C. Wide QRS tachycardia
    • D. Irregularly irregular rhythm
    • E. Regular sawtooth flutter waves at 250-350/min, often with 2:1 AV conduction
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: E. Regular sawtooth flutter waves at 250-350/min, often with 2:1 AV conduction

    Flutter is a single large re-entrant circuit sweeping the atria at 250-350/min, inscribing continuous sawtooth waves with no flat baseline. The AV node cannot conduct that fast, so typically every second impulse gets through (2:1 conduction, ventricular rate ~150) — a regular tachycardia that is often mistaken for SVT.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 13: Cardiac Arrhythmias and Their Electrocardiographic Interpretation

  7. In atrial fibrillation, the ECG shows:

    • A. Regular sawtooth waves
    • B. Short PR with delta wave
    • C. No distinct P waves, an irregularly irregular ventricular response, and a fine fibrillatory baseline
    • D. Wide regular QRS complexes
    • E. Complete AV dissociation with regular QRS
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. No distinct P waves, an irregularly irregular ventricular response, and a fine fibrillatory baseline

    In AF, multiple chaotic wavelets (400-650/min) replace organized atrial contraction — no P waves, just a wavy fibrillatory baseline, and the AV node is bombarded irregularly, giving the classic irregularly irregular pulse. Loss of atrial kick plus stasis predisposes to thromboembolism, especially stroke.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 13: Cardiac Arrhythmias and Their Electrocardiographic Interpretation

  8. A patient with atrial fibrillation and a rapid ventricular response is given a beta-blocker primarily to:

    • A. Convert AF to sinus rhythm directly
    • B. Shorten the QT interval
    • C. Increase myocardial contractility
    • D. Slow AV nodal conduction and thereby control the ventricular rate
    • E. Dissolve atrial thrombus
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Slow AV nodal conduction and thereby control the ventricular rate

    The danger of AF with rapid conduction is the fast ventricular rate impairing diastolic filling. Beta-blockers (or calcium-channel blockers, digoxin) slow AV nodal transmission so fewer impulses reach the ventricles — rate control. Rhythm control (cardioversion, ablation) is a separate strategy.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 13: Cardiac Arrhythmias and Their Electrocardiographic Interpretation

  9. Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome is characterized on ECG by:

    • A. Complete heart block
    • B. Long PR interval and wide QRS
    • C. Diffuse ST elevation
    • D. Short PR interval (<0.12 s) with a slurred upstroke (delta wave) due to an accessory AV pathway
    • E. Sawtooth flutter waves
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Short PR interval (<0.12 s) with a slurred upstroke (delta wave) due to an accessory AV pathway

    An accessory bundle (bundle of Kent) bypasses the slow AV node, pre-exciting the ventricles: the impulse arrives early (short PR) and starts ventricular depolarization sluggishly through muscle (delta wave) before the normal Purkinje wavefront takes over. This sets up re-entrant tachycardias.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 13: Cardiac Arrhythmias and Their Electrocardiographic Interpretation

  10. The greatest danger of WPW when atrial fibrillation develops is:

    • A. Ischemic stroke only
    • B. Vasodilatory hypotension
    • C. High-grade AV block
    • D. Rapid conduction down the accessory pathway precipitating ventricular fibrillation
    • E. Severe bradycardia
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Rapid conduction down the accessory pathway precipitating ventricular fibrillation

    The accessory pathway has a short refractory period and can conduct atrial impulses at 250-300/min straight to the ventricles, degenerating into ventricular fibrillation. That is why AV-nodal blockers alone are dangerous in pre-excited AF — definitive treatment is ablation of the pathway.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 13: Cardiac Arrhythmias and Their Electrocardiographic Interpretation

  11. Ventricular fibrillation is immediately life-threatening because:

    • A. The heart rate is too slow
    • B. It always converts spontaneously to sinus rhythm
    • C. Chaotic ventricular activation produces no coordinated contraction and no cardiac output
    • D. It causes severe hypertension
    • E. The atria stop contracting
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Chaotic ventricular activation produces no coordinated contraction and no cardiac output

    In VF, countless re-entrant wavelets make ventricular fibers contract chaotically and independently — the ventricle quivers instead of pumping, so cardiac output is zero and death follows in minutes. It is the most common terminal rhythm in sudden cardiac death, reversed only by defibrillation.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 13: Cardiac Arrhythmias and Their Electrocardiographic Interpretation

  12. A 60-year-old man collapses. He is pulseless with ventricular fibrillation on the monitor. The most urgent treatment is:

    • A. Immediate electrical defibrillation
    • B. Transcutaneous pacing
    • C. Atropine
    • D. Beta-blockers
    • E. Aspirin
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Immediate electrical defibrillation

    A pulseless patient in VF has no circulation; only a synchronized... actually unsynchronized high-energy shock (defibrillation) can simultaneously depolarize the entire myocardium and let the SA node re-establish order. Every minute of delay cuts survival sharply — drugs and pacing are secondary.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 13: Cardiac Arrhythmias and Their Electrocardiographic Interpretation

  13. Ventricular premature contractions are recognized by:

    • A. Sawtooth baseline
    • B. Early narrow QRS preceded by a P wave
    • C. Delta waves
    • D. Premature wide, bizarre QRS complexes usually followed by a compensatory pause
    • E. Regular narrow tachycardia
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Premature wide, bizarre QRS complexes usually followed by a compensatory pause

    A ventricular ectopic focus fires early; the impulse spreads slowly through muscle (wide, bizarre QRS, discordant T) and the ventricle is then refractory when the next sinus impulse arrives, creating the full compensatory pause. Frequent PVCs after MI warn of an irritable, VF-prone myocardium.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 13: Cardiac Arrhythmias and Their Electrocardiographic Interpretation

  14. Paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia due to AV nodal re-entry can often be terminated by:

    • A. Hyperventilation
    • B. Beta-agonist infusion
    • C. Digoxin loading
    • D. Vagal maneuvers or adenosine, which transiently block AV nodal conduction
    • E. Exercise
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Vagal maneuvers or adenosine, which transiently block AV nodal conduction

    AV nodal re-entrant tachycardia depends on the dual AV nodal pathways; transiently blocking the node breaks the circuit. Vagal maneuvers (Valsalva, carotid massage) boost parasympathetic tone, and adenosine briefly asystoles the AV node — both can snap the rhythm back to sinus.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 13: Cardiac Arrhythmias and Their Electrocardiographic Interpretation

  15. Torsades de pointes is a polymorphic ventricular tachycardia associated with:

    • A. Atrial flutter
    • B. First-degree AV block
    • C. Sinus bradycardia alone
    • D. Prolonged QT interval
    • E. Short PR interval
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Prolonged QT interval

    Torsades ('twisting of points') is a polymorphic VT whose QRS amplitude waxes and wanes around the baseline, classically triggered when repolarization is prolonged — congenital long QT, or acquired from drugs (macrolides, antipsychotics), hypokalemia, or hypomagnesemia. Magnesium and QT-shortening measures treat it.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 13: Cardiac Arrhythmias and Their Electrocardiographic Interpretation

  16. Sick sinus syndrome may present with:

    • A. Delta waves
    • B. Wide QRS complexes
    • C. Alternating bradycardia and tachycardia (bradycardia-tachycardia syndrome) with sinus pauses
    • D. Only tachycardia
    • E. A constantly normal rhythm
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Alternating bradycardia and tachycardia (bradycardia-tachycardia syndrome) with sinus pauses

    A diseased SA node may pause or fail (bradycardia) and then allow irritable atrial foci to fire rapidly (tachycardia) — the bradycardia-tachycardia syndrome. Patients get both syncope and palpitations and often need a pacemaker plus antiarrhythmic control.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 13: Cardiac Arrhythmias and Their Electrocardiographic Interpretation

  17. Sinus arrhythmia in a healthy young adult:

    • A. Indicates myocardial infarction
    • B. Is a normal respiratory variation with heart rate increasing on inspiration (decreased vagal tone)
    • C. Requires a pacemaker
    • D. Indicates heart block
    • E. Is always pathologic
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Is a normal respiratory variation with heart rate increasing on inspiration (decreased vagal tone)

    In young people, vagal tone waxes with expiration and wanes with inspiration, so the SA node speeds up as you breathe in and slows as you breathe out — a benign, normal finding reflecting healthy autonomic responsiveness. It vanishes with age, exercise, or autonomic neuropathy.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 13: Cardiac Arrhythmias and Their Electrocardiographic Interpretation

  18. A 30-year-old woman has episodes of rapid palpitations. ECG in sinus rhythm shows a short PR interval and delta waves. The accessory pathway in WPW is called the:

    • A. Moderator band
    • B. Bachmann bundle
    • C. James fibers
    • D. Bundle of His
    • E. Bundle of Kent
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: E. Bundle of Kent

    The bundle of Kent is the accessory muscular connection bridging atrium to ventricle around the fibrous AV ring, bypassing the node. James fibers (atrio-Hisian) and Mahaim fibers are rarer variants; the bundle of His is the normal path, not the accessory one.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 13: Cardiac Arrhythmias and Their Electrocardiographic Interpretation

  19. The most common cause of sudden cardiac death in the first hours after acute MI is:

    • A. Ventricular fibrillation from ischemic, irritable myocardium
    • B. Complete heart block
    • C. Pulmonary embolism
    • D. Papillary muscle rupture
    • E. Aortic dissection
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Ventricular fibrillation from ischemic, irritable myocardium

    Ischemic myocardium is electrically unstable: injury currents and re-entrant circuits readily degenerate into VF within the first hours after coronary occlusion. This is why MI patients are monitored in coronary care units — most sudden deaths are arrhythmic, not pump failure, and are preventable with prompt defibrillation.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 13: Cardiac Arrhythmias and Their Electrocardiographic Interpretation

  20. Stokes-Adams attacks (syncope in complete heart block) result from:

    • A. Hypoglycemia
    • B. Hypertension
    • C. A primary seizure disorder
    • D. Transient failure of ventricular escape, causing seconds of asystole and cerebral hypoperfusion
    • E. Atrial tachycardia
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Transient failure of ventricular escape, causing seconds of asystole and cerebral hypoperfusion

    When the ventricular escape rhythm transiently fails in complete heart block, the heart stands still for seconds — no output, no cerebral flow — and the patient collapses, then recovers when escape resumes. Recurrent Stokes-Adams attacks are a classic indication for permanent pacing.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 13: Cardiac Arrhythmias and Their Electrocardiographic Interpretation
