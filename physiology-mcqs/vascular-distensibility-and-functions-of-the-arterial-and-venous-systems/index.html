Vascular Distensibility and Functions of the Arterial and Venous Systems MCQs for FMU 1st Year MBBS Physiology

Home › Physiology MCQs › Vascular Distensibility and Functions of the Arterial and Venous Systems

Arteries are relatively stiff tubes that carry blood at high pressure, while veins are highly distensible and act as a reservoir that stores most of the blood. Because veins stretch easily, changes in venous volume have only a small effect on venous pressure. The arterial system delivers blood to the tissues, and the venous system returns it to the heart.

  1. Pulse pressure is defined as:

    • A. Heart rate times stroke volume
    • B. Diastolic minus systolic pressure
    • C. Central venous pressure
    • D. Systolic pressure minus diastolic pressure
    • E. Mean arterial pressure
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Systolic pressure minus diastolic pressure

    Pulse pressure is the phasic swing — systolic minus diastolic. It reflects how much pressure each stroke volume generates in the arterial tree: a big stroke volume into stiff arteries gives a wide pulse; a small stroke volume gives a narrow one.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 15: Vascular Distensibility and Functions of the Arterial and Venous Systems

  2. Pulse pressure is directly proportional to stroke volume and inversely proportional to:

    • A. Blood viscosity
    • B. Body weight
    • C. Arterial compliance
    • D. Heart rate
    • E. Venous return
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Arterial compliance

    Each systole injects the stroke volume into the arteries; compliant vessels stretch and absorb it (small pressure rise), stiff vessels cannot (large rise). Hence PP ≈ SV/arterial compliance — wide in aging and aortic regurgitation, narrow in shock or aortic stenosis.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 15: Vascular Distensibility and Functions of the Arterial and Venous Systems

  3. An 80-year-old man has blood pressure 170/70 mmHg. The wide pulse pressure is best explained by:

    • A. Hyperthyroidism
    • B. Age-related loss of arterial compliance (stiff, non-distensible arteries)
    • C. Aortic regurgitation
    • D. Severe anemia
    • E. Increased stroke volume
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Age-related loss of arterial compliance (stiff, non-distensible arteries)

    With aging, elastin fragments and arteries stiffen (arteriosclerosis). A normal stroke volume then spikes systolic pressure high, while rapid diastolic runoff into the stiff tree drops diastolic pressure — the classic isolated systolic hypertension of the elderly (wide PP, e.g., 170/70).

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 15: Vascular Distensibility and Functions of the Arterial and Venous Systems

  4. Mean arterial pressure can be estimated as:

    • A. Diastolic pressure + one-third of pulse pressure
    • B. Systolic pressure minus pulse pressure
    • C. (Systolic + diastolic) / 2
    • D. Systolic + diastolic pressure
    • E. Cardiac output × heart rate
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Diastolic pressure + one-third of pulse pressure

    Because diastole lasts about twice as long as systole, MAP sits closer to diastolic: MAP ≈ DBP + PP/3 (equivalently, (SBP + 2·DBP)/3). It is the true driving pressure for organ perfusion — a MAP below ~60 mmHg threatens brain and kidney flow.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 15: Vascular Distensibility and Functions of the Arterial and Venous Systems

  5. A blood pressure of 120/80 mmHg gives a mean arterial pressure of approximately:

    • A. 120 mmHg
    • B. 100 mmHg
    • C. 80 mmHg
    • D. 93 mmHg
    • E. 40 mmHg
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. 93 mmHg

    PP = 120 − 80 = 40; MAP = 80 + 40/3 ≈ 93 mmHg. This quick bedside formula is used constantly — for example, judging perfusion pressure in shock or titrating vasopressors in the ICU.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 15: Vascular Distensibility and Functions of the Arterial and Venous Systems

  6. Aortic regurgitation produces a wide pulse pressure because:

    • A. Arterial compliance increases
    • B. Afterload falls
    • C. A large stroke volume is ejected while blood runs back into the ventricle during diastole, lowering diastolic pressure
    • D. Heart rate slows markedly
    • E. Stroke volume decreases
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. A large stroke volume is ejected while blood runs back into the ventricle during diastole, lowering diastolic pressure

    The regurgitant valve lets the ventricle eject an abnormally large total stroke volume (forward + regurgitant), spiking systolic pressure; in diastole, blood drains back into the ventricle, so aortic diastolic pressure plummets. The result is the bounding Corrigan (water-hammer) pulse.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 15: Vascular Distensibility and Functions of the Arterial and Venous Systems

  7. The arterial pulse is damped and disappears by the time blood reaches the capillaries. This is physiologically important because:

    • A. It increases mean flow
    • B. It protects the thin-walled capillaries from pulsatile pressure swings
    • C. It raises diastolic pressure
    • D. It increases capillary filtration
    • E. It is merely a measurement artifact
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. It protects the thin-walled capillaries from pulsatile pressure swings

    Arterial compliance plus arteriolar resistance act as a hydraulic filter (Windkessel), smoothing systolic peaks into near-steady capillary flow. Capillaries have paper-thin walls built for exchange, not pressure — pulsatile hammering would rupture them and make filtration chaotic.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 15: Vascular Distensibility and Functions of the Arterial and Venous Systems

  8. The dicrotic notch on the aortic pressure curve represents:

    • A. Opening of the aortic valve
    • B. Closure of the aortic valve
    • C. Opening of the mitral valve
    • D. The P wave of the ECG
    • E. Atrial systole
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Closure of the aortic valve

    At end-systole the aortic valve snaps shut; blood briefly rebounds against the closed leaflets, inscribing a small secondary wave (dicrotic notch) on the descending aortic pressure tracing. It precisely marks the systole-diastole boundary on arterial waveforms.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 15: Vascular Distensibility and Functions of the Arterial and Venous Systems

  9. Venous return from the legs during walking is aided mainly by:

    • A. Increased arterial pressure
    • B. Decreased respiratory rate
    • C. Gravity alone
    • D. The skeletal muscle pump compressing veins, with valves preventing backflow
    • E. Reflex vasodilation
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. The skeletal muscle pump compressing veins, with valves preventing backflow

    Calf-muscle contraction squeezes the deep leg veins; one-way valves ensure the displaced blood moves only toward the heart, and refilling occurs from the superficial system. This muscle pump is the main anti-gravity mechanism for venous return during locomotion — its failure causes venous stasis.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 15: Vascular Distensibility and Functions of the Arterial and Venous Systems

  10. During inspiration, venous return to the right heart:

    • A. Stops completely
    • B. Reverses direction
    • C. Increases, because falling intrathoracic pressure expands thoracic veins and lowers right atrial pressure
    • D. Is unchanged
    • E. Decreases
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Increases, because falling intrathoracic pressure expands thoracic veins and lowers right atrial pressure

    Inspiration drops intrathoracic (and right atrial) pressure while raising abdominal pressure — squeezing abdominal veins and sucking thoracic blood centrally. This respiratory pump can substantially boost venous return during deep breathing or exercise.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 15: Vascular Distensibility and Functions of the Arterial and Venous Systems

  11. A soldier standing rigidly at attention for an hour may faint because:

    • A. Blood pressure rises sharply
    • B. Dehydration alone
    • C. Heart rate increases excessively
    • D. The arteries constrict
    • E. Venous pooling in the legs reduces venous return and cardiac output, lowering cerebral perfusion
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: E. Venous pooling in the legs reduces venous return and cardiac output, lowering cerebral perfusion

    Motionless standing lets blood pool in the compliant leg veins (muscle pump idle), so venous return and cardiac output sag; cerebral perfusion falls and the soldier faints — restoring the horizontal position (and leg movement) rapidly corrects it. Marching in place prevents it.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 15: Vascular Distensibility and Functions of the Arterial and Venous Systems

  12. Normal central venous (right atrial) pressure is approximately:

    • A. -20 mmHg
    • B. 100 mmHg
    • C. 25 mmHg
    • D. 0 mmHg (range 0-2 mmHg)
    • E. 80 mmHg
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. 0 mmHg (range 0-2 mmHg)

    Right atrial pressure — the downstream pressure for the entire systemic venous return — is normally ~0 mmHg (0-2). Elevation (jugular venous distension, hepatojugular reflux) signals right heart failure or volume overload; a negative value suggests hypovolemia.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 15: Vascular Distensibility and Functions of the Arterial and Venous Systems

  13. Delayed compliance (stress relaxation) of vessels means that:

    • A. Compliance decreases with prolonged stretch
    • B. Veins collapse irreversibly
    • C. Vessels constrict immediately and permanently
    • D. Arteries rupture under load
    • E. A vessel stretched by sustained pressure gradually yields and accommodates more volume
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: E. A vessel stretched by sustained pressure gradually yields and accommodates more volume

    Like a slowly stretching rubber band, a vessel held at high pressure gradually creeps to a larger volume (stress relaxation); when pressure is then lowered, it stays transiently distended (reverse stress relaxation). This delayed compliance buffers long-term pressure changes, e.g., after transfusion.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 15: Vascular Distensibility and Functions of the Arterial and Venous Systems

  14. Compared with arteries, the systemic veins are:

    • A. Thicker-walled than arteries
    • B. Rigid and non-collapsible
    • C. Less compliant than arteries
    • D. Far more distensible and compliant (about eight times), serving as the major blood reservoir
    • E. A higher-pressure system
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Far more distensible and compliant (about eight times), serving as the major blood reservoir

    Veins have thin walls with little elastin and are about eight times as distensible as arteries, so small pressure rises store large volumes — the venous system holds ~64% of blood and functions as the adjustable reservoir the sympathetic system taps in shock or exercise.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 15: Vascular Distensibility and Functions of the Arterial and Venous Systems

  15. Sympathetic stimulation mobilizes blood from the venous reservoir by:

    • A. Dilating the arterioles
    • B. Venoconstriction, decreasing venous compliance and shifting blood centrally
    • C. Slowing the heart rate
    • D. Venodilation
    • E. Increasing capillary filtration
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Venoconstriction, decreasing venous compliance and shifting blood centrally

    Sympathetic venoconstriction reduces venous compliance (the pressure-volume curve steepens), so the same blood volume generates higher venous pressure — raising mean systemic filling pressure and driving more blood centrally. This autotransfusion supports cardiac output without any IV fluids.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 15: Vascular Distensibility and Functions of the Arterial and Venous Systems

  16. In severe right heart failure, the neck veins are distended because:

    • A. Anemia develops
    • B. Venous valves fail acutely
    • C. Elevated right atrial (central venous) pressure is transmitted to the jugular veins
    • D. Arterial pressure rises
    • E. Hypovolemia
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Elevated right atrial (central venous) pressure is transmitted to the jugular veins

    The jugular veins are manometers for right atrial pressure: when the failing right ventricle cannot accept venous return, central venous pressure rises and the column of blood backs up visibly into the neck. Hepatojugular reflux (pressing the liver raises JVP) confirms the finding.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 15: Vascular Distensibility and Functions of the Arterial and Venous Systems

  17. Varicose veins result mainly from:

    • A. Local infection
    • B. Systemic hypertension
    • C. Incompetent venous valves allowing backflow and chronic venous distension
    • D. Arterial occlusive disease
    • E. Sympathetic overactivity
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Incompetent venous valves allowing backflow and chronic venous distension

    Varicose veins begin with valvular incompetence — often after prolonged standing, pregnancy, or thrombosis. Without competent valves, the muscle pump cannot prevent backflow; hydrostatic pressure distends the superficial veins into tortuous, dilated varicosities.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 15: Vascular Distensibility and Functions of the Arterial and Venous Systems

  18. When a person stands, the hydrostatic (gravitational) pressure in the veins of the feet:

    • A. Becomes negative
    • B. Rises by about 90 mmHg due to the blood column from heart to feet
    • C. Remains at 0 mmHg
    • D. Equals mean aortic pressure
    • E. Falls to zero
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Rises by about 90 mmHg due to the blood column from heart to feet

    Hydrostatic pressure = ρgh: a ~120 cm blood column from heart to feet adds roughly 90 mmHg when standing. This is why venous pressure at the ankle is ~90-100 mmHg upright (demanding competent valves) but near 10 mmHg supine.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 15: Vascular Distensibility and Functions of the Arterial and Venous Systems

  19. Severe aortic stenosis tends to produce a:

    • A. Narrow pulse pressure because stroke volume is limited by the fixed obstruction
    • B. Wide pulse pressure
    • C. Normal pulse pressure
    • D. Absent arterial pulse
    • E. Isolated high diastolic pressure
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Narrow pulse pressure because stroke volume is limited by the fixed obstruction

    The stenotic valve throttles ejection, so stroke volume — and the systolic surge it produces — falls, while diastolic pressure is preserved. The small stroke volume into the arteries yields a narrow pulse pressure (e.g., 100/85), opposite to the wide pulse of aortic regurgitation.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 15: Vascular Distensibility and Functions of the Arterial and Venous Systems

  20. The veins of the neck collapse when a healthy person stands because:

    • A. The carotid arteries compress them
    • B. The muscle pump fails
    • C. Venous valves snap shut
    • D. Venous pressure at the neck falls below atmospheric due to gravity, and the thin walls collapse
    • E. Venous pressure rises above atmospheric
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Venous pressure at the neck falls below atmospheric due to gravity, and the thin walls collapse

    Above heart level, gravity subtracts from venous pressure; at the neck it falls below atmospheric, and the floppy thin-walled jugulars collapse — which is why neck veins are flat in healthy standing people but distend when central venous pressure rises in heart failure.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 15: Vascular Distensibility and Functions of the Arterial and Venous Systems

  21. Veins are called capacitance (reservoir) vessels mainly because:

    • A. They have the thickest smooth muscle layer in the circulation
    • B. They are the principal site of nutrient exchange with tissues
    • C. They generate the highest pressures in the circulation
    • D. They contain the largest fraction of total blood volume and can constrict to shift blood centrally
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. They contain the largest fraction of total blood volume and can constrict to shift blood centrally

    Veins are highly distensible and normally hold well over half the blood volume; sympathetic venoconstriction reduces this reservoir and supports venous return and arterial pressure. Exchange occurs in capillaries, the highest pressures are in arteries, and veins have thinner walls than arteries of similar size.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Vascular Distensibility and Functions of the Arterial and Venous Systems
