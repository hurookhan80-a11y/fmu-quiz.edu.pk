Regulation of Respiration MCQs for FMU 1st Year MBBS Physiology

Home › Physiology MCQs › Regulation of Respiration

Breathing is regulated mainly by centres in the brainstem that set the rhythm and depth of respiration. These centres respond to signals from the body, especially the level of carbon dioxide and the acidity of the blood, and to stretch receptors in the lungs. Higher brain centres can also modify breathing voluntarily or during speech and emotion.

  1. A child rebreathes air from a wrapper/bag over the mouth. Which change will increase the ventilation rate?

    • A. Increased alveolar pCO2
    • B. Decreased blood H+
    • C. Increased alveolar pO2
    • D. Decreased alveolar pCO2
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Increased alveolar pCO2

    Rebreathing raises alveolar and arterial pCO2, which stimulates the central chemoreceptors. Ventilation therefore increases to blow off the excess carbon dioxide.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Regulation of Respiration

  2. A child rebreathes exhaled air trapped in a wrapper over the mouth. The ventilation rate will increase mainly because of:

    • A. Increased alveolar PO2
    • B. Decreased arterial H+ concentration
    • C. Increased alveolar PCO2
    • D. Decreased alveolar PCO2
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Increased alveolar PCO2

    Rebreathing raises alveolar and arterial PCO2, which stimulates central chemoreceptors and increases ventilation. A fall in PCO2 would have the opposite effect and depress breathing.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Regulation of Respiration

  3. A crescendo-decrescendo (waxing and waning) breathing pattern with periods of apnea is called:

    • A. Kussmaul breathing
    • B. Biot breathing
    • C. Cheyne-Stokes respiration
    • D. Apneustic breathing
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Cheyne-Stokes respiration

    Cheyne-Stokes respiration shows gradual increase then decrease in tidal volume followed by apnea, seen in heart failure and raised intracranial pressure. Kussmaul breathing is deep rapid breathing of metabolic acidosis without apneic pauses.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Regulation of Respiration

  4. Central and peripheral chemoreceptors regulating ventilation are most strongly affected by changes in:

    • A. PaO2 only at normal CO2
    • B. Blood glucose
    • C. PaCO2 (via H+ in CSF/blood)
    • D. Plasma sodium
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. PaCO2 (via H+ in CSF/blood)

    Ventilation is controlled mainly by CO2: central chemoreceptors respond to H+ from CO2 in cerebrospinal fluid, and peripheral chemoreceptors also respond strongly to CO2/H+. Oxygen becomes the dominant drive only when PaO2 falls below about 60 mmHg.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Regulation of Respiration

  5. In central (cardiac) failure, delayed transport of blood to the brain can produce the breathing pattern called:

    • A. Kussmaul breathing
    • B. Cheyne-Stokes breathing
    • C. Biot breathing
    • D. Apneustic breathing
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Cheyne-Stokes breathing

    In severe heart failure, slow circulation delays feedback of blood-gas changes from the lungs to the brain, causing cyclical waxing and waning of breathing - Cheyne-Stokes respiration. Kussmaul breathing is the deep rapid breathing of metabolic acidosis.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Regulation of Respiration

  6. At high altitude, the low arterial PCO2 produced by hyperventilation tends to limit further breathing because low PCO2:

    • A. Stimulates peripheral chemoreceptors strongly
    • B. Has an inhibitory (opposing) action on the chemoreceptor drive to the respiratory centre
    • C. Directly paralyses the ventral respiratory group
    • D. Raises blood pH so haemoglobin releases no oxygen
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Has an inhibitory (opposing) action on the chemoreceptor drive to the respiratory centre

    Hyperventilation at altitude lowers PCO2 and raises pH, which inhibits central and peripheral chemoreceptors and opposes the hypoxic drive from the ventral respiratory neurones. Over days, renal and CSF bicarbonate adjustments reduce this braking effect (acclimatisation).

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Regulation of Respiration

  7. The pneumotaxic centre in the pons mainly:

    • A. Initiates inspiration
    • B. Limits inspiration and controls the inspiratory off-switch
    • C. Stimulates the inspiratory ramp continuously
    • D. Responds only to blood glucose levels
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Limits inspiration and controls the inspiratory off-switch

    The pneumotaxic centre transmits signals that switch off the inspiratory ramp, thus limiting inspiration and regulating respiratory rate. The dorsal respiratory group in the medulla generates the basic inspiratory rhythm.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Regulation of Respiration

  8. The ventral respiratory group of neurones is located in the:

    • A. Pons
    • B. Medulla oblongata
    • C. Hypothalamus
    • D. Cerebral cortex
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Medulla oblongata

    The ventral respiratory group lies in the medulla (nucleus ambiguus/retroambiguus region) and drives especially expiratory muscles during heavy breathing. The pneumotaxic and apneustic centres are in the pons.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Regulation of Respiration

  9. The dorsal respiratory group neurones that receive sensory information via the 9th and 10th cranial nerves are clustered in the:

    • A. Nucleus tractus solitarius of the medulla
    • B. Red nucleus of the midbrain
    • C. Dentate nucleus of the cerebellum
    • D. Caudate nucleus
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Nucleus tractus solitarius of the medulla

    Vagal and glossopharyngeal afferents from lungs, airways and chemoreceptors terminate in the nucleus tractus solitarius, where the dorsal respiratory group neurones lie. This group generates the basic inspiratory rhythm.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Regulation of Respiration

  10. If both vagus nerves are cut, what is the effect on breathing?

    • A. Breathing ceases immediately
    • B. Breathing is no longer influenced at all
    • C. Respiratory rate increases with shallow breaths
    • D. Respiratory rate decreases with deeper breaths
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Respiratory rate decreases with deeper breaths

    Vagal afferents from pulmonary stretch receptors mediate the Hering-Breuer inflation reflex that terminates inspiration. After bilateral vagotomy inspiration is prolonged, so breathing becomes slow and deep; rhythm continues via medullary centres.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology — Regulation of Respiration

  11. Lungs are prevented from over-stretching (over-inflation) by:

    • A. Carotid baroreflex
    • B. Hering-Breuer reflex
    • C. Bainbridge reflex
    • D. Cushing reflex
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Hering-Breuer reflex

    Stretch receptors in airway smooth muscle send vagal afferents that inhibit inspiration when lungs over-inflate—the Hering-Breuer inflation reflex. Baroreflexes regulate blood pressure, not lung volume.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology — Regulation of Respiration
