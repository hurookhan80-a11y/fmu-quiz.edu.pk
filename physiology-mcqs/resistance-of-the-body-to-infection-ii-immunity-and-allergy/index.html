Home › Physiology MCQs › Resistance of the Body to Infection: II. Immunity and Allergy

Immunity is the body's ability to recognise and resist specific invading organisms and foreign substances. Lymphocytes produce antibodies and killer cells that remember past encounters, so a second exposure is met with a faster and stronger response. Allergy occurs when the immune system reacts excessively to a substance that is normally harmless.

  1. A newborn receives protective antibodies through breast milk (IgA) and across the placenta (IgG). This is an example of:

    • A. Passive artificial immunity
    • B. Active natural immunity
    • C. Innate immunity only
    • D. Active artificial immunity
    • E. Passive natural immunity
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: E. Passive natural immunity

    Passive immunity means receiving ready-made antibodies rather than making them: natural when transferred from mother to child (placental IgG, breast-milk IgA), artificial when injected as antiserum or immunoglobulin. It gives immediate but temporary protection, unlike active immunity, which is slower but long-lasting.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 35: Resistance of the Body to Infection: II. Immunity and Allergy

  2. The clonal selection theory of immunity states that:

    • A. T cells produce antibodies
    • B. Each lymphocyte is pre-committed to one antigen; the antigen selects and expands the matching clone
    • C. Memory cells never divide
    • D. All lymphocytes respond to all antigens
    • E. Antibodies instruct lymphocytes what to make
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Each lymphocyte is pre-committed to one antigen; the antigen selects and expands the matching clone

    Each B or T lymphocyte expresses receptors for a single antigenic specificity, generated randomly during development. When its matching antigen appears, that clone is selected to proliferate and differentiate into effector and memory cells — explaining both specificity and the faster, stronger secondary response.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 35: Resistance of the Body to Infection: II. Immunity and Allergy

  3. Helper T lymphocytes (CD4+) are central to immune responses mainly because they:

    • A. Suppress all immune activity
    • B. Produce antibodies themselves
    • C. Secrete lymphokines that activate B cells, cytotoxic T cells, and macrophages
    • D. Form the blood-thymus barrier
    • E. Directly kill virus-infected cells
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Secrete lymphokines that activate B cells, cytotoxic T cells, and macrophages

    CD4+ helper T cells recognize antigen presented on MHC class II molecules and release cytokines (e.g., interleukins) that orchestrate the response — helping B cells make antibody, activating cytotoxic T cells and macrophages. Their destruction by HIV explains the profound immunodeficiency of AIDS.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 35: Resistance of the Body to Infection: II. Immunity and Allergy

  4. Cytotoxic (CD8+) T lymphocytes kill their targets by:

    • A. Producing IgE
    • B. Activating complement only
    • C. Recognizing antigen on MHC class I molecules and releasing perforin and granzymes that induce apoptosis
    • D. Releasing histamine
    • E. Phagocytosing whole cells
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Recognizing antigen on MHC class I molecules and releasing perforin and granzymes that induce apoptosis

    CD8+ cytotoxic T cells scan MHC class I molecules (present on all nucleated cells) for foreign or abnormal peptides — from viruses, intracellular bacteria, or tumors. On recognition they punch holes with perforin and inject granzymes, triggering programmed cell death of the infected cell.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 35: Resistance of the Body to Infection: II. Immunity and Allergy

  5. IgG is unique among antibody classes in that it:

    • A. Mediates allergy
    • B. Crosses the placenta to protect the newborn
    • C. Is a pentamer
    • D. Cannot activate complement
    • E. Exists only in secretions
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Crosses the placenta to protect the newborn

    IgG, the most abundant circulating immunoglobulin, is actively transported across the placenta, giving the neonate months of passive protection. It also opsonizes microbes, neutralizes toxins, and activates complement — making it the workhorse antibody of secondary immune responses.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 35: Resistance of the Body to Infection: II. Immunity and Allergy

  6. Secretory IgA is especially important for defending:

    • A. The placenta
    • B. The brain
    • C. Mucosal surfaces of the gut, airways, and breast milk
    • D. Bone marrow
    • E. The bloodstream
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Mucosal surfaces of the gut, airways, and breast milk

    IgA is secreted across mucosal epithelia into the gut, respiratory tract, tears, saliva, and breast milk, where it neutralizes pathogens before they invade — 'immune exclusion.' Breast-milk IgA protects the infant gut, complementing the systemic protection of transplacental IgG.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 35: Resistance of the Body to Infection: II. Immunity and Allergy

  7. In a primary immune response, the FIRST antibody class to appear is:

    • A. IgA
    • B. IgE
    • C. IgG
    • D. IgM
    • E. IgD
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. IgM

    Naive B cells first secrete pentameric IgM — a potent complement activator — before class-switching to IgG (or IgA/IgE) with T-cell help. Detecting pathogen-specific IgM therefore indicates recent or acute infection, whereas IgG reflects past exposure or immunization.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 35: Resistance of the Body to Infection: II. Immunity and Allergy

  8. IgE is chiefly involved in:

    • A. Complement activation via the classical pathway
    • B. Allergic reactions and defense against parasitic worms
    • C. Neonatal protection
    • D. Mucosal immunity
    • E. Opsonization of bacteria
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Allergic reactions and defense against parasitic worms

    IgE binds Fc receptors on mast cells and basophils; when allergen cross-links it, the cells degranulate, releasing histamine — the basis of type I hypersensitivity. The same mechanism attacks helminth parasites, with eosinophils delivering the lethal hit.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 35: Resistance of the Body to Infection: II. Immunity and Allergy

  9. A 10-year-old boy develops wheezing, hives, and hypotension within minutes of a bee sting. This type I hypersensitivity reaction is mediated by:

    • A. IgE-triggered mast cell degranulation
    • B. Complement deficiency
    • C. Cytotoxic T cells
    • D. Delayed T-cell inflammation
    • E. IgG immune complexes
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. IgE-triggered mast cell degranulation

    Type I (immediate) hypersensitivity occurs when allergen cross-links IgE on mast cells and basophils, causing explosive release of histamine, leukotrienes, and prostaglandins — producing bronchospasm, vasodilation, and vascular leak within minutes. Epinephrine is the life-saving treatment for anaphylaxis.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 35: Resistance of the Body to Infection: II. Immunity and Allergy

  10. A hemolytic transfusion reaction from ABO-incompatible blood is an example of which hypersensitivity type?

    • A. Type I
    • B. Type III
    • C. Type II (cytotoxic, antibody-mediated)
    • D. Type IV
    • E. Type V
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Type II (cytotoxic, antibody-mediated)

    Type II hypersensitivity involves IgG or IgM antibodies binding antigens on cell surfaces — here, donor red cell antigens — leading to complement-mediated lysis and phagocytosis. The same mechanism underlies hemolytic disease of the newborn and autoimmune hemolytic anemia.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 35: Resistance of the Body to Infection: II. Immunity and Allergy

  11. A 25-year-old woman with systemic lupus erythematosus develops glomerulonephritis. The renal injury is best classified as:

    • A. Type II hypersensitivity
    • B. Type III (immune-complex) hypersensitivity
    • C. Type IV hypersensitivity
    • D. Anaphylaxis
    • E. Type I hypersensitivity
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Type III (immune-complex) hypersensitivity

    In SLE, autoantibodies form immune complexes with nuclear antigens; these deposit in small vessels and glomeruli, activating complement and attracting neutrophils that damage tissue — the hallmark of type III hypersensitivity. Serum sickness and some vasculitides share this mechanism.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 35: Resistance of the Body to Infection: II. Immunity and Allergy

  12. A positive Mantoux (tuberculin) skin test, read at 48–72 hours, represents which type of hypersensitivity?

    • A. Type IV (delayed, cell-mediated)
    • B. Type V
    • C. Type I
    • D. Type II
    • E. Type III
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Type IV (delayed, cell-mediated)

    The tuberculin reaction is delayed-type (type IV) hypersensitivity: previously sensitized T cells recognize mycobacterial antigen, release cytokines, and recruit macrophages over 1–3 days, producing induration. Contact dermatitis (e.g., nickel) and granulomatous reactions share this T-cell–macrophage mechanism — no antibodies involved.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 35: Resistance of the Body to Infection: II. Immunity and Allergy

  13. T lymphocytes mature and acquire immunocompetence in the:

    • A. Lymph nodes
    • B. Bone marrow
    • C. Liver
    • D. Spleen
    • E. Thymus
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: E. Thymus

    T-cell precursors migrate from the bone marrow to the thymus, where they rearrange T-cell receptors and undergo positive and negative selection — learning to recognize foreign antigen on self-MHC while becoming tolerant to self. Thymic output is greatest in childhood, which is why the thymus is largest then.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 35: Resistance of the Body to Infection: II. Immunity and Allergy

  14. Memory B and T cells are responsible for:

    • A. Complement activation
    • B. The rapid, stronger secondary response on re-exposure to an antigen
    • C. Innate phagocytosis
    • D. Histamine release
    • E. The slow primary response
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. The rapid, stronger secondary response on re-exposure to an antigen

    Clonal expansion leaves long-lived memory cells that persist for years. On re-encountering the same antigen they respond within hours with higher-affinity IgG and vigorous T-cell activity — the basis of lasting vaccine protection and why second exposures (e.g., to chickenpox) rarely cause disease.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 35: Resistance of the Body to Infection: II. Immunity and Allergy

  15. Routine childhood vaccination (e.g., measles vaccine) confers:

    • A. No immunological memory
    • B. Passive artificial immunity
    • C. Passive natural immunity
    • D. Active artificial immunity
    • E. Innate immunity
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Active artificial immunity

    Vaccines present antigen (live-attenuated, killed, or subunit) so the recipient's own immune system mounts a response and generates memory — active artificial immunity. Protection takes days to develop but lasts years, unlike passive immunization with ready-made antibodies, which is immediate but transient.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 35: Resistance of the Body to Infection: II. Immunity and Allergy

  16. Administration of rabies immunoglobulin to a bite victim is an example of:

    • A. Passive artificial immunity
    • B. Active natural immunity
    • C. Innate immunity
    • D. Herd immunity
    • E. Active artificial immunity
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Passive artificial immunity

    Rabies immunoglobulin supplies preformed antibodies for immediate protection while the simultaneously given vaccine stimulates the victim's own active response. This combined passive-active strategy is used when the incubation period is too short to wait for active immunity alone (also used for tetanus and hepatitis B exposures).

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 35: Resistance of the Body to Infection: II. Immunity and Allergy

  17. The classical pathway of complement activation is initiated by:

    • A. Bacterial lipopolysaccharide alone
    • B. Histamine
    • C. Antigen-antibody (IgG or IgM) complexes binding C1
    • D. Mannose on microbial surfaces
    • E. T-cell cytokines
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Antigen-antibody (IgG or IgM) complexes binding C1

    In the classical pathway, C1 binds to IgG or IgM already attached to antigen, triggering the cascade C4 → C2 → C3 and onward to the membrane attack complex. The alternative pathway starts spontaneously on microbial surfaces and the lectin pathway via mannose-binding lectin — all converging on C3.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 35: Resistance of the Body to Infection: II. Immunity and Allergy

  18. Natural killer (NK) cells differ from cytotoxic T cells in that NK cells:

    • A. Kill virus-infected and tumor cells without prior sensitization, especially cells with reduced MHC class I
    • B. Require prior sensitization and MHC presentation
    • C. Function only in allergy
    • D. Produce antibodies
    • E. Are a type of B lymphocyte
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Kill virus-infected and tumor cells without prior sensitization, especially cells with reduced MHC class I

    NK cells are innate lymphocytes that destroy abnormal cells — particularly those that have downregulated MHC class I to evade T cells — without needing prior exposure or antigen-specific receptors. They are an early defense against viruses and tumors, bridging innate and adaptive immunity.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 35: Resistance of the Body to Infection: II. Immunity and Allergy

  19. Regulatory (suppressor) T cells are important because they:

    • A. Activate complement
    • B. Produce IgE
    • C. Kill all B cells
    • D. Dampen immune responses and maintain self-tolerance, preventing autoimmunity
    • E. Cause anaphylaxis
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Dampen immune responses and maintain self-tolerance, preventing autoimmunity

    Regulatory T cells secrete inhibitory cytokines (e.g., IL-10, TGF-beta) that restrain effector T cells once an infection clears and suppress reactions against self-antigens. Defects in regulation predispose to autoimmune diseases, while tumors may exploit these cells to evade immune attack.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 35: Resistance of the Body to Infection: II. Immunity and Allergy

  20. Anergy in immunology refers to:

    • A. Complement overactivation
    • B. Mast cell proliferation
    • C. Fever
    • D. Excessive antibody production
    • E. A state of lymphocyte unresponsiveness to an antigen, contributing to self-tolerance
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: E. A state of lymphocyte unresponsiveness to an antigen, contributing to self-tolerance

    When lymphocytes encounter antigen without the proper co-stimulatory signals, they become anergic — functionally silenced rather than activated. This peripheral tolerance mechanism deletes or quiets self-reactive clones that escaped central tolerance in the thymus and bone marrow.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 35: Resistance of the Body to Infection: II. Immunity and Allergy
