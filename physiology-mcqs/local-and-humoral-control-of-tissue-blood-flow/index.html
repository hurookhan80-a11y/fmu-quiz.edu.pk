Local and Humoral Control of Tissue Blood Flow MCQs for FMU 1st Year MBBS Physiology

Home › Physiology MCQs › Local and Humoral Control of Tissue Blood Flow

Each tissue controls its own blood flow according to its activity and needs. When a tissue becomes more active, local chemical signals widen its vessels and increase flow, while reduced activity has the opposite effect. Substances carried in the blood, such as hormones, also adjust vessel tone across wider regions of the body.

  1. Skeletal muscle blood flow rises markedly DURING exercise and also rises transiently AFTER release of a tourniquet. These two responses are respectively called:

    • A. Reactive hyperemia and active hyperemia
    • B. Active hyperemia and reactive hyperemia
    • C. Autoregulation and angiogenesis
    • D. Myogenic response and metabolic response
    • E. Flow-mediated dilation and shear stress response
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Active hyperemia and reactive hyperemia

    Active hyperemia is the increased flow during heightened tissue activity (exercise), driven by local metabolic vasodilators. Reactive hyperemia is the transient overshoot of flow after a period of occlusion, caused by accumulated vasodilator metabolites during the ischemic period. Both demonstrate that tissues regulate their own blood supply according to metabolic need.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 17: Local and Humoral Control of Tissue Blood Flow

  2. According to the vasodilator theory of local blood flow control, which substance released by metabolically active tissues is the most important acute vasodilator?

    • A. Norepinephrine
    • B. Endothelin
    • C. Adenosine
    • D. Angiotensin II
    • E. Serotonin
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Adenosine

    The vasodilator theory holds that active tissues release vasodilator metabolites, with adenosine considered the most important in many tissues including the heart. As tissue metabolism rises, adenosine from ATP breakdown accumulates and relaxes arteriolar smooth muscle, increasing local flow. Other contributors include CO2, H+, K+, lactate, and histamine.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 17: Local and Humoral Control of Tissue Blood Flow

  3. When arterial oxygen delivery to a tissue falls, local blood flow to that tissue typically:

    • A. Increases, because low tissue PO2 directly relaxes vascular smooth muscle
    • B. Decreases, because oxygen is needed for vasodilation
    • C. Remains unchanged due to autoregulation
    • D. Increases only if systemic blood pressure rises
    • E. Decreases due to sympathetic activation
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Increases, because low tissue PO2 directly relaxes vascular smooth muscle

    The nutrient-demand (oxygen-lack) theory states that decreased tissue oxygen directly causes vasodilation, increasing flow to restore oxygen delivery. This is a cornerstone of acute local control: flow is matched to metabolic need. In the coronary circulation, even brief oxygen lack produces powerful vasodilation, which is why the heart can increase its blood flow several-fold during exercise.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 17: Local and Humoral Control of Tissue Blood Flow

  4. A tissue is said to exhibit autoregulation of blood flow when:

    • A. Flow increases whenever arterial pressure rises
    • B. Sympathetic nerves precisely adjust flow
    • C. Hormones maintain constant flow
    • D. Blood flow remains relatively constant despite moderate changes in arterial pressure
    • E. New vessels grow in response to demand
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Blood flow remains relatively constant despite moderate changes in arterial pressure

    Autoregulation is the intrinsic ability of tissues (kidney, brain, heart, skeletal muscle) to keep blood flow nearly constant when arterial pressure varies between roughly 70 and 170 mmHg. It results from the combined myogenic response (vessels constrict when stretched) and metabolic mechanisms. It fails at very low or very high pressures.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 17: Local and Humoral Control of Tissue Blood Flow

  5. Endothelium-derived nitric oxide (EDRF) causes vasodilation by:

    • A. Blocking calcium channels on smooth muscle
    • B. Stimulating guanylate cyclase and raising cGMP in vascular smooth muscle
    • C. Activating sympathetic nerves
    • D. Releasing endothelin
    • E. Inhibiting prostaglandin synthesis
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Stimulating guanylate cyclase and raising cGMP in vascular smooth muscle

    Shear stress and substances like acetylcholine and bradykinin stimulate endothelial nitric oxide synthase to release NO, which diffuses to smooth muscle, activates guanylate cyclase, and raises cGMP, causing relaxation. This flow-mediated dilation is a key acute local control mechanism, and its loss in endothelial dysfunction contributes to hypertension and atherosclerosis.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 17: Local and Humoral Control of Tissue Blood Flow

  6. Endothelin-1, released by vascular endothelium, is best described as:

    • A. A potent vasodilator
    • B. An inhibitor of platelet aggregation
    • C. One of the most potent vasoconstrictors known
    • D. A stimulator of angiogenesis
    • E. A natriuretic peptide
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. One of the most potent vasoconstrictors known

    Endothelin-1 is among the most powerful vasoconstrictors discovered, far exceeding angiotensin II in potency and duration. It is released by damaged endothelium and contributes to vasospasm after subarachnoid hemorrhage and to pulmonary hypertension. It opposes nitric oxide in the local balance of vascular tone.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 17: Local and Humoral Control of Tissue Blood Flow

  7. In a tissue subjected to chronic hypoxia over weeks, blood flow capacity increases mainly through:

    • A. Angiogenesis stimulated by vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF)
    • B. Acute vasodilation by adenosine
    • C. Sympathetic vasoconstriction
    • D. Increased cardiac output
    • E. Baroreceptor resetting
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Angiogenesis stimulated by vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF)

    Long-term local control works by changing the physical number and size of vessels. Chronic hypoxia or high metabolic demand stimulates release of VEGF, fibroblast growth factor, and angiogenin, which drive growth of new capillaries (angiogenesis) and enlargement of collaterals. This structural adaptation takes weeks but greatly increases maximal flow capacity.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 17: Local and Humoral Control of Tissue Blood Flow

  8. A low-dose epinephrine infusion dilates skeletal muscle vessels but constricts skin vessels. This difference occurs because:

    • A. Skeletal muscle lacks alpha receptors
    • B. Skin lacks beta receptors entirely
    • C. Epinephrine only acts on the heart
    • D. Skeletal muscle has dominant beta-2 vasodilator receptors while skin has dominant alpha vasoconstrictor receptors
    • E. Epinephrine is destroyed in skin
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Skeletal muscle has dominant beta-2 vasodilator receptors while skin has dominant alpha vasoconstrictor receptors

    Epinephrine stimulates both alpha (constrictor) and beta-2 (dilator) receptors. In skeletal muscle, beta-2 receptors predominate at low doses, causing vasodilation that anticipates exercise. In skin and splanchnic beds, alpha receptors dominate, causing constriction that diverts blood to muscle. At high doses, alpha effects overwhelm beta-2 everywhere, producing generalized vasoconstriction.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 17: Local and Humoral Control of Tissue Blood Flow

  9. Which humoral agent is the most potent vasoconstrictor in the renin-angiotensin system and also stimulates aldosterone release?

    • A. Renin
    • B. Angiotensin II
    • C. Angiotensin I
    • D. Bradykinin
    • E. Atrial natriuretic peptide
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Angiotensin II

    Angiotensin II, formed by ACE from angiotensin I, is a powerful arteriolar vasoconstrictor and the main stimulus for aldosterone secretion from the adrenal cortex. It also enhances sympathetic activity, stimulates ADH and thirst, and promotes renal sodium reabsorption — making it central to both rapid and long-term blood pressure control.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 17: Local and Humoral Control of Tissue Blood Flow

  10. Bradykinin, formed from kininogen by kallikrein in sweat glands and inflamed tissues, causes:

    • A. Vasoconstriction and decreased permeability
    • B. Bronchoconstriction only
    • C. Arteriolar vasodilation and increased capillary permeability
    • D. Platelet aggregation
    • E. Renal sodium retention
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Arteriolar vasodilation and increased capillary permeability

    Bradykinin is a potent vasodilator that also increases capillary permeability, contributing to the redness and swelling of inflammation. It is formed locally in sweat glands (increasing skin flow during heat), salivary glands, and the GI tract. ACE inhibitors raise bradykinin levels, which explains both their vasodilator benefit and the dry cough side effect.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 17: Local and Humoral Control of Tissue Blood Flow

  11. Histamine released from mast cells and basophils during tissue injury produces:

    • A. Arteriolar dilation and increased vascular permeability
    • B. Arteriolar constriction
    • C. Venous constriction only
    • D. Decreased capillary permeability
    • E. Systemic hypertension
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Arteriolar dilation and increased vascular permeability

    Histamine is a classic inflammatory mediator causing arteriolar vasodilation (redness, heat) and endothelial gap formation with increased permeability (swelling). These are the vascular components of the inflammatory response. H1 blockers (antihistamines) reduce these effects in allergy.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 17: Local and Humoral Control of Tissue Blood Flow

  12. During heavy exercise, coronary blood flow increases 4- to 5-fold. This increase is mediated chiefly by:

    • A. Sympathetic vasoconstriction of coronaries
    • B. Vagal stimulation
    • C. Decreased aortic pressure
    • D. Local metabolic vasodilators, especially adenosine
    • E. Circulating epinephrine constricting coronaries
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Local metabolic vasodilators, especially adenosine

    Coronary flow is controlled almost entirely by local metabolic mechanisms, not nerves. Increased myocardial oxygen consumption raises interstitial adenosine (plus NO, K+, H+), which powerfully dilates coronary arterioles. Because the heart extracts about 70% of delivered oxygen even at rest, the only way to meet higher demand is to increase flow — and adenosine is the key signal.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 17: Local and Humoral Control of Tissue Blood Flow

  13. Which change most potently increases cerebral blood flow?

    • A. Decreased arterial PCO2
    • B. Increased arterial PCO2
    • C. Increased arterial PO2
    • D. Sympathetic stimulation
    • E. Decreased blood viscosity
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Increased arterial PCO2

    Carbon dioxide is the most powerful regulator of cerebral blood flow: a rise in arterial PCO2 (which lowers perivascular pH) markedly dilates cerebral vessels, while hyperventilation-induced hypocapnia constricts them. This couples brain perfusion to metabolic activity and is exploited clinically — hyperventilation temporarily lowers intracranial pressure by constricting cerebral vessels.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 17: Local and Humoral Control of Tissue Blood Flow

  14. Skin blood flow is regulated primarily for temperature control. Cold exposure causes:

    • A. Cutaneous vasodilation via parasympathetic nerves
    • B. Cutaneous vasoconstriction via sympathetic nerves, conserving heat
    • C. Increased sweat gland activity
    • D. Reactive hyperemia
    • E. Angiogenesis
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Cutaneous vasoconstriction via sympathetic nerves, conserving heat

    Skin vessels are richly innervated by sympathetic vasoconstrictor fibers and serve thermoregulation. Cold activates sympathetic constriction, minimizing heat loss; heat withdraws sympathetic tone and activates sweat glands (with bradykinin-mediated dilation), maximizing heat loss. This is why skin is pale in the cold and flushed in the heat.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 17: Local and Humoral Control of Tissue Blood Flow

  15. A patient with gradually progressive coronary atherosclerosis may remain asymptomatic for years because:

    • A. Collateral vessels enlarge over time, providing alternative routes of flow
    • B. Adenosine permanently dilates the stenotic segment
    • C. The heart switches to anaerobic metabolism
    • D. Baroreceptors compensate
    • E. Coronary flow is not needed at rest
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Collateral vessels enlarge over time, providing alternative routes of flow

    Chronic gradual occlusion stimulates long-term vascular remodeling: pre-existing collateral anastomoses enlarge and new vessels grow (arteriogenesis/angiogenesis), bypassing the stenosis. This is why sudden occlusion causes infarction while slowly progressive disease may be clinically silent — the collaterals had time to develop.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 17: Local and Humoral Control of Tissue Blood Flow

  16. Which group of substances released by active tissues directly relaxes arteriolar smooth muscle?

    • A. Norepinephrine and serotonin
    • B. Angiotensin II and vasopressin
    • C. Endothelin and thromboxane
    • D. Adenosine, CO2, H+, K+, lactate, and histamine
    • E. Aldosterone and cortisol
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Adenosine, CO2, H+, K+, lactate, and histamine

    Metabolically active tissues accumulate adenosine, CO2, hydrogen ions, potassium, lactate, and histamine — all of which relax arteriolar smooth muscle and increase local flow. This metabolic vasodilation is the basis of active hyperemia and autoregulation in most tissues, matching perfusion precisely to metabolic demand.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 17: Local and Humoral Control of Tissue Blood Flow

  17. Vasopressin (ADH) at high plasma concentrations:

    • A. Dilates all vascular beds
    • B. Constricts arterioles via V1 receptors
    • C. Has no vascular effect
    • D. Only affects renal tubules
    • E. Causes bradycardia via baroreceptors only
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Constricts arterioles via V1 receptors

    Though famous for renal water reabsorption (V2 receptors), vasopressin is also a potent vasoconstrictor via V1 receptors on vascular smooth muscle — hence its older name vasopressin. In hemorrhagic shock, high ADH levels help maintain blood pressure, and vasopressin infusions are used in vasodilatory shock.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 17: Local and Humoral Control of Tissue Blood Flow

  18. Serotonin released from aggregated platelets at a site of vascular injury causes:

    • A. Vasodilation
    • B. Increased capillary permeability only
    • C. Vasoconstriction, helping to limit bleeding
    • D. Cardiac stimulation
    • E. Bronchodilation
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Vasoconstriction, helping to limit bleeding

    Platelet-derived serotonin (5-HT) constricts damaged vessels, contributing to hemostasis alongside thromboxane A2. On healthy endothelium serotonin can cause NO-mediated dilation, but at injury sites the direct smooth muscle constrictor effect dominates — a useful local response to vascular damage.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 17: Local and Humoral Control of Tissue Blood Flow

  19. Prostaglandin E2 and prostacyclin (PGI2) released by the endothelium generally cause:

    • A. Vasodilation and inhibition of platelet aggregation
    • B. Vasoconstriction
    • C. Increased vascular permeability only
    • D. Sympathetic activation
    • E. Aldosterone release
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Vasodilation and inhibition of platelet aggregation

    Endothelial prostacyclin and PGE2 are vasodilators that also inhibit platelet aggregation, protecting vessels from thrombosis. Their counterpart thromboxane A2 (from platelets) does the opposite: vasoconstriction and platelet aggregation. Aspirin's cardiovascular benefit comes from shifting this balance by blocking thromboxane synthesis.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 17: Local and Humoral Control of Tissue Blood Flow

  20. During the first minute of vigorous leg exercise, blood flow to the exercising muscle rises sharply even before systemic blood pressure changes. The earliest mediator of this response is:

    • A. Increased sympathetic vasoconstriction
    • B. Decreased cardiac output
    • C. Accumulation of local metabolic vasodilators such as adenosine and K+
    • D. Release of aldosterone
    • E. Activation of the renin-angiotensin system
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Accumulation of local metabolic vasodilators such as adenosine and K+

    The immediate hyperemia of exercise is a local metabolic response: contracting muscle rapidly accumulates adenosine, K+, H+, and CO2, which relax arterioles within seconds — before neural or hormonal changes occur. Sympathetic vasoconstriction actually opposes this in non-exercising beds, redirecting flow to where metabolism demands it.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 17: Local and Humoral Control of Tissue Blood Flow

  21. When arterial pressure rises suddenly, local blood flow in most tissues returns toward normal within seconds to minutes despite the higher pressure. This myogenic autoregulation occurs because:

    • A. Stretch of arteriolar smooth muscle triggers its contraction, raising vascular resistance
    • B. Baroreceptors directly paralyse vascular smooth muscle
    • C. Capillaries recruit platelets to block the excess flow
    • D. Venous valves close and trap blood in the tissues
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Stretch of arteriolar smooth muscle triggers its contraction, raising vascular resistance

    Increased transmural pressure stretches arteriolar smooth muscle, opening stretch-sensitive calcium channels and causing vasoconstriction that restores flow toward normal. Baroreceptors act through autonomic reflexes on the whole circulation, not as the local myogenic mechanism, and valves or platelets do not regulate tissue flow in this way.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Local and Humoral Control of Tissue Blood Flow
