Rhythmical Excitation of the Heart MCQs for FMU 1st Year MBBS Physiology

Home › Physiology MCQs › Rhythmical Excitation of the Heart

The heart beats rhythmically because its own pacemaker tissue generates electrical impulses again and again without outside stimulation. The sinoatrial node normally sets the pace, and the impulse then spreads through the atria, the atrioventricular node, and the conducting system of the ventricles. This ordered spread makes the atria contract before the ventricles.

  1. Pacemaker cells in the heart undergo spontaneous rhythmic depolarization by:

    • A. Complete closure of Na+ channels at rest
    • B. High resting membrane potential (-90 mV)
    • C. Partial permeability to Na+ and Ca2+ at less negative RMP
    • D. Continuous K+ influx
    • E. Absence of voltage-gated channels
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Partial permeability to Na+ and Ca2+ at less negative RMP

    SA nodal pacemaker cells have no stable resting potential; after reaching about -60 mV they show a slow diastolic depolarisation because of inward Na+ (funny) current and then Ca2+ current through T-type and L-type channels. This gradual drift to threshold (about -40 mV) produces automatic rhythmic firing. A very negative rest (-90 mV) and continuous K+ influx would prevent, not cause, pacemaking.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 10: Rhythmical Excitation of the Heart

  2. A 19-year-old healthy athlete undergoes a routine ECG. The tracing shows normal P waves before every QRS complex, a constant PR interval, and cyclic variation in the R–R interval that corresponds with respiration. Which is the most appropriate interpretation?

    • A. Atrial fibrillation requiring anticoagulation
    • B. Respiratory sinus arrhythmia, a physiological variation in vagal tone
    • C. Sick sinus syndrome requiring pacemaker implantation
    • D. Second-degree sinoatrial exit block
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Respiratory sinus arrhythmia, a physiological variation in vagal tone

    The ECG shows sinus rhythm with cyclic R-R variation in phase with respiration in a healthy young athlete. This is respiratory sinus arrhythmia: inspiration reduces vagal tone and speeds the heart, expiration increases vagal tone and slows it. It is physiological and requires no treatment. There are no features of atrial fibrillation (P waves are present), sick sinus syndrome, or SA exit block.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 10: Rhythmical Excitation of the Heart

  3. A 55 year old man develops ischemia of the interventricular septum which also affects the Purkinje fibres. What will be its effect on the heart?

    • A. Loss of synchronization
    • B. Refractory period will be prolonged
    • C. Increase in contractility
    • D. Fast generation of impulses
    • E. Enhanced synchronization of atria
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Loss of synchronization

    Purkinje fibres conduct the impulse very rapidly (about 4 m/s) to all parts of the ventricles so contraction is almost simultaneous and effective. Ischaemic loss of Purkinje function causes dyssynchronous, inefficient ventricular activation and predisposes to conduction block. It does not increase contractility or atrial synchrony, and fast impulse generation is a nodal (automaticity) property.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 10: Rhythmical Excitation of the Heart

  4. A 65 year old man presents with dizziness and syncope. ECG shows absence of P waves and a slow ventricular rate of 40 beats/min. Which part of the cardiac conducting system is most likely generating the pacemaker activity in this patient?

    • A. Sinoatrial (SA) node
    • B. Atrioventricular (AV) node
    • C. Bundle of His
    • D. Purkinje fibers
    • E. Atrial muscle fibers
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Atrioventricular (AV) node

    In complete AV block or SA node failure with no P waves and a slow regular ventricular rate of 40-50/min, the escape pacemaker is the AV node (junctional rhythm). Its intrinsic rate is slower than the SA node (60-100/min) but faster than a ventricular focus (20-40/min). The atria alone cannot drive the ventricles without AV conduction.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 10: Rhythmical Excitation of the Heart

  5. A 68-year-old man presents with palpitations and dizziness. His ECG shows a regular narrow-complex tachycardia at 150 beats/min. After carotid sinus massage, characteristic saw-tooth flutter waves become clearly visible. Which mechanism best explains why the flutter waves became apparent?

    • A. Increased vagal stimulation prolongs AV nodal refractoriness, increasing AV block and unmasking ongoing atrial flutter.
    • B. Vagal stimulation suppresses the atrial re-entry circuit, allowing normal P waves to appear.
    • C. Carotid sinus massage slows ventricular conduction by prolonging the His–Purkinje refractory period, exposing flutter waves.
    • D. Carotid sinus massage converts atrial flutter directly to sinus rhythm, making atrial activity more visible.
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Increased vagal stimulation prolongs AV nodal refractoriness, increasing AV block and unmasking ongoing atrial flutter.

    Vagal stimulation releases acetylcholine at the AV node, slowing conduction velocity and prolonging AV nodal refractory period. In AV nodal re-entrant tachycardia this can interrupt the circuit by blocking the slow pathway limb and restoring sinus rhythm, which is why vagal manoeuvres and adenosine terminate many regular narrow-complex tachycardias. It does not shorten AV refractoriness.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 10: Rhythmical Excitation of the Heart

  6. A patient has a PR interval of 0.20 seconds with normal P waves and narrow QRS complexes. Which conclusion best explains the ECG finding?

    • A. The AV nodal delay has been abolished.
    • B. The AV node is delaying impulse conduction to optimise ventricular filling.
    • C. Ventricular depolarization begins before atrial contraction.
    • D. The His–Purkinje system is conducting more slowly than the AV node.
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. The AV node is delaying impulse conduction to optimise ventricular filling.

    A PR interval of 0.20 s is at the upper limit of normal (0.12-0.20 s). The AV nodal delay (about 0.13 s of the PR interval) is physiological: it allows atrial contraction to complete ventricular filling (atrial kick, about 20-30% of filling) before ventricular systole begins. It does not indicate AV block, which requires PR greater than 0.20 s.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 10: Rhythmical Excitation of the Heart

  7. A patient presents with recurrent syncope. ECG demonstrates complete atrioventricular block with independent atrial and ventricular rhythms. Which property of cardiac tissue is primarily affected?

    • A. Automaticity
    • B. Conductivity
    • C. Contractility
    • D. Rhythmicity
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Conductivity

    Complete AV block means atrial impulses are generated normally but none are conducted to the ventricles, which beat independently under an escape pacemaker. The defective property is conductivity (dromotropy) through the AV node/His-Purkinje system. Automaticity, excitability, and contractility of the ventricles themselves may be intact.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 10: Rhythmical Excitation of the Heart

  8. A transplanted donor heart continues to beat rhythmically after complete denervation. Which intrinsic property of cardiac tissue best explains this observation?

    • A. Conductivity
    • B. Automaticity
    • C. Contractility
    • D. Refractoriness
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Automaticity

    A denervated transplanted heart continues to beat because the SA node possesses intrinsic automaticity (spontaneous phase 4 depolarisation independent of nerves). Rate can then be modulated only by circulating catecholamines and temperature. Conductivity, contractility, and excitability alone cannot initiate rhythm without automaticity.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 10: Rhythmical Excitation of the Heart

  9. An action potential characterized by spontaneous phase 4 depolarization, absence of a stable resting membrane potential, slow calcium-mediated phase 0 depolarization, and no distinct plateau phase. Which cardiac tissue is most likely being studied?

    • A. Ventricular contractile myocytes
    • B. Purkinje fibres
    • C. Sinoatrial (SA) nodal cells
    • D. Atrial contractile myocytes
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Sinoatrial (SA) nodal cells

    Spontaneous phase 4 depolarisation, no stable resting potential (maximum diastolic potential about -60 mV), and a slow Ca2+-mediated upstroke characterise SA nodal (pacemaker) cells. Working ventricular myocytes have a stable -90 mV rest, fast Na+ upstroke, and no phase 4 drift. Purkinje cells can show phase 4 but have a fast upstroke and more negative potential.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 10: Rhythmical Excitation of the Heart

  10. The parasympathetic nervous system decreases:

    • A. Heart rate only
    • B. Contractility only
    • C. Both heart rate and contractility
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Both heart rate and contractility

    Parasympathetic (vagal) activity via acetylcholine and M2 receptors slows SA nodal firing (negative chronotropy), slows AV conduction, and reduces atrial contractility; ventricular contractility is modestly reduced. In Guyton's simplified teaching it decreases both heart rate and contractility. It does not increase either, and the effect is greater on rate than on ventricular force.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 10: Rhythmical Excitation of the Heart

  11. The ventricles contract almost simultaneously despite their large mass because of the:

    • A. Purkinje system of conduction
    • B. AV nodal delay
    • C. Large cardiac muscle fibres
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Purkinje system of conduction

    Although ventricular mass is large, activation is almost simultaneous because the His-Purkinje system conducts at about 4 m/s, distributing the impulse rapidly to all ventricular regions within about 0.03 s. Without Purkinje conduction, slow cell-to-cell spread would cause dyssynchronous, inefficient contraction. Nodal tissue initiates the impulse but does not distribute it through the ventricles.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 10: Rhythmical Excitation of the Heart

  12. An ECG showing progressive PR prolongation followed by a dropped QRS complex indicates:

    • A. First-degree heart block only
    • B. Complete (third-degree) heart block with AV dissociation throughout
    • C. Normal sinus rhythm
    • D. Second-degree heart block (Mobitz I / Wenckebach pattern in this recall)
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Second-degree heart block (Mobitz I / Wenckebach pattern in this recall)

    Progressive lengthening of conduction delay until a beat is dropped is second-degree AV block. In first-degree block every impulse is conducted, only with a prolonged PR interval, and no beat is dropped.

    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Purkinje fibers

  15. The AV nodal delay is represented on the ECG mainly by which interval?

    Answer: C. PR interval

    The PR interval (0.12-0.20 s) includes atrial depolarization and the physiological delay in the AV node/bundle allowing atrial emptying before ventricular contraction. The QT interval represents total ventricular depolarization and repolarization.

  16. Which structure is the normal pacemaker of the heart?

    • B. Bundle of His
    • C. Sinoatrial node
    • D. Purkinje fibers
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Sinoatrial node

    The sinoatrial node has the fastest intrinsic rate of spontaneous depolarization and therefore sets the heart rate. The AV node and His-Purkinje system are slower subsidiary pacemakers that normally follow the SA node.

  17. Ventricular fibrillation is best described as:

    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Rapid, uncoordinated and chaotic ventricular contractions with no effective output

    • D. QRS complex
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. QRS complex

  19. An ECG showing a wide QRS complex, absent P waves and tall, peaked T waves most suggests:

    • A. Hyperkalemia
    • B. Hypocalcemia
    • C. Hypercalcemia
    • D. Hypokalemia
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Hyperkalemia

    Rising extracellular K+ depresses atrial activity and slows ventricular conduction, giving absent P waves, wide QRS complexes and tall peaked T waves. Hypokalemia typically gives flattened T waves and prominent U waves.

  20. The slowest speed of impulse conduction in the heart is in the:

    • A. Purkinje fibres
    • B. Atrial muscle
    • C. Ventricular muscle
    • D. AV node
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. AV node

    Conduction is slowest in the AV node (about 0.02-0.05 m/s), producing the AV delay that lets the atria empty before ventricular contraction. Purkinje fibres conduct the fastest.

  21. SA node automaticity (spontaneous diastolic depolarisation) is mainly due to:

    • A. Leakiness of the membrane to Na+ and Ca++ ions in phase 4
    • B. A sudden increase in K+ permeability
    • C. Closure of Ca++ channels in phase 4
    • D. Activation of Na+-K+ ATPase hyperpolarising the cell
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Leakiness of the membrane to Na+ and Ca++ ions in phase 4

    Pacemaker cells have no stable resting potential; slow inward Na+ ('funny') and then Ca++ currents during phase 4 bring the membrane to threshold and fire the next impulse. Increased K+ efflux would hyperpolarise and slow the rate.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 10: Rhythmical Excitation of the Heart

  22. The resting (maximum diastolic) membrane potential of SA node cells is about:

    • A. -90 mV
    • B. -55 mV
    • C. -70 mV stable like ventricle
    • D. 0 mV
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. -55 mV

    SA nodal fibres have a less negative resting potential of about -55 to -60 mV, which inactivates fast Na+ channels so their upstroke depends on slow Ca++ channels. Ventricular muscle rests near -85 to -90 mV.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Ch. 10: Rhythmical Excitation of the Heart

  23. AV nodal delay is mainly due to:

    • A. Fewer and smaller gap junctions with slow conduction in nodal fibres
    • B. Rapid conduction through the His-Purkinje system
    • C. High density of fast sodium channels
    • D. Excess gap junctions in nodal tissue
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Fewer and smaller gap junctions with slow conduction in nodal fibres

    AV nodal fibres are small with fewer gap junctions, so conduction is slow, producing the ~0.1 s AV delay that allows atrial emptying before ventricular contraction. Purkinje fibres, by contrast, conduct very rapidly.

  25. ECG shows absent P waves with normal QRS complexes and a ventricular rate of 15-45/min. This suggests:

    • D. AV node
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. AV node

  28. The SA node normally acts as the cardiac pacemaker because:

    • A. It is the largest mass of nodal tissue
    • B. It receives the densest sympathetic innervation
    • C. It has the longest action potential duration
    • D. It has the fastest intrinsic rate of spontaneous discharge, overdrive-suppressing slower latent pacemakers
    • E. It lies closest to the AV node
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. It has the fastest intrinsic rate of spontaneous discharge, overdrive-suppressing slower latent pacemakers

    The SA node discharges spontaneously at 70-80/min — faster than the AV node (40-60) or Purkinje fibers (15-40). Because the fastest pacemaker drives the whole heart and suppresses slower ones (overdrive suppression), the SA node normally commands the rhythm. Disease of the SA node lets slower latent pacemakers emerge.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 10: Rhythmical Excitation of the Heart

  29. The funny current (If) responsible for SA node automaticity is:

    • A. An inward Cl- current
    • B. An outward K+ current
    • C. A gap-junction current between nodal cells
    • D. An outward Ca2+ current
    • E. An inward mixed Na+/K+ current activated by hyperpolarization
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: E. An inward mixed Na+/K+ current activated by hyperpolarization

    Between beats, the SA node membrane slowly drifts upward (the prepotential) because hyperpolarization activates If, an inward current carried by both Na+ and K+. When threshold is reached, L-type Ca2+ channels fire the action potential. Sympathetic stimulation steepens this drift (faster rate); vagal stimulation flattens it.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 10: Rhythmical Excitation of the Heart

  30. The AV nodal delay (about 0.13 s) serves mainly to:

    • A. Increase the heart rate
    • B. Allow the atria to empty completely into the ventricles before ventricular contraction begins
    • C. Synchronize contraction of the two atria
    • D. Protect against ventricular fibrillation
    • E. Delay ventricular repolarization
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Allow the atria to empty completely into the ventricles before ventricular contraction begins

    The impulse is delayed about 0.13 s in the AV node and penetrating bundle — slow conduction through small fibers with few gap junctions. This pause lets atrial systole finish emptying blood into the ventricles before they contract; without it, atrial and ventricular systole would overlap and filling would suffer.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 10: Rhythmical Excitation of the Heart

  31. Conduction through the AV node is slow primarily because:

    • A. The fibers are small with relatively few gap junctions
    • B. Purkinje fibers bypass the node entirely
    • C. The fibers are large with abundant gap junctions
    • D. Sympathetic tone is maximal at rest
    • E. The node is encased in fibrous tissue
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. The fibers are small with relatively few gap junctions

    AV nodal fibers are tiny with relatively few gap junctions, so the impulse creeps at ~0.05 m/s (versus 1.5-4 m/s in Purkinje fibers). This intrinsic slowness — not fibrosis or nerve tone — creates the physiologic delay essential for sequential chamber contraction.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 10: Rhythmical Excitation of the Heart

  32. If the SA node fails, the ventricles are typically driven by the Purkinje system at an intrinsic rate of:

    • A. 40-60/min
    • B. 15-40/min
    • C. 70-80/min
    • D. 100-120/min
    • E. 60-70/min
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. 15-40/min

    Intrinsic automaticity rates are: SA node 70-80, AV node 40-60, Purkinje 15-40 per minute. If higher pacemakers fail, the ventricles fall back on the slow Purkinje rhythm — enough to sustain life briefly but with very low output, which is why complete heart block needs pacing.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 10: Rhythmical Excitation of the Heart

  33. Vagal (parasympathetic) stimulation of the heart causes:

    • A. Shortened AV nodal delay via beta receptors
    • B. Increased heart rate and faster AV conduction
    • C. No effect on the SA node
    • D. Increased ventricular contractility
    • E. Decreased SA node discharge rate and slowed AV nodal conduction
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: E. Decreased SA node discharge rate and slowed AV nodal conduction

    Vagal fibers release acetylcholine onto SA nodal muscarinic receptors, flattening the prepotential and slowing the rate; they also slow AV nodal conduction markedly (even causing transient AV block). This is why vagal maneuvers can terminate AV-nodal-dependent supraventricular tachycardias.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 10: Rhythmical Excitation of the Heart

  34. A patient with complete heart block develops transient episodes of syncope (Stokes-Adams attacks). These are caused by:

    • A. Atrial fibrillation with rapid conduction
    • B. Transient ventricular asystole before an escape pacemaker takes over
    • C. Excessive vagal tone alone
    • D. Hypoglycemia
    • E. Aortic stenosis
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Transient ventricular asystole before an escape pacemaker takes over

    In complete heart block the ventricles depend on a slow escape rhythm. Before the escape pacemaker captures, there may be several seconds of total asystole — cerebral perfusion stops and the patient faints (Stokes-Adams attack). Recovery follows when the escape rhythm resumes.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 10: Rhythmical Excitation of the Heart

  35. Sympathetic stimulation shortens the AV nodal delay by:

    • A. Decreasing the SA node rate
    • B. Blocking muscarinic receptors
    • C. Closing gap junctions between nodal cells
    • D. Increasing AV nodal conduction velocity via beta-1 receptors
    • E. Hyperpolarizing nodal cells
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Increasing AV nodal conduction velocity via beta-1 receptors

    Norepinephrine acting on beta-1 receptors raises cAMP in AV nodal cells, increasing Ca2+ influx and conduction velocity — so the impulse traverses the node faster and the PR interval shortens. This is the mirror image of vagal slowing and explains PR shortening during exercise.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 10: Rhythmical Excitation of the Heart

  36. Atropine increases heart rate because it:

    • A. Blocking muscarinic receptors, removing vagal slowing of the SA node
    • B. Directly increasing the funny current
    • C. Stimulating beta-adrenergic receptors
    • D. Blocking AV nodal conduction
    • E. Releasing norepinephrine from terminals
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Blocking muscarinic receptors, removing vagal slowing of the SA node

    Resting heart rate reflects a tug-of-war between sympathetic drive and vagal (muscarinic) slowing. Atropine blocks the muscarinic receptors, removing vagal restraint, so the SA node's intrinsic faster rate is unmasked and heart rate rises. It does not stimulate beta receptors.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 10: Rhythmical Excitation of the Heart

  37. The rapid spread of excitation through the ventricular endocardium is ensured by Purkinje fibers, whose conduction velocity is approximately:

    • A. 1.5-4.0 m/s
    • B. 10 m/s
    • C. 0.01 m/s
    • D. 0.05 m/s
    • E. 0.3 m/s
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. 1.5-4.0 m/s

    Purkinje fibers are large with dense gap junctions, conducting at 1.5-4.0 m/s — the fastest in the heart.   38. Unlike the ventricular muscle action potential, the SA node action potential is characterized by:

    • A. Triggering by acetylcholine
    • B. A long plateau phase
    • C. A duration of 300 ms
    • D. Complete insensitivity to autonomic nerves
    • E. A slow Ca2+-mediated upstroke with no sharp Na+ spike and no plateau
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: E. A slow Ca2+-mediated upstroke with no sharp Na+ spike and no plateau

    The SA node action potential has no sharp Na+ upstroke and no plateau: slow diastolic depolarization leads directly into a Ca2+-mediated (L-type) upstroke followed by K+ repolarization. This Ca2+-dependent shape is also why nodal tissue is sensitive to calcium-channel blockers like verapamil.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 10: Rhythmical Excitation of the Heart

  39. Vagal escape refers to:

    • A. Pacemaker failure
    • B. Gradual resumption of SA node discharge during sustained vagal stimulation as reflexes compensate
    • C. Atrial fibrillation
    • D. Complete heart block
    • E. Ventricular tachycardia
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Gradual resumption of SA node discharge during sustained vagal stimulation as reflexes compensate

    Strong sustained vagal stimulation initially arrests the SA node, but over seconds the node gradually resumes firing — partly via reflex sympathetic activation and partly because nodal cells partially adapt. This vagal escape protects against prolonged asystole during intense parasympathetic discharge.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 10: Rhythmical Excitation of the Heart

  40. Beta-blockers are useful for controlling ventricular rate in atrial fibrillation because they:

    • A. Increase SA node automaticity
    • B. Dilate the coronary arteries
    • C. Increase myocardial contractility
    • D. Directly convert AF to sinus rhythm
    • E. Slow AV nodal conduction, reducing the number of atrial impulses reaching the ventricles
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: E. Slow AV nodal conduction, reducing the number of atrial impulses reaching the ventricles

    In atrial fibrillation hundreds of atrial impulses bombard the AV node each minute. Beta-blockers slow AV nodal conduction so fewer impulses penetrate to the ventricles, converting a dangerously rapid irregular response into a controlled one. They do not restore sinus rhythm — that needs cardioversion or antiarrhythmics.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 10: Rhythmical Excitation of the Heart

  41. An ectopic pacemaker is defined as:

    • A. An implanted artificial pacemaker
    • B. A re-entrant circuit around scar tissue
    • C. Any pacemaker site other than the SA node that assumes control of the rhythm
    • D. A blocked AV node
    • E. A normally functioning SA node
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Any pacemaker site other than the SA node that assumes control of the rhythm

    Normally the SA node dominates, but any other automatic focus — AV node, Purkinje fibers, or irritable atrial/ventricular tissue — that fires faster than the SA node becomes an ectopic pacemaker and hijacks the rhythm. Ectopic beats and escape rhythms both arise this way.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 10: Rhythmical Excitation of the Heart

  42. Parasympathetic stimulation has relatively little effect on ventricular contractile force because:

    • B. Ventricles lack calcium channels
    • C. Ventricular muscle lacks muscarinic receptors entirely
    • D. The AV node blocks vagal signals
    • E. Sympathetic tone is absent at rest
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Vagal innervation of the ventricular muscle is sparse

    Vagal fibers densely innervate the SA and AV nodes but send only sparse branches to ventricular muscle. Hence parasympathetic stimulation powerfully slows rate and AV conduction yet barely changes ventricular contractile force — whereas sympathetic fibers richly supply the ventricles and strongly boost contractility.

  43. Overdrive suppression explains why:

    • A. Ectopic beats cannot occur
    • B. The AV node delays conduction
    • C. Beta-blockers slow the heart
    • D. Latent pacemakers in the AV node and Purkinje fibers are kept silent by the more rapid SA node discharge
    • E. The SA node beats faster during exercise
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Latent pacemakers in the AV node and Purkinje fibers are kept silent by the more rapid SA node discharge

    A faster pacemaker repeatedly depolarizes slower automatic cells before they reach threshold on their own, keeping them suppressed. If the SA node slows or fails, this overdrive suppression lifts and the next-fastest focus (AV node, then Purkinje) escapes to drive the heart at its own intrinsic rate.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 10: Rhythmical Excitation of the Heart

  44. The Bachmann bundle is important for:

    • A. Ventricular repolarization
    • B. Rapid conduction of the impulse from the right atrium to the left atrium
    • C. AV nodal delay
    • D. Purkinje fiber activation
    • E. Coronary blood flow
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Rapid conduction of the impulse from the right atrium to the left atrium

    The impulse must reach both atria nearly simultaneously for coordinated contraction. The Bachmann bundle is a specialized interatrial band conducting rapidly from the right atrium (where the SA node sits) to the left atrium. Block here produces a broad, notched P wave.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 10: Rhythmical Excitation of the Heart

  45. During strenuous exercise, the P-R interval typically:

    • A. Shortens due to sympathetic enhancement of AV nodal conduction
    • B. Exceeds 0.30 s
    • C. Remains exactly constant
    • D. Disappears
    • E. Lengthens
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Shortens due to sympathetic enhancement of AV nodal conduction

    Exercise raises sympathetic drive, which accelerates AV nodal conduction and shortens the PR interval — the electrical equivalent of abbreviating the AV delay when cycle length falls. A lengthening PR during exercise would suggest AV nodal disease, not physiology.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 10: Rhythmical Excitation of the Heart

  46. A 70-year-old man on no medications has a resting heart rate of 38/min. ECG shows normal P waves, each followed by a QRS with a normal PR interval. The most likely explanation is:

    • A. Atrial flutter
    • B. WPW syndrome
    • C. Complete heart block
    • D. Ventricular tachycardia
    • E. Sinus bradycardia with intact AV conduction
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: E. Sinus bradycardia with intact AV conduction

    Every P wave is followed by a QRS with a normal PR interval, so AV conduction is intact — the atria are simply being driven slowly. This is sinus bradycardia (common with high vagal tone, hypothyroidism, or beta-blockers), not block. In heart block, P waves would fail to conduct or dissociate from QRS complexes.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 10: Rhythmical Excitation of the Heart

  47. Stimulation of the sympathetic nerves to the heart increases all of the following EXCEPT:

    • A. Force of atrial and ventricular contraction
    • B. AV nodal conduction velocity
    • C. AV nodal delay duration
    • D. SA node discharge rate
    • E. Rate of ventricular relaxation
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. AV nodal delay duration

    Sympathetic activation raises SA node rate, speeds AV conduction (shortening the AV delay), strengthens contraction, and even hastens relaxation (positive lusitropy) to preserve diastolic filling time. Lengthening the AV delay would be a parasympathetic (vagal) effect, not sympathetic.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 10: Rhythmical Excitation of the Heart

  48. The delay of about 0.1 second in conduction through the AV node is physiologically important because it:

    • A. Allows the atria to empty blood into the ventricles before ventricular contraction begins
    • B. Prevents any atrial impulses from ever reaching the ventricles
    • C. Generates the QRS complex on the ECG
    • D. Raises systemic arterial pressure directly
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Allows the atria to empty blood into the ventricles before ventricular contraction begins

    Slow AV nodal conduction gives atrial contraction time to complete ventricular filling before the ventricles are excited, optimising stroke volume, especially at higher heart rates. The node still conducts normal impulses, the QRS reflects rapid ventricular depolarisation via the His-Purkinje system, and the delay itself does not raise arterial pressure.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Rhythmical Excitation of the Heart
