Home › Physiology MCQs › Red Blood Cells, Anemia, and Polycythemia

Red blood cells carry oxygen from the lungs to the tissues and help carry carbon dioxide back. Each cell is packed with haemoglobin, the pigment that binds oxygen, and red cells are produced in the bone marrow. Anaemia means too few red cells or too little haemoglobin, while polycythemia means an abnormally high number of red cells.

  5. A Colony-forming unit-erythrocyte is defined as;

    • A. Pro-erythroblast in differentiation stage
    • B. A committed stem cell for erythrocytes
    • C. Reticulocyte without nucleus
    • D. Multipotent hematopoietic stem cell

  6. A patient appears pale and fragile. The diagnosis is anemia. What is anemia

    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. decrease in RBC count or haemoglobin or both

  7. A physician advised a routine work-up for a 4-year-old fatigued boy. Increased reticulocyte count with low haemoglobin suggests:

    • B. Aplastic anaemia
    • C. Iron deficiency anaemia

    Answer: A. Hemolytic anaemia

    A raised reticulocyte count with low haemoglobin shows the marrow is responding by releasing young red cells, which occurs when red cells are being destroyed early — haemolytic anaemia.   8. Circulatory failure in polycythemia is due to sluggish flow of blood decreasing rate of venous return due to;

    • A. Increased hemoglobin
    • B. Increased cardiac output
    • C. Increased blood viscosity
    • D. Increased blood pressure
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Increased blood viscosity

    In polycythemia the markedly raised haematocrit greatly increases blood viscosity, making flow sluggish, reducing venous return and cardiac output and predisposing to circulatory failure and thrombosis. The problem is viscosity rather than the absolute haemoglobin rise alone.

  9. Extra corpuscular Hb in plasma is bound to

    • A. Albumin
    • B. Transferrin
    • C. Ferritin
    • D. Hemopexin
    • E. Haptoglobin
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: E. Haptoglobin

    Free haemoglobin released into plasma (extracorpuscular haemoglobin from haemolysis) is immediately bound by haptoglobin, and the complex is cleared by macrophages, preventing renal loss of haemoglobin and iron. Hemopexin binds free haem, transferrin carries iron, and ferritin stores iron intracellularly.

  11. Iron is released from macrophages into blood and stored in form of ferritin while porphyrin is converted by macrophages into;

    • A. Pyrrole
    • B. Bilirubin
    • C. Protoporphyrin
    • D. Hemosiderin
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Bilirubin

    When macrophages break down haemoglobin, iron is released to transferrin/ferritin for reuse, while the porphyrin ring is opened and converted through biliverdin to bilirubin, which is carried to the liver and excreted in bile.   16. A high rate of red blood cell production is primarily stimulated by:

    • A. Low oxygen (hypoxia) in tissues
    • B. High oxygen in tissues
    • C. High carbon dioxide only
    • D. Increased plasma glucose
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Low oxygen (hypoxia) in tissues

  17. Iron deficiency characteristically produces which type of anemia?

    • A. Macrocytic megaloblastic anemia
    • B. Normocytic hemolytic anemia
    • C. Aplastic anemia
    • D. Microcytic hypochromic anemia
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Microcytic hypochromic anemia

    Without enough iron, hemoglobin synthesis falls and red cells become small (microcytic) and pale (hypochromic). Macrocytic anemia points instead to vitamin B12 or folate deficiency.

  21. Degradation of red blood cells and hemoglobin is carried out mainly by:

  22. Erythropoietin, the principal hormone stimulating red cell production, is secreted mainly by:

    • A. Splenic macrophages
    • B. The anterior pituitary
    • C. Hepatocytes in response to anemia
    • D. Peritubular interstitial cells of the kidney in response to tissue hypoxia
    • E. Bone marrow stromal cells
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Peritubular interstitial cells of the kidney in response to tissue hypoxia

    About 90% of erythropoietin comes from peritubular interstitial cells of the renal cortex (the rest mainly from the liver). Renal hypoxia stabilizes hypoxia-inducible factor, which drives erythropoietin gene transcription. This is why chronic kidney disease commonly causes anemia — the diseased kidneys cannot produce enough erythropoietin.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 33: Red Blood Cells, Anemia, and Polycythemia

  23. Fetal hemoglobin (HbF) has a HIGHER oxygen affinity than adult hemoglobin (HbA) because:

    • A. It lacks heme groups
    • B. It has a lower molecular weight
    • C. It contains four alpha chains
    • D. Its gamma chains bind 2,3-BPG poorly, so less 2,3-BPG interference keeps affinity high
    • E. It binds more hydrogen ions
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Its gamma chains bind 2,3-BPG poorly, so less 2,3-BPG interference keeps affinity high

    HbF (alpha-2/gamma-2) binds 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate poorly compared with HbA (alpha-2/beta-2). Since 2,3-BPG normally lowers hemoglobin's oxygen affinity, its weak binding to HbF keeps fetal affinity high — allowing the fetus to draw oxygen across the placenta from the mother's lower-affinity HbA.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 33: Red Blood Cells, Anemia, and Polycythemia

  24. An increase in 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate (2,3-BPG) inside red cells:

    • A. Increases carbon dioxide transport
    • B. Prevents sickling
    • C. Has no effect on the dissociation curve
    • D. Decreases hemoglobin's oxygen affinity, favoring oxygen release to tissues (right shift)
    • E. Increases hemoglobin's oxygen affinity (left shift)
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Decreases hemoglobin's oxygen affinity, favoring oxygen release to tissues (right shift)

    2,3-BPG binds deoxyhemoglobin and stabilizes it, lowering oxygen affinity and shifting the dissociation curve to the right — so hemoglobin unloads oxygen more readily. Red cells raise 2,3-BPG during chronic hypoxia, anemia, and at high altitude, improving tissue oxygen delivery as a compensatory adaptation.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 33: Red Blood Cells, Anemia, and Polycythemia

  25. The Bohr effect describes the fact that:

    • A. Myoglobin releases oxygen before hemoglobin
    • B. Increased hydrogen ions and CO2 decrease hemoglobin's oxygen affinity, promoting oxygen release in active tissues
    • C. Fetal hemoglobin binds oxygen weakly
    • D. 2,3-BPG increases oxygen affinity
    • E. Oxygen loading increases carbon dioxide carriage
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Increased hydrogen ions and CO2 decrease hemoglobin's oxygen affinity, promoting oxygen release in active tissues

    In metabolically active tissues, accumulating CO2 and hydrogen ions (lower pH) reduce hemoglobin's oxygen affinity — a rightward shift of the dissociation curve — so more oxygen is released exactly where it is needed. In the lungs the reverse occurs: CO2 is blown off, pH rises, and hemoglobin loads oxygen avidly.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 33: Red Blood Cells, Anemia, and Polycythemia

  26. Dietary iron is absorbed principally in the:

    • B. Esophagus
    • C. Colon by bacterial action
    • D. Stomach as ferric iron
    • E. Duodenum and upper jejunum, in the ferrous (Fe2+) form
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: E. Duodenum and upper jejunum, in the ferrous (Fe2+) form

    Iron is absorbed mainly in the duodenum and upper jejunum, and only in the ferrous (Fe2+) form — gastric acid and dietary factors like vitamin C favor reduction of ferric to ferrous iron. Absorbed iron is carried in plasma by transferrin and stored as ferritin; absorption is upregulated in iron deficiency and pregnancy.

  27. A 45-year-old woman with autoimmune destruction of gastric parietal cells develops fatigue, a smooth beefy-red tongue, and numbness in her feet. Blood film shows macrocytic red cells with hypersegmented neutrophils. The most likely diagnosis is:

    • A. Thalassemia trait
    • B. Anemia of chronic disease
    • C. Pernicious anemia from vitamin B12 deficiency
    • D. Folate deficiency
    • E. Iron deficiency anemia
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Pernicious anemia from vitamin B12 deficiency

    Pernicious anemia results from autoimmune loss of intrinsic factor (made by parietal cells), preventing vitamin B12 absorption in the ileum. B12 deficiency impairs DNA synthesis, producing megaloblastic (macrocytic) anemia with hypersegmented neutrophils, and — unlike folate deficiency — causes neurologic damage (subacute combined degeneration of the cord) with paresthesias.

  28. A key clinical difference between vitamin B12 deficiency and folate deficiency is that:

    • A. Only B12 deficiency responds to oral supplements
    • B. B12 deficiency never causes anemia
    • C. Only B12 deficiency causes demyelinating neurologic damage
    • D. Folate deficiency causes microcytosis
    • E. Only folate deficiency causes macrocytosis
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Only B12 deficiency causes demyelinating neurologic damage

    Both deficiencies impair DNA synthesis and cause megaloblastic, macrocytic anemia. However, vitamin B12 is also required for myelin maintenance, so B12 deficiency — but not folate deficiency — produces subacute combined degeneration of the spinal cord with paresthesias and gait disturbance. Giving folate alone to a B12-deficient patient can mask the anemia while neurologic damage progresses.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 33: Red Blood Cells, Anemia, and Polycythemia

  29. Sickle cell anemia is caused by:

    • A. Autoimmune hemolysis
    • B. Excess production of gamma chains
    • C. Deletion of alpha-globin genes
    • D. Iron overload
    • E. A single amino acid substitution (valine for glutamic acid) at position 6 of the beta-globin chain
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: E. A single amino acid substitution (valine for glutamic acid) at position 6 of the beta-globin chain

    A point mutation substitutes hydrophobic valine for glutamic acid at position 6 of beta-globin. When deoxygenated, the abnormal hemoglobin S polymerizes into fibers that distort red cells into sickle shapes, causing vaso-occlusion, hemolysis, and painful crises — especially under hypoxia, dehydration, or infection.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 33: Red Blood Cells, Anemia, and Polycythemia

  30. A 12-year-old boy with sickle cell disease develops sudden severe bone pain and pallor during a chest infection. The most important immediate precipitant to correct is:

    • A. Hypertension
    • B. Alkalosis
    • C. Hypoxia and dehydration, which promote sickling
    • D. Hyperglycemia
    • E. Hypercalcemia
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Hypoxia and dehydration, which promote sickling

    Sickling is triggered by deoxygenation, dehydration (which concentrates hemoglobin S inside the cell), acidosis, and infection. A chest infection causes hypoxia that precipitates a vaso-occlusive pain crisis. Management centers on oxygen, vigorous hydration, analgesia, and treating the infection — addressing the triggers of polymerization.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 33: Red Blood Cells, Anemia, and Polycythemia

  31. Beta-thalassemia major differs from sickle cell disease in that its primary defect is:

    • A. Excess iron absorption only
    • B. Vitamin B12 malabsorption
    • C. Autoimmune red cell destruction
    • D. Abnormal hemoglobin structure
    • E. Reduced or absent synthesis of beta-globin chains, causing microcytic anemia
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: E. Reduced or absent synthesis of beta-globin chains, causing microcytic anemia

    Thalassemias are quantitative disorders — too few globin chains are made — whereas sickle cell disease is a qualitative (structural) defect. In beta-thalassemia major, absent beta-chain synthesis causes severe microcytic, hypochromic anemia with target cells, requiring lifelong transfusion; the resulting iron overload damages the heart, liver, and endocrine glands.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 33: Red Blood Cells, Anemia, and Polycythemia

  32. A 28-year-old woman with heavy menstrual bleeding has fatigue, koilonychia (spoon-shaped nails), and a blood film showing microcytic, hypochromic red cells. The most likely diagnosis and confirmatory finding are:

    • A. Thalassemia — elevated HbA2
    • B. Sideroblastic anemia — ring sideroblasts
    • C. Anemia of chronic disease — high ferritin
    • D. Lead poisoning — basophilic stippling
    • E. Iron deficiency anemia — low serum ferritin
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: E. Iron deficiency anemia — low serum ferritin

    Chronic blood loss depletes iron stores, impairing heme synthesis and producing microcytic, hypochromic red cells. Low serum ferritin is the hallmark of true iron deficiency, distinguishing it from thalassemia (normal/high ferritin, raised HbA2) and anemia of chronic disease (high ferritin from inflammation).

  33. Polycythemia vera is distinguished from secondary polycythemia (e.g., from chronic lung disease) by:

    • A. An autonomous bone marrow proliferation with high red cell mass but LOW serum erythropoietin
    • B. Low hemoglobin
    • C. Absence of splenomegaly
    • D. Normal blood viscosity
    • E. Low red cell mass
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. An autonomous bone marrow proliferation with high red cell mass but LOW serum erythropoietin

    Polycythemia vera is a primary myeloproliferative neoplasm (often JAK2 mutation) in which the marrow overproduces red cells independently of erythropoietin — so serum EPO is low. In secondary polycythemia, chronic hypoxia drives HIGH erythropoietin. The markedly raised hematocrit increases blood viscosity, risking thrombosis, and patients often show facial plethora and splenomegaly.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 33: Red Blood Cells, Anemia, and Polycythemia

  34. A mountaineer spending weeks at high altitude develops an increased red cell count. The physiological basis is:

    • A. Direct stimulation of marrow by cold
    • B. Decreased plasma volume only
    • C. Increased iron absorption alone
    • D. Splenic contraction
    • E. Hypoxia-driven erythropoietin secretion increasing red cell production
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: E. Hypoxia-driven erythropoietin secretion increasing red cell production

    High-altitude hypoxia stabilizes hypoxia-inducible factor in the kidney, boosting erythropoietin secretion and accelerating erythropoiesis over days to weeks. The resulting higher hemoglobin concentration restores arterial oxygen content toward normal — the classic acclimatization response, alongside increased 2,3-BPG.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 33: Red Blood Cells, Anemia, and Polycythemia

  35. A raised reticulocyte count in an anemic patient indicates:

    • A. Megaloblastic arrest
    • B. An active marrow response, as in hemolysis or recovery from blood loss or B12 therapy
    • C. Bone marrow failure
    • D. Aplastic anemia
    • E. Iron deficiency
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. An active marrow response, as in hemolysis or recovery from blood loss or B12 therapy

    Reticulocytes are immature red cells newly released from the marrow, so a high count proves the marrow is responding vigorously — typical of hemolytic anemias or recovery after hemorrhage or after starting B12/folate/iron therapy. A low reticulocyte count in anemia points to marrow failure (aplastic anemia) or ineffective production (megaloblastic anemia).

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 33: Red Blood Cells, Anemia, and Polycythemia

  36. In acute hemolytic anemia, serum haptoglobin characteristically:

    • A. Falls, because it is consumed binding free plasma hemoglobin
    • B. Remains unchanged
    • C. Increases iron absorption
    • D. Rises markedly
    • E. Is replaced by hemopexin
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Falls, because it is consumed binding free plasma hemoglobin

    Haptoglobin scavenges free hemoglobin released by intravascular hemolysis, and the complex is cleared by the liver — so circulating haptoglobin falls. A low haptoglobin with raised indirect bilirubin and LDH is a classic laboratory triad of hemolysis.

  37. A 20-year-old man of Mediterranean descent develops acute hemolysis with 'bite cells' after taking an antimalarial drug and eating fava beans. The most likely enzyme defect is:

    • A. Aldolase deficiency
    • B. Phosphofructokinase deficiency
    • C. Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency
    • D. Pyruvate kinase deficiency
    • E. Hexokinase deficiency
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency

    G6PD deficiency impairs the red cell's ability to generate NADPH, leaving it defenseless against oxidative stress from drugs (primaquine, sulfonamides), infections, or fava beans (favism). Denatured hemoglobin precipitates as Heinz bodies, and splenic removal of these inclusions leaves 'bite' and 'blister' cells on the film.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 33: Red Blood Cells, Anemia, and Polycythemia

  38. A 35-year-old man presents with fatigue, easy bruising, and recurrent infections. Blood counts show pancytopenia with a hypocellular bone marrow. The most likely diagnosis is:

    • A. Hemolytic anemia
    • B. Iron deficiency anemia
    • C. Polycythemia vera
    • D. Aplastic anemia
    • E. Thalassemia minor
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Aplastic anemia

    Aplastic anemia is bone marrow failure affecting all cell lines — anemia (fatigue), thrombocytopenia (bleeding/bruising), and neutropenia (infections) — with an empty, fatty marrow on biopsy. Causes include drugs, chemicals, radiation, and autoimmune destruction of stem cells; severe cases require immunosuppression or stem cell transplantation.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 33: Red Blood Cells, Anemia, and Polycythemia

  39. Mean corpuscular volume (MCV) is characteristically HIGH in:

    • A. Sideroblastic anemia
    • B. Iron deficiency anemia
    • C. Megaloblastic anemia from B12 or folate deficiency
    • D. Anemia of chronic disease
    • E. Thalassemia
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Megaloblastic anemia from B12 or folate deficiency

    Defective DNA synthesis in B12 or folate deficiency delays nuclear maturation while cytoplasmic growth continues, producing large (macrocytic, high MCV) red cells — megaloblastic anemia. Iron deficiency, thalassemia, and chronic disease instead produce microcytic (low MCV) cells from impaired hemoglobin synthesis.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 33: Red Blood Cells, Anemia, and Polycythemia

  40. The increased blood viscosity in polycythemia raises the risk of thrombosis mainly because:

    • A. Fibrinogen is depleted
    • B. High hematocrit increases resistance to flow, slowing circulation and promoting stasis-related clotting
    • C. Platelets become hyperactive
    • D. Vessel walls weaken
    • E. White cells aggregate
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. High hematocrit increases resistance to flow, slowing circulation and promoting stasis-related clotting

    Blood viscosity rises steeply with hematocrit; in polycythemia the thickened blood flows sluggishly, especially in small vessels, and stasis predisposes to thrombosis. This is why venesection (or myelosuppression) to lower the hematocrit is a cornerstone of managing polycythemia vera.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 33: Red Blood Cells, Anemia, and Polycythemia

  41. In chronic kidney disease, anemia develops primarily because:

    • A. Of excess hepcidin only
    • B. Of iron overload
    • C. Of vitamin B12 excess
    • D. The failing kidneys produce too little erythropoietin
    • E. Red cells live longer
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. The failing kidneys produce too little erythropoietin

    The diseased kidney's peritubular cells cannot secrete adequate erythropoietin in response to hypoxia, so erythropoiesis falls — producing the normocytic anemia of chronic kidney disease. Treatment is recombinant human erythropoietin (with iron supplementation), which corrects the hormonal deficiency directly.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 33: Red Blood Cells, Anemia, and Polycythemia

  42. A man living at high altitude develops a raised haemoglobin concentration. Which hormone, released mainly from the kidneys in response to hypoxia, drives this increase?

    • A. Erythropoietin from peritubular cells of the kidney
    • B. Thrombopoietin from the liver
    • C. Granulocyte colony-stimulating factor from endothelium
    • D. Transferrin from macrophages
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Erythropoietin from peritubular cells of the kidney

    Tissue hypoxia stimulates renal peritubular interstitial cells to release erythropoietin, which acts on marrow erythroid progenitors to increase red cell production. Thrombopoietin regulates platelets, G-CSF regulates neutrophils, and transferrin transports iron rather than signalling red cell production.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Red Blood Cells, Anemia, and Polycythemia

  43. A vegan patient develops a smooth, sore tongue, paraesthesiae and a macrocytic anaemia with hypersegmented neutrophils. Deficiency of which vitamin best explains this picture?

    • A. Vitamin B12 (cobalamin)
    • B. Vitamin C
    • C. Iron
    • D. Vitamin K
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Vitamin B12 (cobalamin)

    Vitamin B12 is needed for DNA synthesis and myelin maintenance; its deficiency causes megaloblastic macrocytic anaemia with hypersegmented neutrophils, glossitis and neurological symptoms. Iron deficiency gives microcytic anaemia, vitamin C deficiency causes scurvy, and vitamin K deficiency impairs clotting, not red cell maturation.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Red Blood Cells, Anemia, and Polycythemia
