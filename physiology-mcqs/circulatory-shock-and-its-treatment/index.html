Home › Physiology MCQs › Circulatory Shock and Its Treatment

Circulatory shock is a state in which blood flow to the tissues is too low to meet their needs, so cells begin to fail. Common causes include severe blood loss, failure of the heart as a pump, widespread vessel widening, and blockage of blood flow. Treatment aims to restore blood volume, support the heart, and correct the underlying cause.

  1. A 25 year old male after a road traffic accident is pale, cold, sweating, with rapid weak pulse, BP 80/50 mmHg and low urine output. Which mechanism best explains his condition?

    • A. Decreased systemic vascular resistance due to widespread vasodilation of arterioles
    • B. Failure of the heart to pump effectively leading to increased venous pressure
    • C. Severe reduction in venous return and cardiac output due to hypovolemia
    • D. Increased capillary hydrostatic pressure causing fluid to shift into interstitial space
    • E. Excessive vagal stimulation leading to sudden bradycardia and hypotension
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Severe reduction in venous return and cardiac output due to hypovolemia

    Pale, cold, sweating skin with rapid weak pulse, hypotension (80/50 mmHg), and oliguria after trauma is hypovolaemic (haemorrhagic) shock. Blood loss reduces venous return and cardiac output, and compensatory sympathetic vasoconstriction and tachycardia fail to maintain pressure. Distributive shock would show warm skin and low resistance, cardiogenic shock would raise venous pressure with pulmonary congestion, and vagal collapse would cause bradycardia.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Circulatory Shock and Its Treatment

  2. Excess fluid retention in acute cardiac failure leads to all of the following EXCEPT:

    • A. Increased preload
    • B. Systemic oedema
    • C. Pulmonary oedema
    • D. Weakness of cardiac muscle
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Weakness of cardiac muscle

    Fluid retention in acute cardiac failure raises blood volume and preload, causing systemic and pulmonary oedema, hepatic congestion, and raised venous pressures, but it does not directly weaken cardiac muscle. Weakness of the muscle is the primary disease causing failure, not a consequence of retained fluid. All the oedema-related consequences follow from excess volume.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Circulatory Shock and Its Treatment

  3. In progressive irreversible shock with hepatic and renal damage, the significant cellular event is:

    • A. Enhanced ATP synthesis
    • B. Lysosomal rupture releasing hydrolytic enzymes
    • C. Up-regulation of oxidative enzymes
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Lysosomal rupture releasing hydrolytic enzymes

    In irreversible shock prolonged ischaemia damages lysosomal membranes; lysosomes rupture and release hydrolytic enzymes that digest the cell and surrounding tissue (autolysis), contributing to hepatic, renal, and myocardial injury. This cellular self-digestion marks the transition to irreversibility despite restored pressure. It is more specific than generalised ATP depletion alone in Guyton's account.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Circulatory Shock and Its Treatment

  4. Severe (increased) blood loss most directly leads to which type of shock?

    • A. Septic shock
    • B. Hypovolemic shock
    • C. Anaphylactic shock
    • D. Neurogenic shock
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Hypovolemic shock

    Loss of circulating blood volume reduces venous return and cardiac output, producing hypovolemic (hemorrhagic) shock. Septic shock is distributive from infection-mediated vasodilation.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Circulatory Shock and Its Treatment

  5. Fever with widespread vasodilation and hypotension in severe infection is characteristic of:

    • A. Hypovolemic shock
    • B. Cardiogenic shock
    • C. Septic shock
    • D. Obstructive shock
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Septic shock

    Septic shock is a distributive shock from infection, with fever, vasodilation and initially warm peripheries despite hypotension. Hypovolemic shock from blood loss typically has cold, clammy skin and no fever as the primary feature.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Circulatory Shock and Its Treatment

  6. How does anaphylactic shock differ from hypovolemic shock regarding cardiac output?

    • A. Cardiac output is markedly decreased
    • B. Cardiac output is increased above normal (distributive, high-output early)
    • C. Cardiac output is always zero
    • D. There is no difference in cardiac output
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Cardiac output is increased above normal (distributive, high-output early)

    Anaphylaxis causes massive histamine-mediated vasodilation and increased vascular permeability; early cardiac output may be high/normal due to low resistance, unlike hypovolemic shock where low volume gives low cardiac output. This high-output distributive pattern is the distinguishing point in the recall.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Circulatory Shock and Its Treatment

  7. Circulatory shock is best defined as:

    • A. Loss of more than 20% of blood volume
    • B. Low arterial blood pressure regardless of tissue perfusion
    • C. Excessive sympathetic nervous system activity
    • D. Generalized inadequate blood flow through the tissues causing cellular hypoxia
    • E. Failure of the heart as a pump
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Generalized inadequate blood flow through the tissues causing cellular hypoxia

    Shock is defined by inadequate tissue perfusion — blood flow too low to meet cellular metabolic needs — rather than by any single blood pressure value. Different types (hypovolemic, cardiogenic, obstructive, distributive) all converge on cellular hypoxia, and if prolonged, progress through compensated, progressive, and irreversible stages.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 24: Circulatory Shock and Its Treatment

  8. Mean arterial pressure is determined by cardiac output and total peripheral resistance. Which relationship is correct?

    • A. MAP = cardiac output × total peripheral resistance
    • B. MAP = venous return + total peripheral resistance
    • C. MAP = cardiac output + central venous pressure
    • D. MAP = cardiac output ÷ total peripheral resistance
    • E. MAP = stroke volume – heart rate
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. MAP = cardiac output × total peripheral resistance

    Mean arterial pressure equals cardiac output multiplied by total peripheral resistance (the circulatory equivalent of Ohm's law: pressure = flow × resistance). In shock, the body defends arterial pressure by raising both variables — increasing cardiac output (faster heart rate, stronger contraction) and constricting arterioles to raise resistance.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 24: Circulatory Shock and Its Treatment

  9. In the early (nonprogressive) stage of hemorrhagic shock, which compensatory response helps maintain arterial pressure?

    • A. Parasympathetic activation slowing the heart
    • B. Suppression of ADH release
    • C. Dilation of capacitance veins
    • D. Decreased myocardial contractility
    • E. Massive sympathetic activation causing vasoconstriction and tachycardia
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: E. Massive sympathetic activation causing vasoconstriction and tachycardia

    Falling arterial pressure unloads the baroreceptors, triggering intense sympathetic discharge: arteriolar vasoconstriction (raising total peripheral resistance), venoconstriction (mobilizing blood toward the heart), tachycardia, and increased contractility. Together with ADH and renin-angiotensin-aldosterone activation, these responses can fully compensate moderate blood loss — the nonprogressive stage.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 24: Circulatory Shock and Its Treatment

  10. A patient with a high spinal cord injury develops warm, flushed skin, severe hypotension, and bradycardia. The mechanism of shock is:

    • A. Cardiac tamponade
    • B. Cardiogenic pump failure
    • C. Pulmonary embolism
    • D. Loss of sympathetic vasomotor tone with widespread vasodilation
    • E. Acute blood loss
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Loss of sympathetic vasomotor tone with widespread vasodilation

    Neurogenic shock follows interruption of sympathetic outflow (e.g., spinal cord injury or spinal anesthesia). Without sympathetic vasomotor tone, arterioles and veins dilate massively, total peripheral resistance and venous return both fall, and cardiac output drops. The skin is warm because cutaneous vessels are dilated, unlike the cold, clammy skin of hypovolemic shock.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 24: Circulatory Shock and Its Treatment

  11. Minutes after a wasp sting, a young man develops wheezing, widespread hives, and collapsing blood pressure. The primary mediator and mechanism are:

    • A. Serotonin causing platelet aggregation
    • B. Norepinephrine causing vasoconstriction
    • C. Bradykinin causing vasoconstriction
    • D. Histamine causing vasodilation and increased capillary permeability
    • E. Acetylcholine causing bradycardia
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Histamine causing vasodilation and increased capillary permeability

    Anaphylactic shock is an IgE-mediated hypersensitivity reaction in which mast cells and basophils release histamine and other mediators. Histamine dilates arterioles and makes capillaries leaky, so plasma escapes into the tissues — blood pressure collapses while venous return falls. Bronchospasm and laryngeal edema add airway obstruction, making intramuscular epinephrine the urgent treatment.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 24: Circulatory Shock and Its Treatment

  12. A 65-year-old woman with a urinary tract infection develops fever, bounding pulses, warm extremities, and hypotension that does not improve with fluids. Blood cultures grow gram-negative rods. This picture is most consistent with:

    • A. Obstructive shock
    • B. Hypovolemic shock
    • C. Neurogenic shock
    • D. Septic (distributive) shock from endotoxin
    • E. Cardiogenic shock
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Septic (distributive) shock from endotoxin

    Septic shock is caused by bacterial toxins (especially endotoxin from gram-negative organisms) that trigger massive vasodilation and capillary leak. Early 'warm' shock shows high cardiac output with low resistance and warm skin, unlike the cold extremities of hypovolemic or cardiogenic shock. Despite the high output, maldistribution of flow leaves tissues hypoxic, and disseminated intravascular coagulation may follow.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 24: Circulatory Shock and Its Treatment

  13. A 58-year-old man develops crushing chest pain, cold clammy skin, pulmonary edema, and hypotension two hours after a large anterior myocardial infarction. The type of shock is:

    • A. Cardiogenic
    • B. Septic
    • C. Anaphylactic
    • D. Neurogenic
    • E. Hypovolemic
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Cardiogenic

    Cardiogenic shock results from pump failure — here, extensive myocardial necrosis after infarction so the heart cannot maintain cardiac output. Blood dams up behind the failing left ventricle (pulmonary edema) while forward flow falls, producing cold, clammy skin and hypotension. Treatment supports the pump (oxygen, inotropes such as dobutamine) rather than fluids alone.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 24: Circulatory Shock and Its Treatment

  14. A young man stabbed in the chest becomes hypotensive with distended neck veins and muffled heart sounds. The mechanism of shock is best described as:

    • A. Neurogenic from fear
    • B. Cardiogenic from myocardial contusion
    • C. Distributive from pain
    • D. Obstructive — impaired cardiac filling from cardiac tamponade
    • E. Hypovolemic from blood loss
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Obstructive — impaired cardiac filling from cardiac tamponade

    This is Beck's triad of cardiac tamponade: hypotension, distended neck veins, and muffled heart sounds. Blood in the pericardial sac compresses the heart so it cannot fill — an obstructive shock in which venous return is blocked despite adequate blood volume. The high venous pressure with low cardiac output distinguishes it from hypovolemic shock, where neck veins are flat.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 24: Circulatory Shock and Its Treatment

  15. Which feature distinguishes progressive shock from the earlier compensated stage?

    • A. Release of ADH and aldosterone
    • B. Increased heart rate
    • C. Shift of fluid from interstitium to capillaries
    • D. Activation of the sympathetic nervous system
    • E. A vicious cycle of falling cardiac output and worsening tissue perfusion that feeds on itself
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: E. A vicious cycle of falling cardiac output and worsening tissue perfusion that feeds on itself

    In progressive shock, tissue hypoxia damages the heart and vessels, which further reduces cardiac output and perfusion — a positive-feedback (vicious) cycle. Depressed myocardium, sludging of blood in capillaries, release of toxins from ischemic tissues, and clotting abnormalities all accelerate the decline. Once this cycle dominates, shock progresses even without further blood loss.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 24: Circulatory Shock and Its Treatment

  16. In irreversible shock, the cellular change that marks the point of no return is best described as:

    • A. Temporary sympathetic exhaustion
    • B. Reversible anaerobic glycolysis
    • C. Widespread cellular deterioration with lysosomal rupture and mitochondrial failure, so tissues cannot recover even if perfusion is restored
    • D. Mild intracellular acidosis
    • E. Transient potassium loss
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Widespread cellular deterioration with lysosomal rupture and mitochondrial failure, so tissues cannot recover even if perfusion is restored

    Prolonged severe hypoxia causes lysosomes to rupture, releasing destructive enzymes, while mitochondria fail and ATP production collapses. Once this cellular disintegration is widespread, restoring blood flow and pressure cannot save the tissues — transfusion and vasopressors become futile. This defines the irreversible stage.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 24: Circulatory Shock and Its Treatment

  17. The FIRST-line treatment for a patient in hypovolemic shock from acute hemorrhage is:

    • A. Rapid intravenous fluid (crystalloid) resuscitation, with blood transfusion if bleeding is major
    • B. Diuretics to reduce preload
    • C. Immediate high-dose vasopressors
    • D. Prolonged Trendelenburg positioning alone
    • E. Beta-blockers to slow the heart
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Rapid intravenous fluid (crystalloid) resuscitation, with blood transfusion if bleeding is major

    The fundamental deficit in hypovolemic shock is lost intravascular volume, so rapid IV crystalloid infusion is the first step, followed by blood transfusion when hemorrhage is significant. Vasopressors without volume are ineffective and can worsen tissue ischemia by further constricting already underfilled vessels. Definitive control of the bleeding source is equally essential.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 24: Circulatory Shock and Its Treatment

  18. Norepinephrine is often the vasopressor of choice in distributive (e.g., septic) shock because it:

    • A. Dilates all vascular beds
    • B. Blocks sympathetic receptors
    • C. Raises arterial pressure mainly by arteriolar vasoconstriction while preserving coronary and cerebral flow
    • D. Slows the heart rate
    • E. Increases capillary permeability
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Raises arterial pressure mainly by arteriolar vasoconstriction while preserving coronary and cerebral flow

    Norepinephrine is a potent alpha-adrenergic vasoconstrictor that raises total peripheral resistance and arterial pressure, with modest beta-1 effects supporting cardiac contractility. Unlike pure vasoconstrictors, it maintains perfusion of vital organs. In septic shock it is the recommended first-line vasopressor after adequate fluid resuscitation.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 24: Circulatory Shock and Its Treatment

  19. Dobutamine is preferred over pure vasoconstrictors in cardiogenic shock because it:

    • A. Blocks beta receptors
    • B. Reduces heart rate
    • C. Constricts veins to increase preload
    • D. Dilates coronary arteries exclusively
    • E. Increases myocardial contractility (positive inotropic effect) with relatively little vasoconstriction
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: E. Increases myocardial contractility (positive inotropic effect) with relatively little vasoconstriction

    In cardiogenic shock the core problem is pump failure, so an inotrope that strengthens contraction is logical. Dobutamine stimulates cardiac beta-1 receptors, increasing contractility and cardiac output, while causing mild vasodilation rather than the intense vasoconstriction of norepinephrine — avoiding extra afterload on the failing heart.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 24: Circulatory Shock and Its Treatment

  20. A 19-year-old woman feels nauseated, sweaty, and light-headed at the sight of blood, then briefly loses consciousness. The mechanism of this vasovagal syncope is:

    • A. Parasympathetic activation with bradycardia plus sympathetic withdrawal causing vasodilation
    • B. Sudden sympathetic surge with vasoconstriction
    • C. Acute blood loss
    • D. Hypoglycemia
    • E. Cardiac arrhythmia
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Parasympathetic activation with bradycardia plus sympathetic withdrawal causing vasodilation

    Emotional stress can trigger a vasovagal response: strong parasympathetic (vagal) outflow slows the heart while sympathetic vasomotor tone withdraws, dilating peripheral vessels. The combined bradycardia and vasodilation drop arterial pressure and cerebral perfusion, causing brief fainting. Recovery is rapid once the person is horizontal.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 24: Circulatory Shock and Its Treatment

  21. 'Sludging' of blood in the microcirculation during progressive shock contributes to deterioration mainly by:

    • A. Dilating precapillary sphincters
    • B. Blocking capillary flow as red cells aggregate, worsening tissue hypoxia and releasing toxic metabolites
    • C. Improving oxygen extraction
    • D. Increasing cardiac contractility
    • E. Increasing plasma volume
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Blocking capillary flow as red cells aggregate, worsening tissue hypoxia and releasing toxic metabolites

    In prolonged shock, sluggish flow lets red cells aggregate into rouleaux ('sludge') that plug capillaries. The resulting stagnant hypoxia injures tissues further and washes out acids, potassium, and toxic metabolites when flow momentarily returns — deepening the vicious cycle of progressive shock.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 24: Circulatory Shock and Its Treatment

  22. Placing a patient in shock with the head lower than the feet (head-down position) helps primarily by:

    • A. Using gravity to promote venous return from the lower body to the heart
    • B. Stimulating respiration
    • C. Reducing intracranial pressure
    • D. Dilating coronary arteries
    • E. Increasing sympathetic tone
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Using gravity to promote venous return from the lower body to the heart

    The head-down (Trendelenburg) position lets gravity assist venous return from the legs and abdomen toward the heart, transiently supporting cardiac output and cerebral perfusion. It is a temporizing measure while fluids and definitive treatment are given, not a substitute for volume resuscitation.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 24: Circulatory Shock and Its Treatment

  23. Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) in septic shock is dangerous because it causes:

    • A. Hypertension
    • B. Widespread microvascular clotting that consumes clotting factors and platelets, followed by severe bleeding
    • C. Isolated platelet increase
    • D. Pure vasodilation
    • E. Only excessive clotting with no bleeding
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Widespread microvascular clotting that consumes clotting factors and platelets, followed by severe bleeding

    Endotoxin and tissue injury in septic shock trigger diffuse activation of coagulation, forming microthrombi throughout the circulation. This consumes platelets and clotting factors (consumption coagulopathy), so the patient paradoxically bleeds — with prolonged clotting times, low fibrinogen, low platelets, and elevated D-dimers.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 24: Circulatory Shock and Its Treatment

  24. Administration of oxygen is a standard part of shock treatment because:

    • A. It replaces lost blood volume
    • B. It stimulates ADH release
    • C. It increases the oxygen content of the limited blood flow reaching tissues, partially offsetting hypoxic injury
    • D. It raises blood pressure directly
    • E. It constricts arterioles
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. It increases the oxygen content of the limited blood flow reaching tissues, partially offsetting hypoxic injury

    In shock, tissue blood flow is critically low, so maximizing the oxygen carried by each milliliter of blood helps preserve cellular metabolism. Supplemental oxygen raises arterial oxygen content and can slow the progression of hypoxic cellular damage while perfusion is being restored with fluids, blood, and vasopressors.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 24: Circulatory Shock and Its Treatment

  25. Which hormonal responses help conserve fluid in the nonprogressive stage of shock?

    • A. Decreased cortisol
    • B. Increased ADH (water retention) and increased aldosterone via renin-angiotensin activation (sodium retention)
    • C. Decreased ADH and decreased aldosterone
    • D. Increased insulin and glucagon
    • E. Increased thyroid hormone
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Increased ADH (water retention) and increased aldosterone via renin-angiotensin activation (sodium retention)

    Falling pressure stimulates ADH release (conserving water at the kidneys) and activates the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (conserving sodium, and therefore water). Together with sympathetic venoconstriction and the shift of interstitial fluid into capillaries, these hormonal responses expand plasma volume during compensated shock.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 24: Circulatory Shock and Its Treatment

  26. A trauma patient has lost 30% of blood volume. The kidneys respond to the resulting hypotension by:

    • A. Increasing glomerular filtration markedly
    • B. Releasing renin, initiating angiotensin II and aldosterone formation to support pressure and volume
    • C. Suppressing erythropoietin
    • D. Excreting large amounts of sodium
    • E. Dilating afferent arterioles maximally
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Releasing renin, initiating angiotensin II and aldosterone formation to support pressure and volume

    Renal hypoperfusion triggers renin release from juxtaglomerular cells. Renin generates angiotensin II — a potent vasoconstrictor that also stimulates aldosterone secretion — and aldosterone promotes renal sodium and water retention. This renin-angiotensin-aldosterone activation is a cornerstone of the body's defense of arterial pressure in hemorrhagic shock.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 24: Circulatory Shock and Its Treatment
