Blood Cells, Immunity and Blood Coagulation MCQs for FMU 1st Year MBBS Physiology

Home › Physiology MCQs › Blood Cells, Immunity and Blood Coagulation

Blood contains red cells, white cells, and platelets suspended in plasma. Red cells carry oxygen, white cells defend against infection, and platelets help stop bleeding. Immunity provides a specific defence against particular organisms, while coagulation seals injured vessels with a fibrin clot.

  1. Which of the following is NOT normally found in basophil granules?

    • A. Heparin
    • B. Histamine
    • C. Heparinase
    • D. Slow-reacting substance of anaphylaxis
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Heparinase

    Basophil granules contain histamine, heparin and other mediators of allergic reactions. Heparinase is not a normal basophil granule constituent.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Blood Cells, Immunity and Blood Coagulation

  2. Which type of white blood cell is most abundant in blood?

    • A. Monocytes
    • B. Neutrophils
    • C. Eosinophils
    • D. Lymphocytes
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Neutrophils

    Neutrophils are the most abundant leukocytes, making up about 50-70% of the total white cell count. They are the first phagocytes to act in acute bacterial infection.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Blood Cells, Immunity and Blood Coagulation

  3. A high rate of red blood cell production occurs mainly because of:

    • A. High carbon dioxide only
    • B. High oxygen in tissues
    • C. Low oxygen in tissues
    • D. Low iron in diet only
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Low oxygen in tissues

    Tissue hypoxia (low oxygen) stimulates erythropoietin release, mainly from the kidneys. Erythropoietin then increases red cell production in the bone marrow.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Blood Cells, Immunity and Blood Coagulation

  4. Iron deficiency typically produces which type of anaemia?

    • A. Microcytic hypochromic anaemia
    • B. Normocytic haemolytic anaemia
    • C. Aplastic anaemia
    • D. Macrocytic anaemia
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Microcytic hypochromic anaemia

    Without enough iron, haem synthesis falls and red cells become small (microcytic) and pale (hypochromic). Vitamin B12 or folate deficiency, by contrast, gives a macrocytic anaemia.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Blood Cells, Immunity and Blood Coagulation

  5. Iron deficiency anaemia will most likely occur in which condition?

    • A. Dehydration
    • B. Acute fever
    • C. Polycythaemia
    • D. Chronic blood loss
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Chronic blood loss

    Chronic blood loss steadily drains body iron and is a common cause of iron deficiency anaemia; increased demand in pregnancy/lactation can also contribute. Acute blood loss first gives a normocytic picture until iron stores are exhausted.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Blood Cells, Immunity and Blood Coagulation

  6. About 15-30% of total body iron is stored in which form?

    • A. Haemoglobin
    • B. Transferrin
    • C. Myoglobin
    • D. Ferritin
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Ferritin

    Roughly 15-30% of body iron is stored as ferritin (and haemosiderin), mainly in the liver, spleen and bone marrow. Most of the rest is in haemoglobin, with only a tiny fraction on transferrin at any time.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Blood Cells, Immunity and Blood Coagulation

  7. If a patient has a parasitic worm infection, which white blood cells will be increased?

    • A. Monocytes
    • B. Neutrophils
    • C. Basophils
    • D. Eosinophils
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Eosinophils

    Eosinophils increase in parasitic (helminth) infections and allergic conditions. Neutrophils rise mainly in acute bacterial infections.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Blood Cells, Immunity and Blood Coagulation

  8. What is the first line of defence in inflammation?

    • A. Tissue macrophages
    • B. Plasma cells
    • C. Neutrophils
    • D. Lymphocytes
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Tissue macrophages

    Resident tissue macrophages act within minutes and form the first line of defence in inflammation. Neutrophils are the second line, arriving in large numbers over the next few hours.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Blood Cells, Immunity and Blood Coagulation

  9. In mid-fetal life, preprocessing (early maturation) of B lymphocytes occurs mainly in which organ?

    • A. Thymus
    • B. Liver
    • C. Tonsil
    • D. Spleen
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Liver

    In mid-fetal life the liver is the main site where B-lymphocyte precursors are processed. Later in life this role is taken over by the bone marrow, while T cells mature in the thymus.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Blood Cells, Immunity and Blood Coagulation

  10. Regarding erythroblastosis fetalis, the baby at risk is:

    • A. Baby with no Rh antigen exposure
    • B. Rh-negative baby of an Rh-positive mother
    • C. Any Rh-negative baby
    • D. Rh-positive baby of an Rh-negative mother
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Rh-positive baby of an Rh-negative mother

    Erythroblastosis fetalis occurs when an Rh-negative mother is sensitised to the Rh antigen of her Rh-positive baby. In a later pregnancy her anti-Rh antibodies cross the placenta and attack fetal red cells.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Blood Cells, Immunity and Blood Coagulation

  11. In erythroblastosis fetalis, what happens to the fetal blood?

    • A. Platelets increase sharply
    • B. Fetal red cells agglutinate and haemolyse
    • C. It becomes polycythaemic
    • D. Haemoglobin becomes entirely HbF in the mother
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Fetal red cells agglutinate and haemolyse

    Maternal anti-Rh antibodies cross the placenta and agglutinate/haemolyse fetal red cells. The resulting severe anaemia and jaundice can cause hydrops fetalis if untreated.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Blood Cells, Immunity and Blood Coagulation

  12. People who secrete blood-group antigens in their body fluids (e.g. saliva) are called:

    • A. Secretors
    • B. Universal donors
    • C. Non-secretors
    • D. Carriers
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Secretors

    About 80% of people are 'secretors' who secrete ABO antigens in saliva and other body fluids. This can be used in forensic and blood-group testing.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Blood Cells, Immunity and Blood Coagulation

  13. Which immunoglobulin can cross the placenta?

    • A. IgE
    • B. IgG
    • C. IgA
    • D. IgM
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. IgG

    IgG is the only antibody class that crosses the placenta and gives the newborn passive immunity. IgM is too large (pentamer) to cross, so raised IgM in a newborn suggests intrauterine infection.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Blood Cells, Immunity and Blood Coagulation

  14. The primary immune response is mainly mediated by which antibody?

    • A. IgG
    • B. IgE
    • C. IgM
    • D. IgA
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. IgM

    IgM is the first antibody produced in a primary immune response. In a secondary response, IgG predominates and is produced faster and in larger amounts. (Note in the recall sheet is read as IgM.)

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Blood Cells, Immunity and Blood Coagulation

  15. Regarding degradation of red blood cells and haemoglobin, which cells carry it out?

    • A. Reticuloendothelial (macrophage) cells
    • B. Endothelial cells only
    • C. Neutrophils
    • D. Platelets
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Reticuloendothelial (macrophage) cells

    Old red cells are phagocytosed and broken down by reticuloendothelial macrophages, mainly in the spleen, liver and bone marrow. The iron is recycled and the haem ring is converted to bilirubin.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Blood Cells, Immunity and Blood Coagulation
