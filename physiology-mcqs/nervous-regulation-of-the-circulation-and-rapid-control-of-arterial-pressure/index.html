Home › Physiology MCQs › Nervous Regulation of the Circulation and Rapid Control of Arterial Pressure

Arterial pressure is controlled from moment to moment mainly by the autonomic nervous system. Pressure sensors in the carotid sinus and aortic arch detect changes and send signals to the brainstem, which adjusts heart rate and the tone of the blood vessels. This reflex corrects a fall or rise in pressure within seconds.

  1. A healthy volunteer receives a rapid intravenous infusion of saline. Heart rate increases shortly after the infusion. Which reflex best explains this response?

    • A. Baroreceptor reflex
    • B. Bainbridge reflex
    • C. Bezold-Jarisch reflex
    • D. Chemoreceptor reflex
    • E. Cushing reflex
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Bainbridge reflex

    Rapid saline infusion increases right atrial pressure and stretches atrial stretch receptors, triggering the Bainbridge reflex: afferent vagal signals to the medulla reduce vagal tone and increase sympathetic drive, producing reflex tachycardia. It is distinct from the baroreflex (which would slow the heart if arterial pressure rose) and from a direct Frank-Starling increase in stroke volume.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Nervous Regulation of the Circulation and Rapid Control of Arterial Pressure

  2. A person stands up quickly and becomes lightheaded. Which reflex compensates to maintain cerebral perfusion?

    • A. Increase in vagal outflow
    • B. Increase in sympathetic activity
    • C. Decrease in venous return
    • D. Decrease in heart rate
    • E. Activation of chemoreceptor reflex
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Increase in sympathetic activity

    Standing pools blood in the legs and threatens cerebral perfusion; the baroreceptor reflex compensates by reducing baroreceptor firing, which increases sympathetic outflow (vasoconstriction and tachycardia) and withdraws vagal tone. Increased sympathetic activity is therefore the compensatory limb. Parasympathetic increase or sympathetic withdrawal would worsen hypotension.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Nervous Regulation of the Circulation and Rapid Control of Arterial Pressure

  3. Baro-receptor control system contributes to blood homeostasis in decreased blood pressure by;

    • A. Noci -ceptors insensitization
    • B. Mechano -receptors activation
    • C. Stretch receptors relaxation
    • D. Tactile receptors sensitization
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Stretch receptors relaxation

    Baroreceptors are stretch receptors in the carotid sinus and aortic arch. When arterial pressure falls, vessel stretch and receptor firing decrease; reduced afferent firing is interpreted by the medulla as hypotension, leading to increased sympathetic and decreased vagal outflow to restore pressure. The receptors therefore signal by relaxation (less stretch) in decreased pressure, not by increased firing.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Nervous Regulation of the Circulation and Rapid Control of Arterial Pressure

  4. Carotid sinus massage slows heart rate. Which reflex mediates this effect?

    • A. Bainbridge reflex
    • B. Bezold-Jarisch reflex
    • C. Baroreceptor reflex
    • D. Chemoreceptor reflex
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Baroreceptor reflex

    Carotid sinus massage increases pressure on carotid baroreceptors, increasing afferent firing via the glossopharyngeal nerve to the medulla. The baroreceptor reflex responds with increased vagal and decreased sympathetic outflow, slowing SA nodal rate and AV conduction and lowering heart rate. It is the arterial baroreflex, not a chemoreflex or Bainbridge reflex.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Nervous Regulation of the Circulation and Rapid Control of Arterial Pressure

  5. In chronic hypertension, baroreceptors fail to correct elevated blood pressure primarily because:

    • A. They adapt to higher pressure set point
    • B. They lose sensitivity to stretch
    • C. Sympathetic nerves degenerate
    • D. Afferent fibers are blocked
    • E. Central integration of signals becomes ineffective
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. They adapt to higher pressure set point

    Baroreceptors are highly effective for acute pressure changes but reset within days to the prevailing higher pressure in chronic hypertension; their firing range recentres around the new set point. They therefore defend the elevated pressure rather than correcting it. They do not fail by ceasing to fire or by structural loss alone.

    Source: Guyton and Hall — Nervous Regulation of the Circulation and Rapid Control of Arterial Pressure

  6. Carotid sinus baroreceptors transmit signals to the brainstem via the ______ nerve, while aortic arch baroreceptors use the ______ nerve.

    • A. Vagus; glossopharyngeal
    • B. Glossopharyngeal; vagus
    • C. Facial; trigeminal
    • D. Hypoglossal; accessory
    • E. Vagus; vagus
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Glossopharyngeal; vagus

    Stretch receptors in the carotid sinus (at the carotid bifurcation) signal via Hering's nerve, a branch of the glossopharyngeal (CN IX), while aortic arch receptors travel in the vagus (CN X). Both converge on the nucleus tractus solitarius in the medulla, which orchestrates the baroreceptor reflex.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 18: Nervous Regulation of the Circulation and Rapid Control of Arterial Pressure

  7. Within seconds of acute blood loss, falling arterial pressure unloads the baroreceptors. The immediate reflex response is:

    • A. Decreased baroreceptor firing, leading to increased sympathetic outflow and decreased vagal tone
    • B. Increased baroreceptor firing with bradycardia
    • C. Vasodilation of skeletal muscle beds
    • D. Suppression of ADH release
    • E. Increased vagal tone to the heart
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Decreased baroreceptor firing, leading to increased sympathetic outflow and decreased vagal tone

    Baroreceptors fire less when stretched less. The medulla responds by withdrawing parasympathetic tone and unleashing sympathetic outflow: heart rate and contractility rise, arterioles and veins constrict. This rapid response (seconds) defends arterial pressure during hemorrhage until slower hormonal and renal mechanisms engage.

  8. The vasomotor center in the medulla and lower pons contains a vasoconstrictor area whose tonic activity maintains basal vascular tone. This area is located:

    • A. In the cerebral cortex
    • B. In the hypothalamus only
    • C. In the spinal cord
    • D. Bilaterally in the anterolateral medulla
    • E. In the cerebellum
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Bilaterally in the anterolateral medulla

    The vasoconstrictor area lies bilaterally in the anterolateral upper medulla, sending tonic sympathetic excitation down the cord to maintain partial arteriolar constriction at rest. Adjacent vasodilator and sensory areas modulate it. Loss of this tone (as in spinal shock) causes profound vasodilation and hypotension.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 18: Nervous Regulation of the Circulation and Rapid Control of Arterial Pressure

  9. Strong parasympathetic (vagal) stimulation of the heart primarily:

    • A. Greatly increases contractility
    • B. Slows the heart rate and slows AV conduction
    • C. Constricts coronary arteries
    • D. Increases venous return
    • E. Releases norepinephrine at the SA node
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Slows the heart rate and slows AV conduction

    Vagal fibers richly innervate the SA and AV nodes (less so the ventricles), releasing acetylcholine that slows diastolic depolarization and AV conduction — decreasing heart rate and potentially causing AV block. This is why carotid massage or the Valsalva maneuver can terminate supraventricular tachycardia.

  10. A patient with a rapidly expanding intracranial hemorrhage develops rising blood pressure with widening pulse pressure, bradycardia, and irregular respirations. This Cushing response is triggered by:

    • A. Carotid sinus stimulation
    • B. Aortic chemoreceptors
    • C. Brainstem ischemia from raised intracranial pressure compressing cerebral vessels
    • D. Hemorrhagic hypotension
    • E. Vagal afferents from the lungs
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Brainstem ischemia from raised intracranial pressure compressing cerebral vessels

    When intracranial pressure approaches arterial pressure, cerebral vessels are compressed and the brainstem becomes ischemic. The CNS ischemic response triggers massive sympathetic vasoconstriction (raising pressure to perfuse the brain), while parasympathetic activation slows the heart — the Cushing triad of hypertension, bradycardia, and irregular breathing, a pre-terminal sign of herniation.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 18: Nervous Regulation of the Circulation and Rapid Control of Arterial Pressure

  11. The peripheral chemoreceptors in the carotid and aortic bodies respond most sensitively to:

    • A. Decreased arterial PO2 (with lesser responses to increased PCO2 and decreased pH)
    • B. Increased arterial PO2
    • C. Decreased blood glucose
    • D. Increased blood pressure
    • E. Decreased body temperature
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Decreased arterial PO2 (with lesser responses to increased PCO2 and decreased pH)

    Carotid and aortic body chemoreceptors fire mainly when arterial PO2 falls (below about 60 mmHg the response becomes steep), with additional stimulation by high PCO2 and low pH. They drive increased ventilation and sympathetic vasoconstriction — a backup pressure defense when hypotension threatens tissue oxygenation.

  12. Rapid intravenous infusion of fluid stretches the atrial walls. The Bainbridge reflex in response to this atrial stretch causes:

    • A. Bradycardia and vasodilation
    • B. Increased ADH release
    • C. Decreased heart rate via vagal afferents
    • D. Increased heart rate via sympathetic activation
    • E. Immediate diuresis
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Increased heart rate via sympathetic activation

    Atrial stretch receptors (low-pressure receptors) trigger the Bainbridge reflex: vagal afferents signal the medulla, which increases heart rate through sympathetic activation and vagal withdrawal. This helps the heart handle increased venous return. The same atrial stretch also suppresses ADH and releases ANP, promoting later diuresis.

  13. During the strain phase (phase II) of a Valsalva maneuver, the pulse pressure typically:

    • A. Widens markedly
    • B. Narrows because decreased venous return reduces stroke volume
    • C. Remains unchanged
    • D. Disappears entirely
    • E. Widens due to increased contractility
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Narrows because decreased venous return reduces stroke volume

    Straining against a closed glottis raises intrathoracic pressure, impeding venous return; stroke volume and pulse pressure fall (phase II). Baroreceptors respond with tachycardia and vasoconstriction. On release (phase IV), the restored venous return ejected into constricted vessels causes a pressure overshoot with reflex bradycardia — the basis of the Valsalva ratio test of autonomic function.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 18: Nervous Regulation of the Circulation and Rapid Control of Arterial Pressure

  14. Firm massage of the carotid sinus in a patient with supraventricular tachycardia is expected to:

    • A. Increase heart rate
    • B. Cause hypertension
    • C. Slow AV conduction and may terminate the arrhythmia via increased vagal tone
    • D. Dilate the pupils
    • E. Increase cardiac contractility
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Slow AV conduction and may terminate the arrhythmia via increased vagal tone

    Carotid massage artificially stretches baroreceptors, increasing vagal outflow and sympathetic withdrawal. The enhanced vagal tone slows SA node firing and AV nodal conduction, which can break re-entrant SVT circuits. Blood pressure falls simultaneously — so it must be done cautiously, especially with carotid disease.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 18: Nervous Regulation of the Circulation and Rapid Control of Arterial Pressure

  15. In chronic hypertension, the baroreceptor reflex becomes less effective at lowering blood pressure over days because:

    • A. The baroreceptors reset (adapt) to the higher pressure level
    • B. The vasomotor center is destroyed
    • C. Sympathetic nerves degenerate
    • D. The heart becomes denervated
    • E. Aldosterone blocks the reflex
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. The baroreceptors reset (adapt) to the higher pressure level

    Baroreceptors adapt: within days of sustained hypertension they reset their firing threshold to the new higher pressure, treating it as normal. This is why the baroreflex powerfully buffers acute pressure swings but cannot correct chronic hypertension — long-term control belongs to the renal-body fluid system, which does not reset.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 18: Nervous Regulation of the Circulation and Rapid Control of Arterial Pressure

  16. Stretch of the cardiac atria by volume expansion causes all of the following EXCEPT:

    • A. Suppression of ADH release
    • B. Increased heart rate via the Bainbridge reflex
    • C. Release of atrial natriuretic peptide
    • D. Increased renal sodium reabsorption
    • E. Increased glomerular filtration via ANP
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Increased renal sodium reabsorption

    Atrial stretch triggers a coordinated volume-unloading response: ADH is suppressed, ANP is released (causing natriuresis, diuresis, and vasodilation), and heart rate rises. Increased renal sodium reabsorption would worsen volume overload, so it does not occur — ANP actually inhibits tubular sodium reabsorption.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 18: Nervous Regulation of the Circulation and Rapid Control of Arterial Pressure

  17. Sympathetic stimulation constricts most arterioles through ______ receptors and increases heart rate and contractility through ______ receptors.

    • A. Beta-2; alpha-1
    • B. Alpha-1; beta-1
    • C. Muscarinic; nicotinic
    • D. Beta-1; alpha-1
    • E. Dopaminergic; alpha-2
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Alpha-1; beta-1

    Norepinephrine released from sympathetic endings constricts arterioles and veins via alpha-1 receptors, while cardiac stimulation (increased rate, conduction, and contractility) is via beta-1 receptors. This dual action — vasoconstriction plus cardiac stimulation — is how the sympathetic system rapidly raises arterial pressure.

  18. The resting heart rate of a transplanted (denervated) human heart is about 100 beats/min, higher than normal. This is because:

    • A. Sympathetic nerves are overactive
    • B. The donor heart is smaller
    • C. Loss of tonic vagal (parasympathetic) slowing leaves the intrinsic SA node rate unopposed
    • D. Circulating catecholamines are absent
    • E. The AV node is damaged
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Loss of tonic vagal (parasympathetic) slowing leaves the intrinsic SA node rate unopposed

    The normal resting rate (about 70) reflects strong tonic vagal braking on the SA node's intrinsic rate (about 100). Transplantation severs both autonomic nerves; without vagal tone, the heart beats at its intrinsic rate. The denervated heart also responds slowly to exercise (relying on circulating catecholamines) and cannot show reflex bradycardia.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 18: Nervous Regulation of the Circulation and Rapid Control of Arterial Pressure

  19. A patient with pure autonomic failure faints on standing. The primary circulatory defect is:

    • A. Failure of baroreceptor-mediated sympathetic vasoconstriction on standing, causing blood to pool in the legs
    • B. Excessive vagal tone
    • C. Mitral stenosis
    • D. Anemia
    • E. Dehydration alone
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Failure of baroreceptor-mediated sympathetic vasoconstriction on standing, causing blood to pool in the legs

    On standing, 500-800 mL of blood pools in leg veins; normally baroreceptors trigger immediate sympathetic vasoconstriction and venoconstriction to maintain venous return. In autonomic failure this reflex is absent, cardiac output falls, cerebral perfusion drops, and syncope follows. These patients depend on physical countermeasures (compression stockings, leg crossing) instead of reflexes.

  20. A young blood donor becomes pale, sweaty, and nauseated, then faints at the sight of blood. The faint (vasovagal syncope) is caused by:

    • A. Sympathetic overactivity
    • B. Seizure activity
    • C. Cerebral hemorrhage
    • D. Paradoxical sympathetic withdrawal with vagal bradycardia and vasodilation
    • E. Acute myocardial infarction
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. Paradoxical sympathetic withdrawal with vagal bradycardia and vasodilation

    Emotional stress can trigger paradoxical withdrawal of sympathetic vasoconstrictor tone plus strong vagal activation: vessels dilate, the heart slows, pressure plummets, and cerebral perfusion fails. Prodromal pallor, sweating, and nausea reflect the autonomic storm. Unlike cardiac syncope, recovery on lying flat is rapid and complete.

  21. Stimulation of chemosensitive vagal C-fiber receptors in the ventricular walls (Bezold-Jarisch reflex), as in inferior myocardial infarction, produces:

    • A. Tachycardia and hypertension
    • B. Bradycardia, hypotension, and coronary vasodilation
    • C. Increased contractility
    • D. Pulmonary edema
    • E. Atrial fibrillation
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Bradycardia, hypotension, and coronary vasodilation

    Ventricular receptor stimulation (by ischemia or chemical stimuli) triggers powerful vagal afferent traffic: bradycardia, hypotension, and apnea. This explains the paradoxical bradycardia and hypotension sometimes seen in inferior MI — a vagal reflex from the ischemic ventricle, not pump failure — and it responds to atropine.

  22. Compared with norepinephrine, an epinephrine infusion tends to produce a wider pulse pressure because epinephrine:

    • A. Is a stronger alpha agonist
    • B. Decreases heart rate
    • C. Stimulates beta-2 mediated vasodilation in muscle beds while increasing cardiac output, limiting the diastolic rise
    • D. Has no cardiac effect
    • E. Constricts veins only
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Stimulates beta-2 mediated vasodilation in muscle beds while increasing cardiac output, limiting the diastolic rise

    Epinephrine's beta-2 vasodilation in skeletal muscle lowers total peripheral resistance (limiting diastolic rise) while its beta-1 cardiac stimulation raises systolic pressure — widening pulse pressure. Norepinephrine, predominantly alpha, raises both systolic and diastolic, keeping pulse pressure narrower. This distinction is a classic pharmacology-physiology crossover.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 18: Nervous Regulation of the Circulation and Rapid Control of Arterial Pressure

  23. After high spinal anesthesia blocks sympathetic outflow, a patient becomes hypotensive mainly because of:

    • A. Loss of sympathetic vasoconstrictor tone causing arteriolar and venous dilation
    • B. Direct myocardial depression
    • C. Increased vagal tone to blood vessels
    • D. Renal failure
    • E. Histamine release
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Loss of sympathetic vasoconstrictor tone causing arteriolar and venous dilation

    Sympathetic block removes tonic vasoconstriction: arterioles dilate (decreasing total peripheral resistance) and veins dilate (pooling blood, decreasing venous return and cardiac output). The result is neurogenic hypotension, often with paradoxical bradycardia from unopposed vagal tone. Treatment is vasopressors and fluids.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 18: Nervous Regulation of the Circulation and Rapid Control of Arterial Pressure

  24. A trauma patient loses 800 mL of blood rapidly. Which control system responds within seconds to defend arterial pressure?

    • A. The renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system
    • B. Increased ADH secretion
    • C. Pressure natriuresis
    • D. The baroreceptor-mediated autonomic reflex
    • E. Renal fluid retention
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: D. The baroreceptor-mediated autonomic reflex

    Circulatory control is layered by speed: neural baroreflexes act in seconds, hormonal systems (RAAS, ADH) in minutes to hours, and renal-body fluid mechanisms over hours to days. Only the autonomic baroreflex is fast enough to counter sudden hemorrhage; the others provide sustained, long-term correction afterward.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 18: Nervous Regulation of the Circulation and Rapid Control of Arterial Pressure

  25. A 25-year-old man with acute blood loss has cold, clammy skin, tachycardia of 130/min, and a narrow pulse pressure. These findings reflect:

    • A. Parasympathetic activation
    • B. Massive sympathetic activation with cutaneous vasoconstriction and cardiac stimulation
    • C. Vasovagal syncope
    • D. Anaphylaxis
    • E. Spinal shock
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Massive sympathetic activation with cutaneous vasoconstriction and cardiac stimulation

    Cold clammy skin reflects sympathetic alpha-mediated cutaneous vasoconstriction shunting blood to vital organs; tachycardia reflects beta-1 cardiac stimulation; narrow pulse pressure reflects reduced stroke volume from low venous return. This is the classic compensated hemorrhagic shock picture — the sympathetic nervous system sacrificing skin and splanchnic perfusion to preserve brain and heart.
