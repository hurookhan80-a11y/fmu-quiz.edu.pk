Cardiac Failure MCQs for FMU 1st Year MBBS Physiology

Home › Physiology MCQs › Cardiac Failure

Cardiac failure occurs when the heart cannot pump enough blood to meet the needs of the body, either because it cannot fill properly or because it cannot contract enough. Blood backs up behind the failing side, fluid may collect in the lungs or tissues, and the body activates compensating mechanisms that help at first but worsen the condition over time.

  1. In chronic heart failure, which compensatory mechanism initially maintains cardiac output but ultimately worsens the failure by increasing myocardial oxygen demand and promoting remodeling?

    • A. Vagal withdrawal
    • B. Sympathetic activation with norepinephrine release
    • C. Increased vagal tone
    • D. Reduced afterload
    • E. Decreased heart rate
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Sympathetic activation with norepinephrine release

    In heart failure, falling cardiac output triggers baroreceptor-mediated sympathetic activation. The resulting norepinephrine raises heart rate and contractility (supporting output short-term) but chronically increases myocardial O2 demand, causes myocyte apoptosis/fibrosis, and drives maladaptive remodeling — which is why beta-blockers improve survival in HFrEF.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 22: Cardiac Failure

  2. A patient with left-sided heart failure develops dyspnea on exertion and pulmonary edema. Which hemodynamic change directly explains the transudation of fluid into alveoli?

    • A. Increased pulmonary capillary hydrostatic pressure
    • B. Decreased plasma oncotic pressure
    • C. Increased alveolar surface tension
    • D. Decreased left atrial pressure
    • E. Increased systemic vascular resistance
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Increased pulmonary capillary hydrostatic pressure

    Left heart failure raises left atrial and pulmonary venous pressures, which back up into pulmonary capillaries. When capillary hydrostatic pressure exceeds oncotic pressure (Starling forces), fluid transudes into the interstitium and alveoli, causing pulmonary edema and dyspnea.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 22: Cardiac Failure

  3. Which finding distinguishes high-output cardiac failure from low-output failure?

    • A. Low ejection fraction
    • B. Elevated cardiac output with signs of congestion
    • C. Hypotension with cold extremities
    • D. Reduced venous return
    • E. Decreased heart rate
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Elevated cardiac output with signs of congestion

    High-output failure (severe anemia, thyrotoxicosis, beriberi, AV fistula, Paget disease) features an abnormally HIGH cardiac output that still cannot meet exaggerated metabolic demands, with congestion signs. Low-output failure shows reduced output with poor perfusion. Ejection fraction is typically preserved in high-output failure.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 22: Cardiac Failure

  4. In the Frank-Starling mechanism as applied to the failing heart, what happens when end-diastolic volume rises beyond the optimal sarcomere length (~2.2 µm)?

    • A. Stroke volume keeps rising indefinitely
    • B. Stroke volume plateaus then falls as actin-myosin overlap becomes suboptimal
    • C. Contractility increases via catecholamines
    • D. Afterload decreases
    • E. Heart rate falls
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Stroke volume plateaus then falls as actin-myosin overlap becomes suboptimal

    The Frank-Starling curve rises with preload up to optimal sarcomere length (~2.0–2.2 µm) then descends: overstretched fibers have poor actin-myosin overlap and generate less force. In decompensated failure the heart operates on the flat/descending limb, so further volume loading worsens output — the rationale for diuretics.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 22: Cardiac Failure

  5. A 68-year-old with long-standing hypertension now has heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF). The most likely underlying myocardial change is:

    • A. Ventricular dilation with thin walls
    • B. Concentric hypertrophy with diastolic stiffness
    • C. Myocyte loss from infarction
    • D. Valvular regurgitation
    • E. Pericardial constriction
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Concentric hypertrophy with diastolic stiffness

    Chronic pressure overload (hypertension, aortic stenosis) causes concentric hypertrophy — wall thickening without chamber dilation. The stiff, noncompliant ventricle fills poorly in diastole (diastolic dysfunction) while systolic ejection fraction stays normal — the hallmark of HFpEF.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 22: Cardiac Failure

  6. Which neurohormonal system, activated in heart failure, causes BOTH vasoconstriction and aldosterone-mediated sodium/water retention?

    • A. Natriuretic peptide system
    • B. Renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system
    • C. Kallikrein-kinin system
    • D. Prostacyclin pathway
    • E. Nitric oxide pathway
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system

    Falling renal perfusion in heart failure activates RAAS: renin → angiotensin II (potent vasoconstrictor that also stimulates ADH and sympathetic activity) → aldosterone (distal nephron Na⁺/water reabsorption). This raises preload/afterload and causes edema — the target of ACE inhibitors, ARBs, and mineralocorticoid antagonists.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 22: Cardiac Failure

  7. Cardiac reserve is best defined as:

    • A. The difference between resting and maximal cardiac output
    • B. The ejection fraction at rest
    • C. The stroke volume during exercise
    • D. The heart rate reserve
    • E. The coronary blood flow at rest
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. The difference between resting and maximal cardiac output

    Cardiac reserve = maximum cardiac output achievable minus resting output (normally ~4–5 fold increase via higher heart rate and stroke volume). In heart failure, reserve is exhausted — output cannot rise with exertion, producing exertional dyspnea and fatigue.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 22: Cardiac Failure

  8. A patient in acute decompensated heart failure has pink frothy sputum, crackles, and SpO2 82%. The immediate physiological goal of sitting upright and giving high-flow oxygen is to:

    • A. Reduce preload and improve alveolar oxygenation
    • B. Increase contractility
    • C. Dilate coronary arteries
    • D. Reduce heart rate
    • E. Increase systemic vascular resistance
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: A. Reduce preload and improve alveolar oxygenation

    Upright posture pools blood in dependent veins, reducing venous return (preload) and pulmonary congestion; oxygen raises alveolar PO2 to counter the diffusion barrier of edema fluid. Both buy time while diuretics/vasodilators reduce filling pressures. Pink frothy sputum indicates alveolar edema with capillary stress rupture.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 22: Cardiac Failure

  9. Which drug improves mortality in HFrEF primarily by blocking the maladaptive effects of chronic sympathetic activation?

    • A. Digoxin
    • B. Furosemide
    • C. Metoprolol succinate
    • D. Amlodipine
    • E. Nitrates
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Metoprolol succinate

    Evidence-based beta-blockers (metoprolol succinate, carvedilol, bisoprolol) blunt chronic catecholamine toxicity — reducing heart rate, myocardial O2 demand, arrhythmia risk, and remodeling — and lower mortality in HFrEF. Digoxin and diuretics improve symptoms but not survival; amlodipine is neutral.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 22: Cardiac Failure

  10. In right-sided heart failure, which sign reflects elevated systemic venous pressure?

    • A. Pulmonary crackles
    • B. Orthopnea
    • C. Jugular venous distension with hepatojugular reflux
    • D. Pink frothy sputum
    • E. Paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. Jugular venous distension with hepatojugular reflux

    Right heart failure backs pressure into the systemic veins: jugular venous distension, hepatojugular reflux (sustained JVP rise with abdominal pressure), hepatomegaly, and dependent edema. Pulmonary crackles, orthopnea, and PND are left-sided signs.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 22: Cardiac Failure

  11. Digoxin helps in heart failure mainly by:

    • A. Blocking beta receptors
    • B. Inhibiting Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase, raising intracellular Ca²⁺ and contractility
    • C. Dilating arterioles
    • D. Blocking aldosterone
    • E. Reducing heart rate via calcium channels
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Inhibiting Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase, raising intracellular Ca²⁺ and contractility

    Digoxin inhibits the sarcolemmal Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase → intracellular Na⁺ rises → reduced Na⁺/Ca²⁺ exchange → more cytosolic Ca²⁺ for troponin C → positive inotropy. It also enhances vagal tone (slows AV conduction in atrial fibrillation). It improves symptoms/hospitalization but not mortality, with a narrow therapeutic index.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 22: Cardiac Failure

  12. A failing heart shows decreased ejection fraction with increased end-systolic volume. Which pressure-volume loop change is most characteristic?

    • A. Loop shifts left with increased stroke volume
    • B. Loop shifts right with decreased stroke width
    • C. Increased end-systolic elastance
    • D. Decreased diastolic filling
    • E. Narrower loop with higher ejection fraction
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Loop shifts right with decreased stroke width

    Systolic failure reduces contractility (lower end-systolic elastance), so the PV loop shifts rightward (larger volumes) and narrows (smaller stroke volume). Ejection fraction (SV/EDV) falls. Diuretics/vasodilators shift the loop back leftward by reducing preload/afterload.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 22: Cardiac Failure

  13. Cheyne-Stokes respiration in advanced heart failure is caused by:

    • A. Metabolic acidosis
    • B. Prolonged circulation time delaying chemoreceptor feedback
    • C. Airway obstruction
    • D. Pulmonary fibrosis
    • E. Diaphragmatic paralysis
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Prolonged circulation time delaying chemoreceptor feedback

    In severe failure, low cardiac output prolongs blood transit time from lungs to brain chemoreceptors. This feedback delay makes ventilatory control oscillate: crescendo-decrescendo breathing with apneic pauses (Cheyne-Stokes), especially during sleep. It signals advanced low-output failure.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 22: Cardiac Failure

  14. Which laboratory marker rises in heart failure due to ventricular wall stretch and promotes natriuresis?

    • A. Troponin I
    • B. Creatine kinase-MB
    • C. B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP)
    • D. Myoglobin
    • E. D-dimer
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: C. B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP)

    Ventricular myocytes release BNP (and ANP from atria) in response to wall stretch/volume overload. BNP causes natriuresis, diuresis, and vasodilation — a compensatory brake on RAAS. Elevated BNP/NT-proBNP is the key diagnostic and prognostic biomarker in heart failure.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 22: Cardiac Failure

  15. In cardiogenic shock complicating massive myocardial infarction, the primary hemodynamic defect is:

    • A. Decreased systemic vascular resistance
    • B. Pump failure with low cardiac output and elevated filling pressures
    • C. Hypovolemia
    • D. Anaphylactic vasodilation
    • E. Obstructed venous return
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Pump failure with low cardiac output and elevated filling pressures

    Cardiogenic shock = pump failure: extensive myocyte loss drops contractility → low cardiac output and hypotension DESPITE elevated filling pressures (high PCWP, JVD, pulmonary edema). This contrasts with distributive shock (low SVR) and hypovolemic shock (low filling pressures).

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 22: Cardiac Failure

  16. Why do ACE inhibitors improve survival in heart failure with reduced ejection fraction?

    • A. They directly increase contractility
    • B. They block angiotensin II–mediated vasoconstriction, remodeling, and aldosterone release
    • C. They slow AV nodal conduction
    • D. They increase heart rate
    • E. They block sympathetic beta receptors
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. They block angiotensin II–mediated vasoconstriction, remodeling, and aldosterone release

    ACE inhibitors reduce angiotensin II (vasoconstrictor, growth factor driving hypertrophy/fibrosis) and aldosterone (Na⁺/water retention, fibrosis), lowering preload and afterload while slowing remodeling. Unlike pure vasodilators, they interrupt the neurohormonal vicious cycle — hence mortality benefit.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 22: Cardiac Failure

  17. A patient with chronic heart failure is on furosemide and develops muscle weakness with flattened T waves and prominent U waves on ECG. The most likely cause is:

    • A. Hyperkalemia
    • B. Hypokalemia from loop diuretic–induced K⁺ loss
    • C. Hypercalcemia
    • D. Hyponatremia
    • E. Hypomagnesemia alone
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Hypokalemia from loop diuretic–induced K⁺ loss

    Loop diuretics block the NKCC2 transporter in the thick ascending limb, wasting K⁺ (and Mg²⁺/Ca²⁺). Hypokalemia causes muscle weakness and ECG changes: flattened T waves, prominent U waves, ST depression — and predisposes to digoxin toxicity and arrhythmias. K⁺/Mg²⁺ monitoring is essential.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 22: Cardiac Failure

  18. Eccentric hypertrophy with chamber dilation in heart failure most often results from:

    • A. Chronic pressure overload
    • B. Chronic volume overload
    • C. Acute pericarditis
    • D. Constrictive pericarditis
    • E. Cardiac tamponade
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Chronic volume overload

    Volume overload (mitral/aortic regurgitation, anemia, AV fistula) causes eccentric hypertrophy: sarcomeres added in series, chamber dilates with proportional wall thickening. Pressure overload instead causes concentric hypertrophy (sarcomeres in parallel, thick walls, small cavity).

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 22: Cardiac Failure

  19. In acute left heart failure, giving a venodilator such as nitroglycerin helps primarily by:

    • A. Increasing contractility
    • B. Reducing preload via venous pooling, lowering pulmonary congestion
    • C. Increasing heart rate
    • D. Constricting arterioles
    • E. Increasing plasma volume
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. Reducing preload via venous pooling, lowering pulmonary congestion

    Venodilators pool blood in the systemic veins, reducing venous return (preload) and thus left atrial/pulmonary capillary pressures — relieving pulmonary edema. The failing heart on the flat Frank-Starling curve loses little stroke volume from modest preload reduction while congestion improves markedly.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 22: Cardiac Failure

  20. Which statement about diastolic heart failure is correct?

    • A. Ejection fraction is always below 40%
    • B. It results from impaired ventricular relaxation/filling with preserved systolic function
    • C. It never causes pulmonary edema
    • D. It is caused by decreased afterload
    • E. Beta-blockers are contraindicated
    Show answer and explanation

    Answer: B. It results from impaired ventricular relaxation/filling with preserved systolic function

    Diastolic (HFpEF) failure = stiff, hypertrophied ventricle that cannot relax/fill normally, so end-diastolic pressure rises while ejection fraction stays ≥50%. It still causes pulmonary congestion (elevated filling pressures back up). Management targets the cause (hypertension), rate control, and diuretics — not inotropes.

    Source: Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology, 14th ed. — Ch. 22: Cardiac Failure
